@@2699@@
Please look at this sentence once more: “I will bring a sword on you.” This is not an accident. He said it with his own mouth. But please notice that he spoke first, and acted afterward. He spoke to the mountains first, gave people a chance to hear first, and put his word before everyone first. We often think God’s judgment comes suddenly, but the Scriptures show again and again that he speaks first, then waits, then speaks again. His patience is longer than we imagine.
@@2700@@
Why tear them down? Because people put the most precious things on that altar. Time, strength, and affection were all offered to something that cannot answer. Think about it: the cruelest thing about an idol is that it never says “enough.” However much you give, it takes; it is never satisfied. Perhaps you have no altar in your home, but is there one thing you keep feeding that has never once said “enough”?
@@2701@@
II. The Altars on the Mountains Are Evidence (6:6–7)
@@2702@@
Verse 6 says: in all your dwelling places the cities will be laid waste, the high places will be desolate, your altars will be laid waste and made desolate, your idols broken and ceasing, and your works abolished. Please notice the last phrase: your works abolished. It is not only the idols that are torn down; everything made by human hands will be torn down. What people lean on most easily is often their own work: career, reputation, service. These things are not bad in themselves, but once a person treats them as the final thing to rely on, they become an altar.
@@2703@@
There was a brother who had risen to be a supervisor in a small company, and he was always the last to leave the office. He told me that every Sunday, sitting in church, his mind was on what he had to do the next week. He said: Pastor, it’s not that I don’t love the Lord. I just felt that the things in my hands could not go on without me. Later the company downsized, and he was let go. He said: That month I discovered that I had been worshiping it all along. I said: But you are still here. He said: Yes, I’m still here, but I’m empty. I said: An empty place is the easiest place for God to come in.
@@2704@@
Verse 7 closes this section, and it is a line that comes back again and again through the whole chapter: “The slain will fall among you, and you will know that I am Yahweh.” Notice the structure of this sentence. Before it is a very heavy picture, and after it is a very large purpose: you will know that I am Yahweh. This sentence appears more than sixty times in Ezekiel. God said so many heavy things, and the purpose was not to make people afraid, but to let them know him.
@@2705@@
When we read “you will know that I am Yahweh,” we need to remember who this sentence was spoken to. It was spoken to people who had built altars, who had heard again and again and still would not turn back. It was not spoken to those who had just lost a loved one, nor to those weeping in the land of exile. The same sentence, spoken to the wrong person, becomes a wounding word. So whoever teaches this chapter must first know whose side they are standing beside.
@@2706@@
But why must people come to know him in such a way? We often ask this question. The text does not give an easy answer. We can only say: unless a person is torn down to nothing, they often will not stop. It is not that God enjoys tearing down; it is that people hold on too tightly. If you happen to be on the road of being torn down, please know that it is not that he has left you, but that he wants your hands empty, so that they can receive something greater than an altar.
@@2707@@
“You will know that I am Yahweh”: does God make people suffer so that they will know him?
@@2708@@
No. This sentence must be read within the whole book. God says that he has no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live (33:11). The “knowing” of 6:7 is the purpose of the judgment, not the pleasure of the judgment; what he wants is a real meeting with people. Please be careful not to preach this as “you are suffering because God wants you to know him.” Saying this to someone who is suffering may be a second wound. To those who suffer, accompany them first, and do not explain. This passage was spoken to idols, not to victims. Let me say it again: accompany first, do not explain, and only when the other person is willing to ask, say it gently.
@@2709@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Remember, Broken, Know (6:9, 10)
@@2710@@
This chapter has three original words. Each is worth looking at slowly, and each can be turned into a way of living today in thirty seconds. The first is “remember,” in Hebrew זָכַר (zakhar). Verse 9 says: “Those of you that escape will remember me among the nations where they are carried captive.” This word means more than “to think of.” It means to think of, and to act. God remembered Noah, and he made a wind pass over the earth; when people remember God, they turn around. So “remember” is not a thought flashing through the mind; it is the beginning of a person turning around. Is there one thing you did today because you remembered him?
@@2711@@
The second is “broken,” in Hebrew שָׁבַר (shavar). Verse 9 says: “how I have been broken with their lewd heart.” This word means to snap or to shatter. For example, a branch snapped off, or a cup smashed. The grammar of this verse can be read in two ways: one is that God’s heart is broken because his people have gone far from him; the other is that the people’s heart is broken, and only in the land of exile do they see that they have left. These notes will not choose a side for you, because both readings point to the same thing: sin wounds a relationship, and inside the relationship there is a broken heart. You do not need to decide whose it is first. First ask yourself: have I made someone’s heart break because of me?
@@2712@@
The third is “know,” in Hebrew יָדַע (yadaʻ). Verse 7 says: “you will know that I am Yahweh.” In the Bible this word often points to a personal knowing, like the knowing between husband and wife. It is not “knowing a doctrine,” but “knowing a person.” This is the hope of the whole chapter: at the end of the judgment, people have not learned one more doctrine; they have truly met Yahweh. We read Ezekiel not to understand one more prophecy, but to be known by him, and to know him.
@@2713@@
Put the three words together, and you have the journey of this chapter: a broken heart (shavar), remembering him in the land of exile (zakhar), and then, at last, knowing him (yadaʻ). Broken, remembering, knowing: this is a road home.
@@2714@@
Is “remembering” only thinking of God? I often think of God, but my life does not change. Is something wrong?
@@2715@@
You can check it this way. In the Bible, “remember” is thinking of him with action. You can ask three questions. First, when I think of him, is there a very small action, such as stopping to say a sentence? Second, is there one thing I have done recently because I remembered him? Third, what I remember, is it his name, or what he has done for me? If you find that you only think of him and do not act, there is no need to blame yourself. You can start with the smallest action: every night before bed, say: “Lord, today I remember you.” Turn thinking into speaking, and speaking is the beginning of action.
@@2716@@
IV. Leaving Some Behind (6:8–10)
@@2717@@
Verse 8 is the turning point of the chapter: “Yet I will leave a remnant, in that you will have some that escape the sword among the nations, when you are scattered through the countries.” This sentence appears very suddenly. Before it there are desolate altars, falling bodies, destruction, and suddenly God says: I will leave some of you. Please think about it: this is a very big turn. Right in the middle of the heaviest words, God says: I will leave some. He did not wipe the people out; those he leaves are in the scattering.
@@2718@@
Verse 9 says that those who escape the sword will remember him among the nations where they are carried captive. This picture is important: not in the temple, not in Jerusalem, but among the nations where they were carried captive. God’s people had left home and left the temple, but he said that there, they will remember me. I think of many who have left their hometowns: working in a foreign land, studying in a foreign land, living in a strange city. They have no church, no familiar hymns, but God says that there, they will remember me. God is not limited by place, and even the land of exile can become a place where people come to know him.
@@2719@@
Verse 9 goes on to say: “Then they will loathe themselves in their own sight for the evils which they have committed in all their abominations.” Please notice that this “loathing themselves” is said by God. He announces beforehand that these people will one day see what they have done and be grieved in their hearts. This is what repentance looks like. But we must be careful not to preach “loathing themselves” as telling people to hate themselves. What the Bible describes is seeing the truth about sin, not denying one’s worth. Seeing that we have left him and wounded him is the beginning of turning back, not stepping on ourselves on the ground.
@@2720@@
Verse 10 says: “They will know that I am Yahweh. I have not said in vain that I would do this evil to them.” “Not in vain” means, in the original, “not said for nothing.” What he says, he does; and what he does, he does for a purpose. This sentence has two sides: to those who will not listen, it is a warning; to those in exile, it is a comfort: he has not spoken for nothing, and he will not let you walk this road for nothing. What you are going through today, he knows.
@@2721@@
“Some will escape the sword”: does this mean some people are saved and some are not?
@@2722@@
Please do not read it this way. This verse is not sorting people into categories; it is speaking of God’s sovereignty and mercy: even in judgment he still keeps. Scholars read “those who are left” in several ways: one takes it as the Israelites preserved in the land of exile in history; another emphasizes the personal “remembering” and “loathing themselves,” which is personal turning; another emphasizes God’s sovereign keeping. None of these three readings says “this person is left, that person is not.” These notes stand here: God has no pleasure in the death of anyone, but wants them to turn and live. Do not put a label on anyone living today; this is an invitation, not a classification.
@@2723@@
The first deviation is to preach the “sword” of 6:3 and the “sword, famine, and pestilence” of verse 11 as prophecies of modern disasters and to name a certain country or group. The text is addressed to the mountains of Israel at that time, and we do not look for modern matches. We want the congregation to fear God more and be more willing to turn back, not to be more eager to predict.
@@2724@@
The second deviation is to describe the fallen people in as much detail as possible in order to frighten the congregation. The pictures in verses 4, 5, and 13 are the consequences of sin, not God’s pleasure; we touch on them lightly and do not enlarge the picture. Whether the listeners become more afraid or love the Lord more is the question we should ask after we finish.
@@2725@@
The third deviation is to quote this chapter to someone who has lost a loved one or is seriously ill, saying “this is the consequence of your idols.” The text speaks to idols, not to the one who suffers. If these words cannot be said to someone who has just lost a loved one, do not say them; accompany first, do not explain.
@@2726@@
The fourth deviation is to preach “you will know that I am Yahweh” as God tormenting people so that they will know him. Verse 9 in chapter 6 says at the same time “how I have been broken,” and 33:11 says he has no pleasure in the death of anyone. We must speak both of his seriousness and of his broken heart.
@@2727@@
The fifth deviation is to turn the clapping of hands and stamping of feet into cheering for judgment, and to speak of idols only as other people’s problem. Verse 11 is joined with “Alas!”, which is grief, not gloating. After the sermon, the listeners should first ask themselves: what is my altar? Not first point at others.
@@2728@@
I know some relatives and friends still worship idols. Should I teach this chapter to them?
@@2729@@
Be very careful. This chapter was spoken to idols; it was not meant to be used against people. If you take this chapter to a family member who worships idols and say: your altars will be torn down, it will probably only make them close up more. A better way is: accompany first, ask about their life first, pray for them first, and let them see that your life is a little different from others. If they are willing to ask, then say why you live this way. In Chinese families especially, respect the elders’ face and feelings. First ask yourself: what is my altar? Then speak of others’. This week, first pray for that relative or friend; there is no need to force yourself to speak.
@@2730@@
The first question: we are very used to the altars of “ancestors, family, and tradition.” In Chinese families there are often rituals, rules, and festivals that slowly get mixed with worship, until it is hard to tell whether it is filial piety or idol worship. Verse 3 says that God speaks to the great mountains, the small hills, the watercourses, and the valleys, which means that even the smallest places are under his eyes. You can start like this: do not argue first; ask yourself one by one: am I doing this thing out of love for my family, or out of fear? Is this thing glorifying him, or glorifying something else? When you have asked, talk with a pastor or coworker you trust. Do not decide alone, and do not use this verse to judge the elders.
@@2731@@
The second question: we are very used to “holding it in, not saying anything, pretending nothing is wrong.” People in the land of exile often can only hold it in. But 6:9 says that they will “remember me” and “loathe themselves,” which means bringing their real feelings before God. Chinese people are not used to weeping over their own weakness, and even less used to mourning over other people’s failures. You can start like this: this week, find a quiet place and say one true sentence to God: “Lord, there is something I have been holding in lately.” Just one sentence, with no explanation. Mourning is not weakness; it is the way of the prophet.
@@2732@@
I seldom cry, and I am not used to mourning for others. Does that mean I am cold?
@@2733@@
Not necessarily. Some people are not cold; they were taught from childhood to endure. Verse 11 of chapter 6 says that the prophet is told to clap his hands and stamp his foot and say “Alas!”, which means using the whole body to express God’s feeling. You can start with a very small action: when you see someone fall, first say in your heart: “Alas, Lord, have mercy on him.” Do not comment, do not compare, do not pass it on. After a while, the heart will slowly soften. If you feel nothing for a long time, there is no need to force yourself; you can bring it to a pastor, because there may be an old wound inside that needs someone to walk with you slowly. Mourning has no standard shape: some people weep, some are silent, some simply sit quietly beside someone, and God sees all of it.
@@2734@@
VII. Living Out 321: Remember Only Him
@@2735@@
Jesus is my Role Model. The prophet clapped his hands and stamped his foot and said “Alas!” and wept standing on the ground of disaster. When Jesus saw Jerusalem, he also wept. He is the Judge, and he is also the one who weeps. Those who follow him do not learn “I told you so,” but to mourn for people; they do not learn to stand on high ground, but to kneel on the ground and sigh for the people of this place.
@@2736@@
The Bible is my Standard. Verse 3 of chapter 6 says to hear the word of the Lord Yahweh. We read this chapter not to analyze how many altars there are on the mountains, but to let God’s word ask us: what is my altar? The Bible is a standard; it is not used to point at other people’s altars, but to show ourselves. One verse and one small action is the best response to this chapter.
@@2737@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who makes the people in the land of exile remember God, and who makes people see that they have left him and soften in their hearts. The “remembering” and “loathing themselves” of verse 9 are not forced out by ourselves; they are the work of the Spirit. Every day be quiet for one minute and ask: Lord, what altar have I built with my own hands today? The Holy Spirit will point it out, not to condemn us, but to bring us back.
@@2738@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is putting down the self that says “it can’t go on without me,” and putting the altar I built with my own hands back from the altar into his hands. Career, reputation, and service were all given by him in the first place. To let Jesus be King is to give them back to him, and then to remember only him. Selflessness is not having nothing; it is still knowing him after everything has been torn down.
@@2739@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we have been left and kept, let us not say “I am stronger than others”; those who escaped the sword did so because God made a remnant remain. All the glory belongs to the Lord who still leaves people in the middle of the scattering. If we can remember him in the land of exile, that too is his grace.
@@2740@@
Build God’s system. God’s system does not rely on a temple, walls, or a career, but on a group of people who remember him. Let man be man, and let God be God: first life, seeing that we once left him; then relationships, mourning for others without judging them; then ministry, becoming his witnesses in the land of exile. A small group like this is the people God leaves behind in the scattering.
@@2741@@
How are “Let Jesus be King” and “putting down the altar I built” connected in everyday life?
@@2742@@
Very directly. The altar is what I rely on most; the King is the one I obey most. If Jesus is King, the altar I built must be put back into his hands. You can practice it this way: every morning write down the three things you rely on most, such as work, money, or other people’s approval, and then say to Jesus: these three are all in your hands, today you are King. In the evening look back: which one did I grab back today? There is no need to blame yourself; just put it down once more. After a while you will find that your hand slowly loosens and your heart slowly settles. The one you cannot put down is the one most in need of being handed over first.
@@2743@@
The proud type says: “I built these altars; I worked hard for them. Without me, all of this would have disappeared long ago.” The text says to him: your works will be abolished. The proud leave their position and grasp at power, trying to become their own god; returning to our position is admitting: everything my hands have made was given by him. You can start by saying to God: “Lord, all of this is yours, not mine.”
@@2744@@
The fearful type says: “This chapter has swords and famine. I dare not read it, afraid that I too will be torn down.” He says: I will leave some of you; he says: not in vain. What the fearful need to learn is not to fear more, but to draw near: read verse 8 first, then 33:11. Change “afraid of being torn down” into “kept by a hand that does not let go.” His seriousness and his keeping belong to the same God.
@@2745@@
The ashamed type says: “I have left him and done abominable things. I have no right to go back.” Verse 9 says that those who leave will loathe themselves, but what he says is “remember me,” not “hate yourself.” Shame says: you are no longer worthy; the gospel says: you can still remember him. The ashamed do not need to wash themselves clean first. Today, just say: “Lord, I remember you.”
@@2746@@
The wounded type says: “I lost someone very important, and this chapter seems to say that I did something wrong.” No. This chapter is not speaking to you. If you are weeping, please do not read the pictures of the fallen for now. The wounded can slowly find someone to be with them, saying only a name, without details; the one who accompanies should not say “you have to have faith,” nor hurry to urge “you should forgive,” but only say: “I’m here, I’m with you.”
@@2747@@
First, spend three minutes each day on “remembering”: think first of who he is, then of what he has done for you, write down one sentence, “I remember you as …”, read it aloud, and write down the date.
@@2748@@
Second, write down three things made by human hands that you thought were reliable (career, reputation, service), say to God: “These are all in your hands,” and say whom you are willing to let know.
@@2749@@
Third, when you see someone fall, first say “Alas” in your heart, pray for them, and do not comment or pass it on; say that person’s name and the date.
@@2750@@
Fourth, find a quiet place and say one true sentence to God: “Lord, there is something I have been holding in lately.” Write down which day it was, and say who will ask you about it.
@@2751@@
Fifth, ask someone who has left home or just moved: “How are you doing here?” Give no advice, only listen, and say their name and the time.

Which one do I need most this week? How do I know where to start?
@@2757@@
You can use the simplest way: see which one makes you feel a little resistance, and that is often the one you need. If it is the second, and writing down the things made by human hands feels hard, you may rely on them most; if it is the fourth, and saying something true feels hard, you may have held in the longest. You do not need to do all five. Choose one, do it this week, and find one person to tell which one you chose. This too is remembering: to think of him, and to take one small action.

Chapter 6 begins with a turning of the face and ends with the sentence “they will know that I am Yahweh.” At the beginning the prophet turns to face the mountains and says: hear the word of the Lord Yahweh; in the middle the altars are desolate and the idols broken, and God also says: I will leave some of you; at the end there is the clapping of hands and stamping of feet and the cry “Alas!”, the prophet on his knees on the ground of disaster, weeping for this people. That God came among us in Jesus; he wept over Jerusalem, and he weeps for us too.

The focus of this lesson is not how many altars are on the mountains, not who will be left, and not how big your altar is, but the Lord who still leaves people in the scattering and wants people to know him.

① Preaching this chapter as “every kind of disaster is God’s judgment”
7:4, 9 were spoken to one particular city, a generation that kept refusing to turn back. To pass them on to someone who has lost a loved one, is seriously ill, or has lost a job is to add another cut to the wound. The whole book of Job is there to correct this inference.
Test: do the listeners leave more able to bring their pain before God, or more afraid of being struck by God?
@@2815@@
② Using “the end has come” to make timetables and count days
The “now” and “already” of 7:2--3 are a warning, not a calendar; the text gives no date, and does not tell anyone to prepare for a particular year.
Test: when the congregation leaves, are they counting days, or turning around and letting go?
@@2816@@
③ Preaching “my eye will not spare” as God’s temper
This is a God who spared for a very long time and finally says enough; and the closing of 7:4, 9, and 27 is “you will know that I am Yahweh.” The purpose of the judgment is to know him, not to destroy.
Test: in the hearer’s heart, does an irritable God come to mind, or a God who is just and long-suffering?
@@2817@@
④ Preaching only judgment, and not that Christ has already received the “no sparing”
This lets people hear only intimidation, and not the gospel. Romans says that God did not spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all.
Test: at the end of the sermon, has the congregation heard the invitation “Come”?
@@2818@@
⑤ Preaching “silver and gold are a stumbling block” as “being rich is sin” or “only the poor are spiritual”
7:19 speaks of putting silver and gold in God’s place; money itself is not sin, and poverty is not a virtue.
Test: will this way of preaching make the poor feel ashamed, or make the rich or the generous feel accused?
@@2819@@
⑥ Using this chapter to identify a certain person, group, or nation as “being judged”

Test: does this sermon contain the pattern “they are …, therefore God …”?
@@2820@@
⑦ Preaching “everyone” (7:16) as “other people, nothing to do with me”

Test: has the speaker first said “I need this too”?
@@2821@@
⑧ Demanding that victims “repent” while skipping over the violence of 7:23 and the oppressed

Test: after listening, do those who have been hurt still feel that this place is safe?
@@2822@@
⑨ Using panic to lead people to turn back
The fruit of Ezekiel is patience and more love for the Lord, not hoarding, anxiety, and sleepless nights.
Test: when the congregation walks out, do they love the Lord more, or fear the world more?
@@2823@@
⑩ Preaching the cutting off of the three channels in 7:26 as “today’s pastors and elders cannot be trusted”
The text says that when a people will not listen, even the channels have nothing to say; it is not teaching people to doubt all leaders.
Test: is the congregation more willing to seek in God’s word and among spiritual companions, or more distrustful of one another?

<p><strong>Gold and silver were their sense of security; now they are rubbish on the ground.</strong></p><p>What is “cast in the streets” (יַשְׁלִיכוּ) is the silver, and what is “unclean” (נִדָּה) is the gold. The word “unclean” originally meant the uncleanness that comes with menstruation, something people want to avoid. What they most wanted to grasp became what they most wanted to throw away.</p><p>Three negatives follow: they cannot deliver, cannot satisfy the soul, and cannot fill the belly. This is a step-by-step descent: from life, to heart, to belly. Gold and silver cannot save a life, cannot feed a heart, and cannot even fill a stomach.</p><p>The heaviest line is at the end: gold and silver became “the stumbling block of their iniquity” (מִכְשׁוֹל). It is not that gold and silver are sinful, but that they put them in God’s place. What can trip people is often not a bad thing, but a good thing lifted too high.</p><p>Verse 20 goes on to speak of “beauty”: the beauty God gave (read as the temple in the CUV), which they used to make idols. God gave beauty, and they used it to make other gods. The pain here is that the gift was turned to another use.</p><p>Landing: money, talent, and reputation are not bad. But when they become the guarantee that “nothing will happen to me,” they become a stumbling block. Ask: if all these were taken away tomorrow, what would I have left?</p>

<p><strong>The most frightening sentence is: “I will turn my face from them.”</strong></p><p>Strangers come in, the wicked come in, robbers come in, wave after wave, until even the most hidden place is entered. In history the temple was indeed broken into by foreigners. But the weight of this passage is not on the outsiders; it is on God: “I will turn my face from them.”</p><p>“Turning the face” (הֲסִבּוֹתִי פָנַי) is one of the heaviest actions in the whole Bible. Aaron’s blessing says: “Yahweh make his face to shine on you” (Num 6:25). That is the face turned toward you. Here, the face has turned away. No one is blocking the sanctuary from outside, because the one inside is no longer there.</p><p>This also prepares for the visions to come. From chapter 8 on, the prophet will be taken to see what happens in the temple; later, the glory will leave. Here it is already said first: the temple is not unsafe because the walls were broken through, but because God’s face turned away.</p><p>Landing: a church’s walls can be very strong and its activities very lively, but if his face has turned away, we may not notice. Ask: how long has it been since we truly sought his face? To let Jesus be King is not to put him on a list, but to keep his face in the middle.</p>

<p><strong>The prophet is to make chains, because prisoners are coming.</strong></p><p>Verse 23 begins with a command: “Make chains.” The word “chain” (רַתּוֹק) appears very rarely, and even its meaning carries a little uncertainty. But the context is clear: the land is full of bloodshed, the city is full of violence, so something must bind it.</p><p>“Violence” (חָמָס) appears for the second time in this chapter (verse 11), like a word knocking on the door again. It is not only brutality; it is power treating people as something to be eaten. When the city is full of such things, God says: I will bring “the worst of the nations.”</p><p>See the irony: the houses they built by violence are finally possessed by those more violent than they. “The pride of the strong ceases” is not God showing off his might, but saying: whatever strength people rely on, there is always something stronger than it.</p><p>Landing: if we have any position by which we can press down on others, seniority, voice, authority, remember that this is the seed of “violence” that God sees. Those who can press down on others must first learn to be pressed low by God.</p>

<p><strong>Three channels: prophet, priest, elder</strong></p><p>Verse 26 places “vision,” “law,” and “counsel” after prophet, priest, and elder respectively, each marked with מִן (from) to show the source. Jer 18:18 also has three similar items. Together these three are a people’s complete channel for seeking direction.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding:</strong> the three channels are cut off at the same time: not that God no longer speaks, but that the channels they had refused to listen to now have nothing to say.</p></blockquote>
@@2850@@
<p><strong>When all three voices fall silent, the people lose their direction.</strong></p><p>Verse 25 says: “They will seek peace, and there will be none.” “Peace” (שָׁלוֹם) is the word the whole people longed for most, the blessing of the covenant, the words of greeting. But they seek it only after the disaster arrives, and they seek only peace, not God.</p><p>Verse 26 is like a string of echoes: disaster upon disaster, rumor upon rumor. Bad news no longer comes alone, but in a line.</p><p>Then three kinds of people appear: prophet, priest, elder. The prophet gives vision, the priest teaches the law, the elder offers counsel; these are the three channels by which a people seeks direction. All three are cut off. It is not that God no longer speaks, but that the channels they had refused to listen to now have nothing to say.</p><p>The order of verse 27 is: the king mourns, the prince is clothed with desolation, and finally the hands of the people tremble. From top to bottom, no level is an exception. The ending echoes the beginning: “they will know that I am Yahweh.” This sentence appears once each in verses 4, 9, and 27, like three punctuation marks.</p><p>Landing: before God falls silent, it is best to learn to listen to him speak. Reading the Bible, praying, and fellowship with spiritual companions are not to become “better Christians,” but so that before disaster comes, our ears are already used to his voice.</p>
