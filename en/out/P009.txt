I. The End Has Come (7:1–4)
@@3124@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever moved house? On the night before the move, the boxes are all taped shut, the lights are still on, and you sit on the floor of the empty living room. That night you notice one thing: most of what you stuffed into the cupboards for over ten years is not needed. The only things you want to carry in your own arms, and would not hand over to the movers, are a few: a photo album, an identity document, a photograph of one person. What we thought was important in ordinary days loses its color, one thing after another, on that last night.
@@3125@@
Today we read Ezekiel chapter 7. It has only twenty-seven verses, but it is like that night before the move. The first thing God says to the whole land is “The end has come.” Let me be honest first: this chapter is not easy to read. It has no comfort, no turning point, no “but.” I do not intend to make it lighter than the text, nor heavier than the text. Let us just let the text finish what it has to say. When it has finished, you will find that it is not trying to frighten people. It is trying to make them turn around before the last moment.
@@3126@@
Look first at verse 1: “Moreover Yahweh’s word came to me, saying,” This sentence is short. The prophet does not say, “I have a feeling,” and he does not say, “I have analyzed the situation.” He says, the word came to me. This is not his opinion; it is a weight he has to carry. Please notice the word “moreover.” In the six chapters before this, the word had already come many times. God is not speaking for the first time; he speaks again and again. To a people who would not listen, he patiently opens his mouth once more.
@@3127@@
Verse 2 says: “You, son of man, the Lord Yahweh says to the land of Israel, ‘An end! The end has come on the four corners of the land.’ ” In the last chapter, the prophet spoke to the mountains; in this chapter, he speaks to the whole land. “The four corners” is, in the Hebrew, four wings or edges. All four corners have been reached, and there is no direction left to run. Then look at the small word in verse 3: “Now is the end on you.” Now. Not in the future, not in the next generation. While people were still talking about “later,” God’s word had already come to “now.”
@@3128@@
Then in verses 3 and 4 come two of the heaviest sentences, one after the other. The first: “and will judge you according to your ways. I will bring on you all your abominations.” According to your ways. Not according to his passing mood, but according to the road you have walked, counted out to you stroke by stroke. Justice means: not more, and not less. The second: “My eye will not spare you, neither will I have pity.” Please notice who is speaking. It is the God who, from Egypt, from the wilderness, from the age of the judges, spared them again and again. This “will not spare” does not mean that he suddenly changed his face. It is the God who spared for so long, finally saying: Enough.
@@3129@@
Which day does “the end has come” mean? Can we use this chapter to work out the date?
@@3130@@
No. The “now” and “already” of verses 2 and 3 are the tone of a warning, not a mark on a calendar. The whole chapter gives no date and does not tell people to prepare for any year. Interpreters read it differently: most hold that it was first spoken to the land of Judah and to Jerusalem at that time, the end that city was about to face; others hear a more distant echo here, pointing to the last days. These notes set out both readings and do not make either one a condition of faith. But whichever reading we take, one thing is shared: the purpose of the text is to make people turn around, not to make them watch the calendar. Jesus himself also said that no one knows that day or that hour. So if anyone uses this chapter to set a timetable or calculate years, please gently give the chapter back to the text. What you need to do today is not guess which day it is, but ask yourself: what is the one thing I have put off for too long?
@@3131@@
II. Light from the Original Language: קֵץ, הֵקִיץ, וִידַעְתֶּם (7:5–9)
@@3132@@
Verses 5 and 6 say: “An evil! A unique evil! Behold, it comes. An end has come. The end has come! It awakes against you. Behold, it comes.” In these few lines there are three Hebrew words worth stopping to look at.
@@3133@@
The first is “end,” in Hebrew קֵץ. It means the finish, a cutting off. You can picture a rope that has been cut down to its last length. What you can turn into life within thirty seconds is this: some things have their end. Delay is not neutral; the longer you drag, the shorter the road that remains. This is not God trying to scare us. It is someone who truly loves us, standing at the door and saying: if you do not deal with this now, it will be too late.
@@3134@@
The second is “awakes,” in Hebrew הֵקִיץ. Please look at it together with קֵץ; it sounds like the same word turned over in bed. Many scholars point out that this is a deliberate wordplay by the prophet: the word carries the sense of “waking up.” The end, like something that has slept a long time, has woken, and it has woken against you. The Chinese translation “rises up” does not show this playfulness, but the meaning can be caught: the things we think we are putting off are asleep, but in fact they have been growing all the time. That phone call not returned, that relationship growing colder and colder, that habit you know best yourself, none of them is standing still.
@@3135@@
The third is “you will know,” in Hebrew וִידַעְתֶּם, from the root יָדַע. This word does not mean knowing a doctrine; it is the knowing that comes after personal experience. “Then you will know that I am Yahweh” appears in this chapter at verses 4, 9, and 27, like three nails fastening the whole chapter together. Verse 9 adds two words: “Then you will know that I, Yahweh, strike.” When they are struck, they finally understand: the one striking me is not fate, and not the times, but the One whose name I have been calling all along. The purpose of judgment is that people may know him.
@@3136@@
The sentence in verse 7, “a day of tumult, and not of joyful shouting, on the mountains,” is even more startling. The joyful shouting on the mountains was familiar to them: the feasts, the worship, the crowds. God says this day is tumult, it is chaos. Noise without God turns out, in the end, to be only noise. Brothers and sisters, God rarely says a thing only once. It is not that he fears you did not hear; it is that he cannot bear for you not to listen.
@@3137@@
Having read this far, I must say a word of protection first. If you are suffering today, whether through illness, the loss of someone you love, the loss of work, or the loss of a relationship, please do not take this chapter and say to yourself: Am I being struck by God? This chapter was spoken to a people who worshiped idols, shed blood, and refused again and again to turn back. It was not spoken to someone who is weeping. He sees your tears. This chapter is not here to put you on trial.
@@3138@@
“Then you will know that I am Yahweh” appears three times. Does that mean people come to know God only through judgment?
@@3139@@
No. The tone of this chapter is judgment, but the hope of the whole book of Ezekiel is knowing. God does not make people know him only when he strikes; he also makes them know him when he heals, rescues, and gives a new heart, and this phrase will appear again and again in the later chapters. The point here is: when people keep refusing to know him in other ways, God does not give up even the heaviest way, because what he wants is for people to know who he is. For us today, there is a much quieter road: we do not need to wait until we are struck to know him; we can know him now. You can practice it like this: every day, read once the words “Then you will know that I am Yahweh,” asking for nothing, and ask only: in my ordinary days this week, how have I come to know him? If your answer is “very little,” there is no need to blame yourself; just begin today.

@@3142@@
Verse 11 says: “Violence has risen up into a rod of wickedness.” “Violence” in Hebrew is חָמָס. This word is not only about hitting people; it is the strong treating the weak as something to be eaten. The word appears twice in the chapter, in verses 11 and 23, like someone knocking twice at the door. Interpreters read this verse differently: some say violence itself becomes the rod that strikes them; others say that violent people are used by God as the instrument of judgment. Whichever it is, the meaning is the same: what they sowed comes back to strike them.
@@3143@@
In verses 12 and 13, we come back to the market we began with: “Don’t let the buyer rejoice, nor the seller mourn.” Why? Because this deal is no longer the great matter of life. If you gained, do not boast; if you lost, do not despair. Something greater has come. Verse 13 says the seller “won’t return to that which is sold,” and many interpreters here hear an echo of the Jubilee in Leviticus: land that was sold was to return at the Jubilee, grace God prepared for those who had lost their land. The text does not mention the Jubilee, but the meaning is clear: even that road back is shut.
@@3144@@
Verse 14 says: “They have blown the trumpet, and have made all ready; but no one goes to the battle.” The trumpet is blown, everything is ready, and not one person goes out. Not because they are afraid, but because God’s burning wrath rests on every one of them. Verse 15 speaks of sword, pestilence, and famine. Some escape, but those who escape are “on the mountains like doves of the valleys, all of them moaning.” In the Bible the dove is a bird of peace, but here the doves moan in the valleys. The same kind of bird, a different song. Being alive does not mean having come back. Many people get through a hard time and say, “Thank goodness, we made it.” But no one asks: how are things between God and me now?
@@3145@@
In verses 17 and 18, the body speaks first: “All hands will be feeble, and all knees will be weak as water.” It turns out the strength we were leaning on was borrowed.
@@3146@@
Then comes the turning point of the whole chapter, verse 19: “They will cast their silver in the streets, and their gold will be as an unclean thing.” The silver that was locked in the cupboard yesterday is thrown in the streets today. “Unclean” is נִדָּה, the uncleanness people most want to avoid. What they most wanted to hold has become what they most want to fling away. “Their silver and their gold won’t be able to deliver them in the day of Yahweh’s wrath. They won’t satisfy their souls or fill their bellies.” Three “cannots”: it cannot save a life, it cannot satisfy the heart, it cannot fill the belly. Look at the order: from life, to the heart, to the stomach. Gold and silver cannot fill even the lowest level. The last sentence is the heaviest: “because it has been the stumbling block of their iniquity.” It is not that gold and silver are sinful; it is that they put it in the place of God. What trips a person up is often not a bad thing, but a good thing lifted too high. Back to that night before the move: if all of this were gone tomorrow, what would you have left?
@@3147@@
Gold and silver are called “the stumbling block of their iniquity.” Does that mean having money is sin and only poverty is spiritual?
@@3148@@
No. Verse 19 says that gold and silver “have been” the stumbling block of their iniquity, meaning that they put gold and silver in the place of God and staked on it the guarantee that “I will be fine.” Money itself is good, talent is good, and reputation and position are good; the Bible never says poverty is a virtue. A person with money can use it to provide, to help, to share; a person without money can just as easily put “a little more and I will be safe” first in his heart. There is only one test: is it serving me, or is it replacing God? You can check in a very concrete way: in this month’s accounts, which expense is there only to make you feel secure? Pick it out and change it into giving to God, giving to people, or giving to quiet. The generous and the hard-working should not be condemned by this chapter; they should be reminded by it.
@@3149@@
IV. The Temple, the Face, and the Chains (7:20–27)
@@3150@@
From verse 20 the pain cuts deeper: “As for the beauty of his ornament, he set it in majesty; but they made the images of their abominations and their detestable things therein.” God gave beauty, and they used it to make other gods. A gift put to another use: this is the deepest pain of the verse. So: “Therefore I have made it to them as an unclean thing.” The temple they were proud of, the one they thought could never fall, became in God’s mouth an unclean thing.
@@3151@@
Verse 22 has two sentences to read slowly: “I will also turn my face from them, and they will profane my secret place. Robbers will enter into it, and profane it.” What the “secret place” is, interpreters see differently: some say it is the innermost part of the temple, the Most Holy Place; others say it is what God treasures, meaning his people or his precious possession. Whichever it is, the main point is the same. Please notice “turn my face.” What is a blessing? A blessing is the face turned toward you. The face turned away is judgment. Outsiders break into the sanctuary, not because the walls are too low, but because the One inside has already turned his face away. The church walls can be very high and the meetings very lively, but if his face has turned away, we may not know it. The most dangerous thing is not that he is angry, but that he quietly turns his face away while we are still busy.
@@3152@@
Verse 23 says: “Make chains; for the land is full of bloody crimes, and the city is full of violence.” The prophet is to make chains, because prisoners are coming. Reading this, I think of those who are oppressed, those who have been eaten alive, those for whom no one speaks. God has not failed to see; the violence of this land, he has seen. So this chapter is comfort to the oppressed and a warning to the oppressor. The same words, heard by two kinds of people, are heard as two different things.
@@3153@@
Verses 25 and 26: “Destruction comes! They will seek peace, and there will be none. Mischief will come on mischief, and rumor will be on rumor; they will seek a vision of the prophet; but the law will perish from the priest, and counsel from the elders.” These are three roads: the prophet’s vision, the priest’s law, the elders’ counsel. In ordinary times all three roads are there, but nobody walks them seriously. When trouble comes, people go to seek them, and the roads are cut off. Please notice that this is not telling us to doubt every pastor and elder. It says that when a community keeps refusing to listen, in the end even the channels have no word to carry. The ear has to learn, in ordinary times, to know his voice.
@@3154@@
Verse 27 is the ending of the whole chapter: “The king will mourn, and the prince will be clothed with desolation. The hands of the people of the land will be troubled. I will do to them after their way, and according to their own judgments I will judge them. Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” From king to people, there is no one who can stand above it. But please look at the direction of the last word: it is not “then they will be destroyed,” it is “then they will know that I am Yahweh.” From beginning to end of this chapter, God’s mind is on one thing only: that people would know him.
@@3155@@
God says, “I will turn my face from them.” Do believers today still face such a thing?
@@3156@@
We need to speak on two levels. The first is the gospel: the weight of the sentence “My eye will not spare you” has already been borne once and for all by the Son of God on the cross, and to everyone who turns back today, what they hear is not “I will not spare,” but “Come.” Those who trust in Christ do not have to live in the fear that God might turn his face away at any moment. The second is a warning: the Bible also has the warning to a church that lost its first love. A person can have lively meetings and high walls, and yet the heart slowly leaves him. These two levels do not conflict: one lets us rest secure, the other keeps us from taking things lightly. You can quietly ask yourself one question: when was the last time I truly sought his face? Do not blame yourself, and do not rush to do more; just today, turn your face toward him. His face has never left the one who turns back.
@@3157@@
The first deviation is to preach “the end has come” as a clock for counting days. What the text gives is “now” and “already,” a warning and not a calendar; the whole chapter has no date and does not tell people to prepare for any year. We want the congregation to turn around and loosen their grip, not to watch the years with more anxiety.
@@3158@@
The second deviation is to take “the one who strikes is I, Yahweh” of verses 4 and 9 and turn it toward the bereaved, the seriously ill, the unemployed, and the victimized, telling them to ask themselves whether God is striking them; or to tell someone who has been hurt, “You must repent, you must forgive,” while skipping over the violence of verse 23. This chapter was spoken to one particular city, one generation that kept refusing to turn back. We want those who suffer to be more willing to come to God with their wounds, not more afraid of being condemned.
@@3159@@
The third deviation is to preach “My eye will not spare you” as God’s temper. This is the God who spared for a very long time, finally saying enough, and verses 4, 9, and 27 all end with “Then you will know that I am Yahweh.” If we preach only intimidation and do not say that Christ has already taken on that “will not spare,” the congregation hears only judgment and does not hear “Come.” We want the congregation to see a God who is righteous and also long-suffering.
@@3160@@
The fourth deviation is to preach “because it has been the stumbling block of their iniquity” as meaning that wealth is sin and only the poor are spiritual. Verse 19 speaks of putting gold and silver in the place of God; money is not sin, and poverty is not a virtue. We want every person, rich or not, to ask more honestly: is it serving me, or is it replacing God?
@@3161@@
The fifth deviation is to bring people back by panic, or to preach this chapter as being about “others, not me,” or to use it to point out some individual or some group as the ones being judged. The fruit of Ezekiel is patience and a deeper love for the Lord, not hoarding, anxiety, and sleepless nights; for the “everyone” of verse 16, the speaker must first stand on the side of the listener. We want the congregation to go out loving the Lord more, not fearing the world more.
@@3162@@
I often hear people use great disasters to explain God’s will. How do I tell the difference, so that I do not get it wrong?
@@3163@@
You can use two questions to tell the difference. First, to whom was this verse spoken? Ezekiel 7:4 and 9 were spoken to one particular city, one generation that kept refusing to turn back; they were not spoken to someone who is weeping. To turn them toward the bereaved, the seriously ill, the unemployed, or those who have been hurt is to add another cut on top of the wound. The whole book of Job exists to correct the inference that “suffering must be punishment.” Second, after listening, do the hearers become more willing to come to God with their wounds, or more afraid that they are being struck by God? If the latter, that way of speaking has gone wrong. If you are the one speaking, please first stand on the side of the listener and say, “I need this too,” and do not stand in the judge’s seat. If you are the one who is suffering, please remember: God sees your tears, and this chapter is not here to put you on trial. If the pain is deep and long, please find a pastor or a professional to walk with you for a while.
@@3164@@
The first question: we shy away from talking about “the end.” When an elder mentions a will, we say, “Pfft, pfft, don’t say such unlucky things.” Talking about funeral arrangements, about illness, about the worst case, seems to make it come true the moment it is said. But Ezekiel 7:2 says that God himself said it twice to this land: the end has come. He is not afraid of this word, because he is the God who holds the end. You can begin like this: this month, find one family member and spend ten good minutes talking about one thing you have been avoiding, such as a medical checkup, a document, or an apology you have never said. After you have talked, the heart is often lighter. It is not a curse; it is preparation.
@@3165@@
The second question: we are used to putting our peace in “insurance”: savings, a house, our children’s education, connections, a stable job. “Better safe than sorry”: there is nothing wrong with that saying in itself. But verse 19 says that gold and silver “won’t satisfy their souls.” You can check it like this: write down the three things you are most afraid of losing, and next to each, honestly write one sentence: is it serving me now, or is it replacing God? There is no need to throw anything away right away; just see clearly where you put your peace.
@@3166@@
I work hard, save money, and plan for my family. Does that count as leaning on silver?
@@3167@@
Not necessarily. Planning for your family and working diligently are virtues the Bible praises, not sins. The key is not how much you have prepared, but the sentence in your heart: once I am prepared, I will be fine. When that sentence appears, preparation turns from a tool into a god. You can use three questions to tell the difference: First, if all of this were gone tomorrow, what would I have left? Second, am I afraid to do something God has put on my heart because of the number in my savings? Third, am I unable to be generous because I am afraid of losing? If your answers leave you a little uneasy, there is no need to blame yourself; bring it to the Lord and say honestly: this, I give to you. Someone who plans for his family is not someone who leans on silver; someone who lives only to feel secure is.
@@3168@@
VII. Living Out 321: Put Our Peace Back in His Hand
@@3169@@
Jesus is my Role Model. When he saw Jerusalem, he wept over the city. He knew better than anyone the end of this city, yet he did not look on coldly. Knowing the truth is not for condemning; it is for weeping, interceding, and turning toward him. When those who follow him hear “the end has come,” their first reaction is not to look at others, but to bow down.
@@3170@@
The Bible is my Standard. Ezekiel 7:26 says that when trouble comes, vision, law, and counsel are cut off. Do not wait until they are cut off to look for a road; let God’s word be my standard in ordinary times: read a passage every day, and let the ear come to know his voice.
@@3171@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. Ezekiel 7:17 says, “All hands will be feeble, and all knees will be weak as water.” It is only when we are weakest that we find out our strength was borrowed. The Holy Spirit is the One who is already with us before our hands go limp and our knees give way. Be quiet for a moment each day and ask: Lord, what is the one thing today that you want me to let go of?
@@3172@@
Let Jesus be King. The silver of verse 19 became a stumbling block because it sat in a place that did not belong to it. To let Jesus be King is to let him sit in the place where the silver used to sit. Selflessness is not having nothing; it is no longer relying on my own preparation, position, and reputation to guarantee that “I will be fine,” but entering into the Lord who gives peace.
@@3173@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we are fortunate enough to get through a hard time safely, let us not say, “Thank goodness I was prepared.” All the glory goes to the Lord who did not turn his face away, and who is willing to let us come back.
@@3174@@
Build God’s system. God’s system is a group of people who, before God’s voice becomes rare, remind one another: where do you put your peace? In such a community, some receive God’s word, some walk alongside, and some intercede; they do not wait until all three roads are cut off before they start looking for a road, but there is a road in ordinary times.
@@3175@@
What is the connection between “Let Jesus be King” and “loosen your grip on the silver”?
@@3176@@
The connection is direct. The silver became a stumbling block not because it exists, but because it sat on the throne. With our mouths we say Jesus is King, but when we meet something uncertain, our first reaction is often: let me prepare a little more. That is the silver speaking. To let Jesus be King is not to tell you not to prepare; it is that when you prepare, the one sitting on the throne is him, not your preparation. You can practice it like this: every morning, say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to hold on to today; what I most fear losing today; and today I put them back in your hand. After a while, you will find that letting go is not losing, but placing your peace in a place where it cannot be taken away. His hand is a hand that does not turn away its face.

VIII. Four Kinds of Heart, One Turning
@@3178@@
The proud person says: “I have prepared thoroughly; I will be fine.” The text says to him: the rod has blossomed, and pride has budded. The proud leave their proper position, put safety in their own hands, and try to take God’s place. Returning to our position means admitting: my peace is not in my preparation. First say one sentence to God: “Lord, I let go.”
@@3179@@
The fearful person says: “Seeing that the end has come, I am so afraid I cannot sleep.” The text does not want you to hoard, and it does not want you to be anxious. What God wants from you is to turn, not to be in a panic. The fearful can hand their fear to him and start letting go with the smallest thing: tonight, read one fewer piece of news that sets your heart racing, and read more of the sentence in Ezekiel 7:1, “Yahweh’s word came to me.”
@@3180@@
The ashamed person says: “I have delayed so long that it is already too late.” God says it twice to give a chance to someone who is willing to listen. As long as there is a “now” today, there is still a road. Shame says, “You are already too late.” The gospel says, “Come.” The ashamed do not have to fix themselves first; they only need to turn their face back today.
@@3181@@
The wounded person says: “I was violated, I was pressed down; where is God?” Verse 23 says that God sees the violence. This chapter is not here to put you on trial; it is here to tell you: he has not failed to see. The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story; those who accompany them should not rush to say “you must forgive,” but only say: “I am here, I am with you.”
@@3182@@
First, for five minutes every day, read only Ezekiel 7:1–9, say the words “Then you will know that I am Yahweh,” ask for nothing, and only listen. And write down the date.

Third, find a family member or friend you have been wanting to talk with but have kept avoiding, set a time, say his name, and spend ten minutes talking about one thing.

Fifth, find someone who is in hard circumstances, offer no opinions, ask only “How can I help you?”, and actually do one thing, saying the name and the date.

What is one thing I can do right away this week, so that I do not stop at only listening to the sermon?
@@3192@@
The simplest thing is to take a piece of paper today and write down the three things you are most afraid of losing, and next to each one honestly write one sentence: is it serving me now, or is it replacing God? No need to polish it, and no need to show it to anyone. Then say to God: Lord, this, I give to you. Next choose one small action you can do today: open your contacts, find someone you have been putting off, and send one line: “I thought of you; how have you been lately?” Or take one item that you set up only to make yourself feel secure and change it into giving to someone in need. This is the answer of chapter 7: turning around does not begin with a big resolution, but with a small action. At the next group meeting, just say: this week I did one thing, and there is no need to say what it was.
@@3193@@
What is most distinctive about chapter 7 is that it begins with one sentence and ends with one sentence. It begins with “Yahweh’s word came to me” and ends with “Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” In between, the end, the silver, the temple, and the chains are taken away, one after another. But there is not one word in the whole chapter spoken for the sake of destruction. Behind every sentence of judgment there is one purpose: that people would know him.
@@3194@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that the end is not a clock for counting days, but a bell that calls people to turn around; God wants us to put our peace back from the things in our hands into the hand of the One who gives peace; and God also sees every oppressed and weeping person. That God, in Jesus, came among us. He took on himself that sentence “My eye will not spare you,” and today, to a person who turns back, what he says is: Come.

The focus of this lesson is not which day, not how much you have prepared, and not how unsettled the world is, but Jesus, who turns his face toward you and waits for you to turn back.

About Twenty-Five Men with Their Backs to Yahweh’s Temple
@@3225@@
Is there a room in your home that guests never enter? Everyone has one in the heart. From the outside, everything looks as usual; inside, there are corners we dare not show to people, and dare not show to God, and we have even found reasons for ourselves: he does not mind, he does not see. Ezekiel chapter 8 is the story of God lifting the prophet out of his house and taking him to see the dark rooms of a temple. The wonderful thing is that what he saw first was not the abominations, but that the glory was still there. He did not come to catch you in your faults; he came to ask: Have you seen? Only after we are seen by him do we have the strength to see ourselves.
@@3226@@
The glory is still above the dark rooms; God asks again and again “Have you seen?”, not to shame us, but to turn us from having our backs to him to facing him again.
@@3227@@
Ezekiel was sitting in his house when God’s hand took him by a lock of his hair, and the Spirit lifted him up to the temple in Jerusalem, where the glory was still there. In order, he was taken to see four scenes: the image of jealousy at the north gate; the seventy elders with censers in their hands, in the room with pictures on the wall; the women at the door weeping for Tammuz; and the twenty-five men in the inner court with their backs to the temple, worshiping the sun. Four times God asks “Have you seen?”, connecting the abominations in the temple with the violence in the land, and finally says he will not spare and will not hear their cry.
@@3228@@
“Have you seen?” (רָאִיתָ): The glory is still above the dark rooms; he comes to look so that people may be honest, not to shame them.
@@3229@@
The glory is “still there” (present tense): he did not leave because of our dark rooms; he first holds a person up, and then lets him see. This is the order of grace.
@@3230@@
Selflessness is no longer needing two rooms, one to show people and one for ourselves. Humility is admitting that our face once turned away, and letting that hand turn our face back.
@@3231@@
“Where are you? I am here. You do not have to see it all at once. I will be with you, and we will walk room by room.”

① Preaching this chapter as “there are idols everywhere in the church, find them”
8:5--16 are four scenes in a vision, not a list of dark rooms for us to use to point out a certain brother or sister or a certain ministry team.
Test: after listening, do people go looking for other people’s dark rooms, or do they first open their own door?
@@3250@@
② Turning the four scenes into an exact map or a ranking
The focus of the text is “Have you seen?”, not the building layout or the rank of the abominations; interpreters do not agree on the details.
Test: does the sermon force any scene to correspond to some modern practice?
@@3251@@
③ Preaching the women weeping for Tammuz as “weeping is sin”
8:14 rebukes where the tears were going, not the tears; take special care when the bereaved and the wounded are present.
Test: do people in grief, after listening, still feel they can weep before God?
@@3252@@
④ Implying that the bereaved and the wounded “do not have enough faith”
Do not equate anyone’s grief with the women weeping for an idol; do not press for details, and do not urge “be strong.”
Test: is there any sentence that makes someone feel that their weeping is sin?
