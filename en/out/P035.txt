@@11562@@
The third deviation is to preach Tyre’s end as “doing business is sin” or “wealth means God hates you,” or to say to someone who is out of work, bankrupt, or sick: this is what you get for leaning on a wall. What the text condemns is a heart that treats other people’s pain as an opportunity, a heart that leans on its walls. We want businesspeople and those with wealth to learn compassion and dependence, and we want those who are losing things to be accompanied, not condemned.
@@11563@@
The fourth deviation is to take the “Sheol” of verses 19 to 21 and use it to pass judgment on someone who has died. The text speaks of a proud city, not of anyone’s loved one. We want the bereaved to be comforted, not judged.
@@11564@@
The fifth deviation is to use “Nebuchadnezzar is God’s instrument” to defend anyone’s violence or persecution, or to preach chapter 26 as judgment only and forget the line in verse 20: “I will set glory in the land of the living.” We want victims to know that God sees, and we want those who listen to end up knowing God better and loving the Lord more, not left with nothing but fear.
@@11565@@
First question: we are very used to treating another person’s exit as our own opportunity in the marketplace and the workplace. “He is gone, so his seat is mine.” “That shop is closing, so its customers are mine.” This is almost called being realistic. Chapter 26 says: God heard that click of the abacus. You can begin like this: the next time you hear that a colleague or a competitor is in trouble, ask yourself, “Is my first thought for him, or for me?” Then do one small thing: make a phone call and ask, “Do you need help?” Not for business, only for him.
@@11566@@
Second question: we are very used to building our sense of security on walls: savings, a house, degrees, connections, a steady job. The older generation says, “Be prepared and you will have no worries.” That saying is not wrong in itself, and God never told people not to save. Chapter 26 asks something else: has this wall become my god? You can check like this: list the three walls you lean on most, and ask of each one, “If it were gone tomorrow, what would I have left?” What remains is what you truly lean on.
@@11567@@
Chinese people often say, “Be prepared and you will have no worries.” Does that mean savings and insurance are all “leaning on a wall”?
@@11568@@
No. Preparing and depending are two different things. The Bible praises the wisdom of preparing, and it never tells people to make no plans. The problem is when preparation becomes the source of our security and takes God’s place. You can check with a simple question: when my wall starts to shake a little, is my first reaction to grip it tighter, or to come to God first? The first deserves caution; the second is dependence. You prepare so that you can better care for your family and help others; you depend on God so that you can rest. The two can exist together, but there is only one master. Starting this week, before each financial or career decision, pause and say to God, “I hand this over to you.”
@@11569@@
VII. Living Out 321: Not Leaning on Walls, Leaning Only on the One
@@11570@@
Jesus is my Role Model. When he entered Jerusalem, there was also a city before his eyes that was about to fall. He did not calculate what that city’s fall would mean for him; he wept over it (Luke 19:41). When others’ doors were broken, he did not step into their place; he shed tears. Those who follow him learn this order: see the pain first, the opportunity later.
@@11571@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 26 not to compare it with history, but to let God’s word ask us: “Is there any place in my heart where ‘I am against you’ is said to me?” God’s word is a mirror; it shows me first, and only then do we talk about others.
@@11572@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. That thought, “So now I can…,” often comes so fast that we cannot stop it, and then it is the Holy Spirit who lights it up. He does not come to shame us; he comes to remind us: do you see it? Be quiet for ten minutes each day and ask: “Lord, which wall am I leaning on today?”
@@11573@@
Let Jesus be King. Self-centredness puts myself at the center and treats another person’s gap as my own chance to step in. Pride leaves man’s position, seizes God’s authority, and wants to take God’s place. Selflessness is not having no feelings; it is putting down the abacus and letting Jesus be King: my abundance does not depend on someone else’s collapse; it depends on his provision. Let man be man, and let God be God.
@@11574@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If our business stands firm and our walls have not fallen, we must not say, “I built it well.” Everything is his grace, and the glory belongs to him. God himself says he will set glory in the land of the living: it is not that our names are sought out, but that his beauty stands among us.
@@11575@@
Build God’s system. God’s community is a group of people who do not treat a brother’s or sister’s exit as their own opportunity. Here, when someone loses a job, everyone first keeps them company and does not compare; when someone falls, we first crouch down beside them and do not take their place. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: if there is still an abacus in our life, there will be competition in our relationships and turf in our ministry.
@@11576@@
“Glory in the land of the living” sounds very far away. How can I live it out today?
@@11577@@
You can begin in a very small place. Verse 20 says God will set his beauty in the land of the living, and this “land of the living” is the office, the kitchen, the classroom, and the bus stop you walk through today. There is one thing you can do today: at a moment when you would normally say “So now I can…,” change it to “How can I help you?” This turn has no applause, yet it is the way his beauty lands on the earth. God’s glory often does not appear on a big stage; it begins with someone who puts down the abacus, with the moment you are willing to let him be King. You do not need to wait until you are ready. One small action today is enough.
@@11578@@
The proud type says, “I rely on my own strength. This is just how the business world is.” The text says to him: Tyre, I am against you. The proud person treats the wall as his own achievement; to return to one’s position is to admit that the wall is borrowed and the owner is him. You can start by saying to God: “Lord, I rely on you.”
@@11579@@
The fearful type says, “I am sure of nothing right now. Is God also saying ‘I am against you’ to me?” No. That sentence was said to a proud city, not to a frightened person. God hears your fear. The fearful type needs to learn to change “gripping every wall” into “coming to him first,” beginning with the smallest thing: tell him one worry.
@@11580@@
The ashamed type says, “I too have taken advantage of someone else’s fall and picked up a bargain. What right do I have?” Shame says: you are that kind of person. The gospel says: you have seen it, and this is the starting point of turning back. When the city of Tyre fell, God did not stop at judgment; he will set glory in the land of the living. You do not have to clean yourself up first before you come to him.
@@11581@@
The wounded type says, “When I fell, someone took my place the moment I did. I don’t want to trust people anymore.” God does not blame you for your wound. He sees your pain, and he also sees the one who took advantage. Wounded one, you can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story. To the one who accompanies: do not rush to say, “You need to forgive.” Just say, “I am here. I am with you.”
@@11582@@

@@11584@@

@@11585@@

@@11586@@

I declare: God stands on my side and also on the side of my opponent; I will not treat another person’s fall as my own opportunity.
@@11588@@

@@11589@@

@@11590@@
I declare: my abundance is in his provision; my name is in his hand.
@@11591@@
The simplest thing is to take a piece of paper today and write: “The ‘So now I can…’ thought in my heart lately is ______.” You do not have to show it to anyone. Then say to God: “Lord, I see it.” Next, choose one small action you can do today: send a word to a colleague or fellow worker who has just run into trouble: “I thought of you. I am here when you need me.” At the next group meeting, just say: “This week I did one thing,” and there is no need to say what it was. The answer of chapter 26 often begins with a small turn like this: not tearing down every wall at once, but asking today, “What am I leaning on right now, the wall or the One?”
@@11592@@
Chapter 26 is like a great tide, rising from the seashore: first the click of the abacus in Tyre, then God standing before her, then the horses’ hooves, the battering rams, the songs falling silent, and finally the deep waters and the pit. But before the last verse there is one line that is different: “I will set glory in the land of the living.” Judgment is not the last sentence of the story.
@@11593@@
So today’s lesson is not to blame businesspeople, and not to fear the falling of walls. It is to ask ourselves: where is the abacus in my heart? Which wall am I leaning on? The Jesus who wept outside the city of Jerusalem is our Role Model and also our Lord.
@@11594@@

The focus of this lesson is not how strong Tyre was, nor how great Nebuchadnezzar was, nor how thick your wall is, but the Jesus who stands before you and weeps for you.

<p><strong>① How do we read “ivory inlaid in boxwood from the isles of Kittim”?</strong> The original שֵׁן בַּת־אֲשֻׁרִים is literally “ivory, daughter of Asshurites,” which does not read smoothly.</p><ul><li><strong>Boxwood</strong>: read בַּת־אֲשֻׁרִים as one word, a kind of wood (the CUV and most modern translations)</li><li><strong>Proper name</strong>: treat it as a place name or a tribal name, meaning unclear</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: ivory inlaid in boxwood.</strong> The literal wording is uncertain, but the meaning of the whole sentence is clear: the deck was assembled from precious materials.</p></blockquote><p><strong>② The repairers of seams:</strong> מַחֲזִיקֵי בִדְקֵךְ is literally “those who strengthen your cracks.” They were old hands, not decoration; how good the ship was depended on them.</p><p><strong>③ The second half of verse 8:</strong> the CUV renders only the first half (the inhabitants of Sidon and Arvad were rowers). The original continues with “Your wise men, Tyre, were in you. They were your pilots,” and the literal translation here has added it.</p>
@@11659@@
<p><strong>Of this most beautiful ship, almost not one piece of wood grew by herself.</strong></p><p>The prophet is like an inspector, starting from the bottom of the ship and checking one thing at a time: the planks are fir from Senir, the mast is cedar from Lebanon, the oars are oak from Bashan, the deck is inlaid with ivory, the sail is embroidered fine linen from Egypt, and the awning is blue and purple cloth from the isles of Elishah. The rowers are people of Sidon and Arvad; the repairers of seams are the old men of Gebal.</p><p>After reading this list, you notice one thing: almost nothing on the list was supplied by Tyre herself. She is an enormous gathering point, and the timber, the craftsmanship, the manpower, and the cloth all came from elsewhere. Her greatness was borrowed, bought, and hired.</p><p>This is not mocking her for lacking ability. Knowing how to borrow, buy, and hire is itself an ability. The problem is in the previous verse: she took this beauty pieced together from others and said, “I am perfect in beauty.”</p><p>Landing: think about who you are today. Who taught you what you know? Who polished your speech? Who gave you your body, your children, and the job in your hands? When the list has been checked off, how much of “I” is left? What remains is the place where we should bow down and give thanks.</p>

<p><strong>① Who are the גַּמָּדִים?</strong> This word appears only this once in the whole Old Testament.</p><ul><li><strong>Valiant men</strong>: the CUV takes this meaning, following some ancient versions</li><li><strong>Proper name</strong>: some tribe or place name</li><li><strong>Watchmen</strong>: inferred from the context, guards on the watchtowers</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: guards in the watchtowers (valiant men).</strong> The meaning of the word cannot be certain, but it does not affect the direction of the whole verse: they hung up their shields and “perfected” Tyre’s beauty.</p></blockquote>
@@11662@@
<p><strong>Even her security was hired in.</strong></p><p>The ship was borrowed, the people were hired, and so was the army. Persians, Lydians, and Put were originally other peoples, serving as soldiers in Tyre’s camp; the men of Arvad stood on the city walls; shields and helmets were hung on the walls “to display your splendor.”</p><p>Notice the verb “hang” (תִּלּוּ). The shields were hung up, not only for fighting but also for looking at. It is like a decoration: see how many guards my walls have.</p><p>The original says twice that they “perfected your beauty” (כָּלְלוּ יָפְיֵךְ). In verse 4, the builders of the ship made her beautiful; in verse 11, the defenders of the city made her beautiful. The beauty of the whole city was outsourced layer by layer: outsourced timber, outsourced hands, outsourced security.</p><p>Landing: we are also very good at “outsourcing” security: the figure in the bank account, the list of contacts, insurance, degrees. None of these is bad; it is just that they are all shields hung on the wall. What can truly protect us is not how much is hung on the wall, but who is with us.</p>

<p><strong>① What kind of merchandise is בְּנֶפֶשׁ אָדָם?</strong> Literally it is “the life of man,” “man.”</p><ul><li><strong>Slaves</strong>: people who are sold (the reading of most commentaries)</li><li><strong>Labor</strong>: a general reference to hired hands</li></ul><p>It is preceded by “Javan, Tubal, Meshech” and followed by “bronze”: people and bronze are priced together in a single sentence.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: people who are sold.</strong> The CUV’s rendering “population” is a euphemism.</p></blockquote>
@@11665@@
<p><strong>In the list of goods, there is a line that says “persons.”</strong></p><p>From this verse on, the prophet begins to recite a very long list of goods. Silver, iron, tin, and lead from Tarshish, vessels of bronze from Javan, Tubal, and Meshech, horses and mules from Togarmah, ivory and ebony from Dedan. Place names follow one after another, from the far west to the north, all sending goods to Tyre and taking goods from Tyre.</p><p>In this list, there is a phrase in verse 13 that should be read slowly: בְּנֶפֶשׁ אָדָם, “the life of man.” The CUV renders it “population.” It is wedged between silver, iron, and bronze, in the same tone. A human being has been put on the list of goods.</p><p>The text adds no comment, simply noting it flatly. But “adding no comment” is itself the prophet’s way of writing: he lets the list speak for itself. A commercial system that counts “people” as merchandise, God has already seen.</p><p>Landing: any kind of work or system that slowly begins to see people as numbers, this tendency deserves our alertness. Whether we are bosses, supervisors, or parents, we can ask: today, have I treated a person as a “usefulness”?</p>

<p><strong>① “Wheat of Minnith, and pannag”</strong></p><p>Both words are uncertain: is מִנִּית a place name (compare the Minnith of Judg 11:33) or a kind of grain? Is פַנַּג some kind of bread, millet, or another food?</p><ul><li><strong>Wheat of Minnith</strong>: take מִנִּית as a place name, the wheat fields of the land of Ammon (the CUV)</li><li><strong>Some kind of crop or food</strong>: do not take it as a place name, but read the words as a list of food names</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: follow the CUV; the wheat comes from Minnith, and there is another food called פַנַּג.</strong> The details of these two words are uncertain, but this does not affect the point of this verse: what Judah and the land of Israel sold was the most ordinary local produce.</p></blockquote>
@@11668@@
<p><strong>The name of Yahweh’s people is also on this list of goods.</strong></p><p>The list goes on: Aram sends precious stones and fine linen, Damascus sends wine of Helbon and white wool, Vedan and Javan send bright iron, cassia, and calamus. Then, in verse 17, a familiar name appears: Judah and the land of Israel.</p><p>What did they sell? Wheat, pannag, honey, oil, and balm. Things that grew in the field, were baked in the oven, were taken from the hive, and were pressed from the olive tree. The most ordinary, most down-to-earth things.</p><p>The produce of the promised land went into Tyre’s warehouses. This is not an accusation, and the text does not condemn this transaction. It only lets the reader see that for a seaport city to be so lavish, many people’s fields, kilns, and hives were feeding her. They also lived by her.</p><p>So Tyre’s fall is not only her own matter. The people who sell wheat will lose their buyer, and those who store the goods will lose their market. The ripples of judgment will spread all the way to the fields.</p><p>Landing: how tightly are we tied to a huge system? A company, an industry, a person who has the final say. Living off it is not sin. Just leave a place in your heart so that he knows: I do not live by it; I live by him.</p>

<p><strong>① What is שָׁרוֹתַיִךְ?</strong> The root of this word is uncertain.</p><ul><li><strong>Your caravans</strong>: read as “your carriers, your convoys” (the CUV and most translations)</li><li><strong>Sang for you</strong>: inferred from שִׁיר (to sing): “the ships of Tarshish sang of your merchandise” (as in the King James Version)</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the fleet that carried the goods.</strong> The whole passage up to here has been about goods, and here it goes on to speak of the ships that carried them, which follows naturally.</p></blockquote><p><strong>② The two layers of meaning in וַתִּכְבְּדִי:</strong> the basic meaning of כָּבֵד is “heavy,” extended to “having weight, honored.” Both layers of meaning exist here: the ship was loaded full, and she was also made glorious.</p>
@@11671@@
<p><strong>The ship was filled, and also made heavy. “Glory” and “heavy” are the same root in the original.</strong></p><p>The list of goods goes on to the end: flocks from Arabia and Kedar, spices, precious stones, and gold from Sheba and Raamah, Haran, Canneh, Eden, Asshur, Chilmad; even the wrappings of blue cloth, the cords for bundling, and the cedar chests for clothing are all clearly recorded. Finally comes the fleet of Tarshish, sailing in convoy and carrying her goods.</p><p>Then a one-line summary: וַתִּמָּלְאִי וַתִּכְבְּדִי מְאֹד, “You were filled, and you were made very glorious.”</p><p>That “glorious” (כָּבֵד) originally means “heavy.” In Hebrew, glory means “having weight.” A fully loaded ship sits deep in the water: it is glory, and it is also weight. The same word is half splendor and half danger. This root will be used of God in the next chapter: “I will be glorified among you” (28:22).</p><p>Tyre took her merchandise as her glory. God will take himself as his glory. Only one glory will not sink the ship.</p><p>Landing: our lives too are loading things into the ship: titles, achievements, relationships, responsibilities. Being full is not necessarily bad; but we should know where the waterline of the ship is. Some weights are ones he does not want us to carry.</p>

<p><strong>① What kind of wind is the “east wind”?</strong> The original רוּחַ הַקָּדִים is a hot, dry, strong wind blowing from the east. In the Bible it is often a tool God uses: it dried up the Red Sea (Exod 14:21), scorched Jonah (Jonah 4:8), and broke the ships of Tarshish (Ps 48:7).</p><ul><li><strong>Natural weather</strong>: a real storm at sea</li><li><strong>Figurative language</strong>: the “east wind” stands for a military force about to come</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: both layers exist together.</strong> The text borrows the storm every seafarer knows to speak of a judgment under God’s control; who exactly it is and by what means, the text does not say in detail, and we need not ask in detail.</p></blockquote>
@@11674@@
<p><strong>It was her own rowers who brought her into the great waters.</strong></p><p>When the list of goods is finished, the rhythm suddenly changes: “Your rowers have brought you into great waters. The east wind has broken you in the heart of the seas.”</p><p>The first verb is הֱבִיאוּךְ, “they have brought you.” Those who took her out were the rowers she hired, the very people who made her beautiful and kept her running. They meant no harm; they only followed her direction, stroke by stroke, rowing to a place too far away.</p><p>Then comes the east wind. Psalm 48:7 says: “With the east wind, you break the ships of Tarshish.” The east wind is a wind under God’s control, and it listens to no captain’s command.</p><p>The most important four words in the whole chapter are בְּלֵב יַמִּים, “in the heart of the seas.” In verse 4, her borders are in the heart of the seas; in verse 25, she is made very glorious in the heart of the seas; in verse 26, she is broken in the heart of the seas; in verse 27, everything falls into the heart of the seas. The place of her pride is her tomb.</p><p>This verse lists no charges. The wind only proves that the most beautiful position of this ship is also the position with the least shelter.</p><p>Landing: the highest place we stand is often the place where the wind is strongest. It is not that we are asked to hide in a corner, but that we ask the Lord to let us know, even in our most glorious moments, that the wind is under his control.</p>

<p><strong>The ship sank, and at last someone truly wept.</strong></p><p>Everyone before this was counting: which goods were lost, which customers were gone. Here, the sailors and the pilots come down from the ship and stand on the shore. These are the people who were truly with the ship, with the ropes and the oars in their hands every day, who knew the ship’s temperament best.</p><p>What they do is a complete rite of mourning: dust on their heads, rolling in ashes, shaved heads, sackcloth at their waists, and loud weeping. Every gesture uses the whole body. They are not squeezing out tears; their whole selves fall down.</p><p>The text does not tell them not to weep. It first lets the weeping be there, so that a fallen city has someone who weeps for her. Even for a city under judgment, the Bible does not hold back tears. God himself is like this: “I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked” (33:11).</p><p>The last sentence asks: “Who is there like Tyre, like her who is silent in the midst of the sea?” From here on, the sea no longer has any noise.</p><p>Landing: if you are mourning over something you have lost, you do not need to hurry to tell yourself, “Be strong.” The text first lets people weep until they have wept enough. He does not rush us; he draws near to the brokenhearted.</p>

<p><strong>① What sound is שָׁרְקוּ (hiss)?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Horror</strong>: seeing a great disaster, drawing a sharp breath (compare Jer 49:17)</li><li><strong>Mockery</strong>: a hiss that kicks someone when they are down (compare Lam 2:15)</li><li><strong>Both together</strong>: one sound, two feelings</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: a hiss in which astonishment and calculation are intertwined.</strong> The merchants’ hiss is half shock and half recalculation.</p></blockquote>
@@11680@@
<p><strong>She fed many peoples, and now everyone, from far away, goes “hiss.”</strong></p><p>Verse 33 is the warmest sentence in this chapter: “When your wares went out of the seas, you filled many peoples. You enriched the kings of the earth with the multitude of your riches and of your merchandise.” Tyre was once truly a benefactor to many. This is not sarcasm, nor a charge made up out of nothing.</p><p>Then verse 34 closes it with a “now”: “In the time that you were broken by the seas, in the depths of the waters, your merchandise and all your company fell within you.”</p><p>The last picture of the whole chapter is of the onlookers: the people of the islands are stunned, the kings look grieved, and the merchants among the peoples “hiss at you.” That “hiss” (שָׁרְקוּ) can be astonishment, or it can be mockery. When a great market is gone, the first reaction in a businessperson’s heart is often: now, who will I sell my goods to?</p><p>The last line is like the one in chapter 26: “You have become a terror, and you will be no more.” But here there is one more phrase: “forever.” This is the final position of the city that placed herself in the “heart of the seas”: forever not existing.</p><p>Landing: being needed by many people is good, but it is not the root of life. When people need us, may we serve humbly; when people no longer need us, may our root still be in him.</p>
