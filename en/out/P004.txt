@@1023@@
<strong>He first takes away the prophet’s voice, and only then lets him be his mouth.</strong><p>The tongue sticks to the roof of the mouth. This is a very concrete picture: he cannot speak. He makes him mute, not as a punishment, but to take away the prophet’s own words and leave only his.</p><p>Look at the dividing line: “not able to correct them.” The prophet can no longer reprove in his own name, cannot voice opinions, cannot argue.</p><p>Then comes a conditional sentence: “But when I speak with you.” When he speaks, the prophet opens his mouth. The mouth is not his own; it is lent to him.</p><p>And the content of what he says is just one sentence: This is what the Lord Yahweh says. Those who hear, let them hear; those who refuse, let them refuse. And the same closing words again: a rebellious house.</p><p>Landing: What you most need to learn may not be how to speak better, but when not to speak. Wait for him to open your mouth, and then that one sentence will carry weight.</p>

I. The Word You Eat Is the Word You Can Speak (3:1–3)
@@1323@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. When I was a child and caught a cold, my mother cooked a bowl of medicine, so bitter that my face scrunched into a knot. She said nothing, turned, took honey from the cupboard, and put a small spoonful into the bowl. I drank it in one gulp, and my tongue was sweet, but the medicine was still medicine, and after it went down, it was bitter all the way to my stomach. I have always remembered this picture. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 3, and it opens with something to be eaten: not medicine, but a scroll. Sweet as honey in the mouth, yet with weight in the belly.
@@1324@@
In verse 1 God says to him: “Son of man, eat what you find. Eat this scroll, and go, speak to the house of Israel.” Please notice the order: first eat, then speak. Not first memorize the manuscript, not first learn eloquence, but first eat. Verse 2 says: “So I opened my mouth, and he caused me to eat the scroll.” The prophet’s mouth was opened by himself, but the scroll was fed by God. Obedience is my opening my mouth; the supply is what he gives.
@@1325@@
Verse 3 says again: “Son of man, cause your belly to eat, and fill your bowels with this scroll that I give you.” Then I ate it; and it was as sweet as honey in my mouth. The word is not kept in a pocket, not a bookmark tucked in the Bible, but put into the belly and turned into flesh and blood. When a person speaks words that he has not first digested within himself, the listeners can very quickly smell it: it was brought in from outside, and did not grow out of the belly.
@@1326@@
Please think: why “sweet”? The content of the scroll, as we read later, was lamentation, mourning and woe. The content itself is not sweet. What is sweet is “this was given to me by him.” The very same heavy sentence, from a stranger’s mouth, is a knife; from the mouth of a father who loves you, you are able to hear it. The taste of a word often depends on who hands it over. The sweetness the prophet tasted was the hand that passed him the scroll.
@@1327@@
There was a sister who sold vegetables at the market. Every day she got up at four in the morning and read one verse at her vegetable stall. She said she did not know how to preach. One day the neighbor at the next stall was in a very bad mood, and she opened her mouth and said just one sentence: I read recently that God’s word is sweeter than honey. The other person was stunned for a moment, and said: Really? I would like a taste too. She gave no big argument; she simply said what she had tasted herself. For one who has tasted, the mouth naturally has flavor when it opens.
@@1328@@
When I read the Bible I often feel dry, with no “sweet as honey” feeling. Is something wrong with me?
@@1329@@
Not necessarily. The sweetness of verse 3 is the experience of the prophet after he obeyed and ate, and in verse 14 of the same chapter he also went “in bitterness.” Sweet and bitter stand side by side; it is not that only the sweet counts as spiritual. If you feel dry, you can start by making the portion smaller: read only three verses a day, say them slowly out loud, and after you finish, stop for one minute and ask yourself: What did this sentence say to me today? The flavor often lies not in reading more, but in chewing slowly. If this dryness has lasted a long time, and comes with deep tiredness or low mood, please talk with a pastor or a trusted companion. You do not need to carry it alone.

II. A Forehead Like a Diamond, and a Bitter Heart (3:4–15)
@@1331@@
In verses 4 to 6, what God says is very interesting. You are sent not to a people of a strange speech and of a hard language, but to the house of Israel; not to many peoples whose words you can’t understand. Then comes a very heavy sentence: “Surely, if I sent you to them, they would listen to you.” It means: language is not the obstacle. People of the same land and the same tongue are, instead, the hardest ones to speak to. A stranger who doesn’t understand is a problem of the ears; one of our own who will not listen is a problem of the heart.
@@1332@@
Verse 7 makes it plain: “But the house of Israel will not listen to you, for they will not listen to me; for all the house of Israel are obstinate and hard-hearted.” It is not that they refuse to listen to the prophet; they refuse to listen to God, and the prophet only stands in the middle and takes his share. If you have ever spoken many times at home to your parents, your spouse, your children, and they simply would not listen, please remember: it is not necessarily because you spoke badly. Sometimes the one they really will not listen to is the One behind you.
@@1333@@
So God says: “I have made your face hard against their faces, and your forehead hard against their foreheads.” He also says the forehead is like a diamond, harder than flint. Please notice that “I have made.” This is not telling him to train himself a hard face; it is equipment given by God. Some people preach this verse as telling people to become unafraid of pain and not to shed tears, and that is preaching it off the mark, because verse 14 follows right away and says the prophet himself went “in bitterness, in the heat of my spirit.” The forehead is hard, but the heart is soft. Both things are in one person at the same time.
@@1334@@
In verses 12 to 15, the Spirit lifts him up, and he hears the voice of a great rushing: “Blessed be Yahweh’s glory from his place.” Then he comes to Tel Aviv, to those of the captivity living by the River Chebar. He did something very special: he sat there overwhelmed among them seven days. No preaching, no prophesying, no explaining. The text does not tell us the name of his illness, and we do not add one for him. We only see one thing: God’s servant first sat down, and sat in the middle of the people.
@@1335@@
There is one more detail: he was taken by the Spirit, and yet he went in bitterness. Obedience does not always come with a good mood. Some people think that if it is God’s leading, the heart must surely be at peace and joyful, but in fact the Bible has quite a few servants who walked in God’s way with tears in their eyes. A bitter heart does not mean you took the wrong road; and being willing does not necessarily mean smiling.
@@1336@@
Have you ever accompanied someone who had just sent off a loved one? You sat beside him, unable to say anything, able only to hand him a cup of water. At that moment you felt very useless. But these seven days tell us: there are some words that can be spoken only by first sitting. Before God’s word came, there was a stretch of road, and it was companionship.
@@1337@@
God makes a person’s forehead harder than others’. Does that mean I also have to become very strong and not show weakness?
@@1338@@
No. The hardness in verses 8 and 9 is equipment from God, from the “I have made”; it is for standing firm in hard places, not for making a person cold. Look at the prophet himself: verse 14 says he went in bitterness, in the heat of his spirit, and he did not pretend that nothing was wrong. So you may hurt, and you may be held up. If someone uses this verse to tell you to toughen up and forbids you to cry, please know that this is not what this verse means. You can say to God: Lord, I am very tired; please give me strength to stand, and please keep my heart from becoming hard. You can also find someone who understands you, and tell him the one thing that has tired you most recently, so that you are not hard-pressed alone.

III. Light from the Original: Watching, Warning, Living (3:17, 21)
@@1340@@
Verse 16 says that at the end of seven days, Yahweh’s word came to him. The word came after the sitting. Verse 17 says: “Son of man, I have made you a watchman to the house of Israel. Therefore hear the word from my mouth, and warn them from me.” In this verse there are three words worth a closer look.
@@1341@@
The first is “watchman,” in Hebrew צֹפֶה (tsofeh). This word is for one who stands high and looks far. The watchman on the wall does not make the danger, and does not judge the enemy; his work is very simple: see, and then raise his voice. This word reminds us that the starting point of watching is “seeing.” A person unwilling to see the condition of those near him cannot watch. As you walked through your home, your workplace and your church today, did you truly see whose face looked wrong?
@@1342@@
The watchman standing on the wall has a very real limit: he sees farther than the people in the city, but he cannot live their days for them. What he can do is say what he has seen. So what a watchman needs most is not a heart of iron, but a pair of eyes willing to look, and a mouth that opens only at the right time.
@@1343@@
The second is “warn,” in Hebrew הִזְהִיר (hizhir). Its root זָהַר (zahar) carries the meaning of “to shine,” so to warn is to shine light on the road ahead, so that people can see where the pit is. This is not pointing a finger and scolding; it is lifting a lamp on a dark road. If you want to warn someone, first ask yourself: what am I holding in my hand, a lamp or a stone?
@@1344@@
The third is “live,” in Hebrew חָיָה (khayah). Verse 21 says: “he will surely live, because he took warning.” In the original the word is used twice, and literally it is “living, he shall live.” The whole passage on watching comes to rest on this word. God appoints the watchman not to condemn people, and not to make the watchman carry a guilt that leaves him unable to breathe, but so that people may live.
@@1345@@
Put the three words together: stand high and see (tsofeh), lift the light to show the road (hizhir), so that people may live (khayah). See, shine, live: that is the path of this passage. As for the sentence in verses 18 to 20, “I will require his blood at your hand,” it was said by God to this prophet who was clearly appointed a watchman, and whether it extends directly to every believer today, the views differ: some think it refers only to this office, and some think that shepherds who are entrusted also have this responsibility. Whichever way you take it, you must not use it to frighten the congregation.
@@1346@@
There was an elderly brother who stood for a while every evening at the entrance of the neighborhood. When he saw that a neighbor who lived alone had not come out, he would go upstairs and knock on the door, saying: I didn’t see you today, so I came to check. He was not the building manager, and no one had sent him. But the old people in that area all said he was more reliable than the doorbell. That is the taste of watching: not finding other people’s faults, but not letting anyone fall without a sound.
@@1347@@
“I will require his blood at your hand”: does this mean that for everyone I have not shared the gospel with, God will require their blood from me?
@@1348@@
This is the place in this chapter most easily preached too heavily. Verses 17 to 21 are God’s charge to Ezekiel, the one specially appointed as a watchman. The shepherds and apostles of the New Testament also had a similar sense of responsibility, so some think that everyone entrusted carries a duty to warn; others hold that this verse refers specifically to the prophet’s office. The views differ, and we do not need to force a single answer. What can be sure is this: what God wants is for people to turn and live (Ezekiel 3:21; 18:23). If you often lie awake at night thinking, “Did I harm someone?”, please stop first, and bring this worry to the Lord.
@@1349@@
IV. The Glory on the Plain, the Tongue That Sticks (3:22–27)
@@1350@@
In verse 22, Yahweh’s hand was there, and he said: Arise, go out into the plain, and I will talk with you there. Verse 23: “Behold, Yahweh’s glory stood there, like the glory which I saw by the river Chebar.” The glory did not leave because he was sitting in the land of exile, and was not absent because he was in a foreign land. He is by the river, and he is also on the plain. The place you go, he is already there first.
@@1351@@
Verse 24 says: “Then the Spirit entered into me, and set me on my feet.” Please notice that he was lying on the ground, and it was the Spirit who set him on his feet. We often think that standing up depends on willpower, but sometimes it is the Spirit who lifts a person up. Then God says: go, shut yourself inside your house. Verse 25 says they will put ropes on him and bind him. Verse 26 says: “I will make your tongue stick to the roof of your mouth, that you will be mute, and will not be able to correct them.”
@@1352@@
Did you notice? His first response was to fall on his face. He did not stand and converse, did not take out a notebook, but went down. Someone who has seen the glory naturally lowers his voice. Many times, we do not speak well, not because our eloquence is lacking, but because we have not fallen down enough. First fall down, then be lifted up, and only then open the mouth: this is the order God gave.
@@1353@@
About this action, the views differ: some read it as a literal muteness, some read it as a symbolic action, and some think he was not entirely silent, but spoke only what God spoke. These notes do not force a single explanation. We also do not treat it as a pattern for people to copy, and we certainly cannot use it to mock anyone who has difficulty speaking. We hold on only to verse 27: “But when I speak with you, I will open your mouth, and you shall tell them, ‘This is what the Lord Yahweh says.’”
@@1354@@
The mouth is in God’s hand. When he speaks, the prophet opens his mouth; when he does not speak, the prophet does not add one word for himself. This is exactly opposite to our instinct. We often think faithfulness means speaking more, until the other person takes it in. But here, even speaking more or less belongs to God. “He who hears, let him hear; and he who refuses, let him refuse.” The watchman’s responsibility is to say what God says, not to guarantee that the other will listen.
@@1355@@
There was a mother whose son had not been in touch with the family for many years. She said: Every day I want to talk, every day I want to send a message. But I learned one thing: I first ask the Lord, is this a sentence you want me to say? Sometimes the Lord says, just send a greeting. She said that in these two years she has spoken less, but every sentence, her son has read.
@@1356@@
Does “the tongue sticks to the roof of the mouth” mean that Christians should speak less and not express themselves?
@@1357@@
No. This is a special charge to Ezekiel, and the views on the details differ, so we do not force a single explanation. The point is: his mouth was in God’s hand, he opened it when God spoke, and when he opened it, it was “This is what the Lord Yahweh says.” This is not telling people to suppress themselves, and it cannot be used to demand that anyone be silent. What we learn is: before speaking, ask first, is this what he wants me to say? If speaking has always been hard for you, or if there was a season when you could not speak, this chapter is not condemning you; your worth does not lie in speaking much or little. This week you can try it once: before speaking, say this sentence quietly in your heart, and then open your mouth.
@@1358@@
The first deviation is to preach Ezekiel 3:17–21, “I will require his blood at your hand,” as saying that everyone who has not shared the gospel will be held to account for blood. This is God’s charge to the prophet who was clearly appointed a watchman, and whether it applies directly to every believer, the views differ, so we must not use intimidation as a basis. We want the congregation to love unbelievers more, not to feel more guilt.
@@1359@@
The second deviation is to preach watching as constantly judging others and digging out their sins. Watching is seeing and warning, not condemning; what God wants is for people to turn and live (Ezekiel 3:21). We want warning to have love as its foundation, not to take pleasure in finding fault.
@@1360@@
The third deviation is to diagnose the prophet’s seven days of being overwhelmed as depression or trauma, or to preach it as a way of praying that everyone must follow. The text gives no diagnosis, and we add none. We say only what the text says: he sat among the people, overwhelmed, and did not speak at once.
@@1361@@
The fourth deviation is to preach the tongue sticking to the roof of the mouth as saying Christians ought to be silent, or that God makes people disabled or takes away people’s speech. This was a special charge to Ezekiel, who only opened his mouth when God spoke. We want the congregation to learn to wait for God’s word, not to suppress themselves and be afraid to speak.
@@1362@@
The fifth deviation is to preach the forehead like a diamond as becoming a hard person who is never hurt, and to urge the wounded to be strong. This is equipment given by God, from “I have made,” not a hardness squeezed out of people; the prophet himself also went in bitterness. We want the wounded to be held up, not to be told they are not allowed to hurt.
@@1363@@
Someone near me keeps doing wrong. I am afraid that if I don’t speak I will harm him, and afraid that if I speak I will offend him. How do I tell?
@@1364@@
You can ask yourself three things. First, is this what he wants me to say? Second, am I speaking out of love, or out of anger? Third, after I say it, can I keep walking alongside him? If all three pass, choose a quiet time, and begin with “I see,” for example “I see that you have been very tired lately,” and not “You are just like this.” After speaking, leave the result with the Lord, because verse 27 says: he who hears, let him hear; and he who refuses, let him refuse. If the danger to the other person has already touched his personal safety, please also seek help from a pastor or a professional, and do not handle it alone. If you are still unsure, pray for him for three days first, and then decide whether to open your mouth; there is no need to rush.
@@1365@@
The first question: we value harmony very much, and “don’t offend people” is our wisdom for survival, so the words that ought to be said are often swallowed back. Yet Ezekiel 3:17 says the watchman is to warn. What do we do? Please remember: you are not the watchman of the whole world; you have been entrusted with only one small piece: family, colleagues, a small group. In that small piece, choose one person, choose a quiet time, begin with “I see,” and say one true word carried by love. Not criticism, but lifting a lamp. After saying it, keep him company.
@@1366@@
The second question: we are also used to saying too much: the nagging of elders, the suggestions in church, the forwards in group chats. We use saying more to prove that we care, saying it three times, five times. Verse 27 reminds us: “He who hears, let him hear; and he who refuses, let him refuse.” The responsibility is to speak; the result does not belong to me. You can practice this way: with a person to whom you have said a lot, this month say it only once, and then turn the rest of the words into prayer, and keep a place for him: when he wants to talk, you are there.
@@1367@@
I have spoken for many years and my family still does not believe. Have I failed?
@@1368@@
No. Verse 7 says it very clearly: they will not listen to the prophet because they will not listen to God, and the prophet’s responsibility is to say what God has said, and the result is not in his hands. Your worth does not lie in whether the other responds. Sometimes what you are entrusted with is a very long companionship. You can speak less and pray more, and each day say one sentence over his name: Lord, in your time, open his ears. Take care of yourself too, and find a companion, and each month talk about your weariness. Long years of disappointed hope really are tiring, and there is no need to pretend that nothing is wrong. May you still be held up by God in the long waiting.

VII. Living Out 321: Eat It First, Then Open Your Mouth
@@1370@@
Jesus is my Role Model. His words were what the Father commanded (John 12:49). He listened to the Father first, and then opened his mouth; when the Father did not speak, he did not add one word for himself. Those who follow him learn his pattern: like Ezekiel 3:1–3, first eat the word into the belly, and then speak it out.
@@1371@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 3 not to learn how to be a watchman who manages others, but to let the scroll first be eaten into ourselves. “Eat this scroll, and go, speak to the house of Israel”: the order is eat first, speak afterward; the standard lies in the scroll, not in my opinion.
@@1372@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. The Spirit lifted him up, and it was also the Spirit who set him on his feet on the plain (Ezekiel 3:14, 24). We do not rely on gritting our teeth, nor on eloquence. Each day, be quiet for a moment and ask one sentence: Lord, which word today is the one you want me to speak?
@@1373@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is to admit that when I close my mouth and when I open it is not up to me. What I need to lay down is the right of “I have an opinion, so I will speak”; when my mouth belongs to the King, I can speak quietly.
@@1374@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The glory stood on the plain (Ezekiel 3:23), and the prophet was only on the ground on his face. A watchman is no hero, and has no merit to boast of. If someone lives because he was warned, the glory goes to the Lord who makes people live.
@@1375@@
To build God’s system. Watching does not mean everyone is responsible for the whole world, and it does not mean everyone stands aside; each person is faithful within the range he has been entrusted with: family, workplace, church, each with the place that was pointed out. In such a community, people see one another and lift the lamp for one another, and no one falls without a sound.
@@1376@@
What does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with “Should I speak up?”
@@1377@@
The connection is direct. If Jesus is King, my mouth also belongs to him. Ezekiel 3:27 says that when he speaks with the prophet, he opens his mouth. This is not telling us to be afraid to speak, but to change “I want to say” into “he wants me to say.” You can practice this way: this week, at the moment when you most want to cut in and most want to correct someone, stop for three seconds first and say in your heart: Lord, is this sentence yours, or mine? If it is his, speak the truth in love; if not, be quiet, and pray one sentence for that person. After a while you will find that being quiet is also a kind of faithfulness. This kind of practice carries more weight than ten more sentences.
@@1378@@
The proud type says: “I have an opinion, so I will speak; if I don’t speak, who will?” The text says to him: the mouth is in God’s hand; when he speaks, you open your mouth. The proud one leaves his position and grasps the authority to open his mouth, wanting to speak in place of God; to return to one’s position is to admit: I am the one who passes on the word, and he is the one who speaks. You can try first once: before you want to correct someone, first pray one sentence for him.
@@1379@@
The fearful type says: “If I speak, I fear the relationship will break; if I don’t, I fear it is my sin.” God says: “Don’t be afraid of them, neither be dismayed at their looks.” The fearful need to learn: not by working up courage on their own, but by receiving the strength to stand that God gives. You do not need to say everything at once; just choose one sentence, and say it with love.
@@1380@@
The ashamed type says: “I once did not speak, and now I deeply regret it, and I have no right to speak again.” God appointed the watchman so that people may live, not so that someone carries that one silence for the rest of his life. Please come to the Lord with your regret, and do not carry it alone. He can let you open your mouth again today and say one loving sentence.
@@1381@@
The wounded type says: “I have said so much and no one listens; I am tired and do not want to speak again.” God does not blame you for your tiredness. The prophet also sat overwhelmed among the people seven days, and no one hurried him to get better. The wounded can first sit down and find someone willing to keep them company; the one who keeps company should not rush to say “You must have faith,” but say only: I am here.

I very much want to watch for my unbelieving family, but every time I think about it I am filled with guilt. What should I do?
@@1392@@
Guilt is usually a warning sign, reminding you that this matter has been preached too heavily. The purpose of watching in chapter 3 is for people to live, not for you to carry a responsibility that can never be finished. You can do this: first, write your family member’s name on paper, and say one short prayer for him each day. Second, watch for one small opportunity, and do only one thing at a time, for example a meal, a phone call, a favor. Third, leave the result with the Lord, because whether he listens or not is between him and God. If you find that the guilt has already left you unable to sleep or eat, please talk with a pastor or a trusted companion, and do not carry it alone.
@@1393@@
What is most special about chapter 3 is that it begins with a mouthful of honey, passes through seven overwhelmed days, and finally comes to rest in a shut room. At the beginning is eating the scroll, sweet as honey; in the middle is a forehead like a diamond, with a heart that is bitter; then comes the charge to watch; and last is the glory standing on the plain, the tongue stuck to the roof of the mouth, waiting only for him to speak. From eating to watching, from watching to muteness, this is a journey of handing one’s mouth over.
@@1394@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that his word is food, God wants us to see that watching is so that people may live, and God also wants us to know that our mouths are not our own. That God, in Jesus, came among us; he is the Lord who became flesh, who listened to the Father first and then opened his mouth, and who also sat among people and wept for them.
@@1395@@
So please remember the three key points of this lesson today: the word you eat, watching is for living, and the mouth is in his hand. The focus of this lesson is not whether you speak enough, not whether others are willing to listen, and not whether you have fulfilled the responsibility of the whole world, but Jesus, who put the word into your belly and who also holds you up.

What the besieged most want to hear is “the city will not fall.” But in this chapter, God does not say a word of comfort to them, and the prophet hardly opens his mouth. He tells him to take a tile, lie on his side, be bound with ropes, and eat bread and drink water by measure. This is saying: a siege is not a sudden disaster, but an everyday life tightened day by day, and sin has weight, and sin has length. Yet in the deepest part of this picture there is one who lies down for others, and an “Ah!” that God hears, and even the order about the dung fire is changed for him. He does not treat judgment lightly, and he does not treat people’s hardships lightly.
@@1427@@
Sin has weight, and judgment is real; but the prophet who lies down for others, and the God who hears that “Ah!”, already foretell the Lord who truly bears iniquity.
@@1428@@
God told the prophet to portray the besieged Jerusalem on a tile, and told him to lie on his side and bear the iniquity of the house of Israel and the house of Judah, and to eat bread by weight and drink water by measure. The bitterness of the siege, the weight of sin, the fear of the staff of bread being broken: all were acted out with the body for people to see. The prophet cried “Ah!”, and God changed the method, but did not cancel the truth.
@@1429@@
Sin is not a thought, but a weight that must be “lifted” (נָשָׂא); God lets the prophet’s body become a sign, so that people can see.
@@1430@@
The gospel in this chapter is a foretelling: the prophet could only “lie there and bear” a sign, and the one who truly bore iniquity is the Lord who finished it on the cross (now we can say: it has been done).
@@1431@@
Selflessness is not no longer feeling that it is hard, but bringing the “Ah!” before God, and still being willing to be bound by him and sent by him; I lay down my own dignity, so that what people see is him.
@@1432@@
“That cry of ‘Ah!’ you let out, I heard it. What I changed was the method, not my heart of love for you.”
