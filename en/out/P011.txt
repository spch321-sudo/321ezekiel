@@3689@@
The fourth deviation is to preach “the sun” as if every good thing were an idol, so that the congregation is afraid to enjoy God’s gifts; or to use “Yahweh doesn’t see us” to mock people who are struggling. Work, family and service are good in themselves. They become idols when they stand between God and people. Verse 12 is the elders finding reasons for their own choices; it is not a genuine question from a weak person. We must let those who are truly struggling still dare to ask God: Where are you?
@@3690@@
The fifth deviation is to preach only judgment and not “the glory is still there,” to say “I will not hear them” as if it were spoken to every person who prays, or to use panic to push people into “opening the dark rooms.” The order of this chapter is glory first, abominations after. Skip the glory, and it becomes intimidation. The fruit of Ezekiel is endurance and a deeper love for the Lord, not sleepless nights and going back to blame yourself over and over. We want the congregation to walk out loving the Lord more, not fearing themselves more.
@@3691@@
Are the four scenes in this chapter things that really happened in history, or symbols in a vision?
@@3692@@
Interpreters hold three views. Some say these were idolatrous practices that really existed in the temple in Jerusalem at that time, and the prophet was brought in the vision to see them. Some say the four scenes are symbols of the overall spiritual condition, and not every detail necessarily has a corresponding historical event. And some say both: the vision is built on a real situation, and it also becomes a mirror for every generation. These notes lean toward the third view. Reading it this way has one benefit: it does not let us treat this chapter as a story that belongs only to ancient times, and it does not turn it into a guessing game. Whichever view you take, one thing is shared: the prophet was brought to see so that he could tell others what he had seen. When we read this chapter, we are not going to look for dark rooms. We are letting God’s light shine on ourselves first.
@@3693@@
The first question: we are very used to “don’t air the family’s dirty laundry.” Family matters are handled behind closed doors, and church matters are the same, because we fear the shame of letting them out. This protective heart is sometimes good, but it can easily turn into a dark room: the more a matter is hidden, the more it molds. The elders in 8:12 are saying exactly this: “God doesn’t see.” You can begin like this: find someone you trust, and in one sentence say one thing you have never told anyone, with no details needed. If it is harm that others did to you, you need not force yourself; first find a pastor or a professional to walk with you. Saying it is not shameful. It lets the light in.
@@3694@@
The second question: we easily have a heart of “better safe than sorry.” Worship one more, burn one more stick of incense, buy one more insurance policy, just in case it works. In 8:11, what those elders held in their hands were censers that were originally for offering to Yahweh. It is not that they did not believe in Yahweh; they added a little something else besides Yahweh. That sentence is honest: what God wants is not “him too,” but him. You can check yourself this way: when something happens, is the first thing I think of to seek him, or “let me try another way first”? If it is the latter, there is no need to blame yourself. Honestly put that “a little extra” before the Lord.
@@3695@@
There are some things in my past that I have never dared to let anyone know. Must I tell all of it?
@@3696@@
Not necessarily. God wants you to be honest with him. That is the first step, and he already knows, so you need not fear his reaction. As for whether to tell people, it depends. If it is something you did that hurt others, you need to confess and apologize, and you can find a pastor or a trusted spiritual companion to walk with you step by step. If it is harm that others did to you, you owe no one an account, and no one may demand that you forgive. You can first protect yourself and find professional counseling or a pastor to accompany you. The point is not to make everything public, but to stop hiding it alone. You can start with one sentence: There is something I am not ready to say yet, but I would like you to pray for me.

@@3698@@

@@3705@@

I. Summoned: The Glory Rises to the Threshold (9:1–3)
@@4116@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. When I was little, my mother had a habit: before school started, she would sit in the living room under the lamp with needle and thread and sew a small strip of white cloth into the lining of each of my uniforms and coats, with my name written on it in black pen. I was embarrassed and said: Who is going to turn over your collar to look? She did not even look up. She said: If it is dropped or mixed up, you can be found again. Later, on a school outing, a big pile of coats lay beside the bus, and mine was handed back to me only after the teacher turned over the collar and read out the name. At that moment I understood: to have your name recorded is such a safe thing. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 9. This chapter has blades and killing, and it is hard to read. But before the blade, there is first a pen.
