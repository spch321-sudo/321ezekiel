<p><strong>The first eagle came, and its target was not a person but the top of a tree.</strong></p><p>It was full of feathers, brightly colored, an eagle that lacked nothing. The cedars of Lebanon are a symbol of nobility, and the beams of royal palaces come from them. The eagle did not cut down the tree. It broke off only the topmost, most tender twig, and carried it away.</p><p>Notice where it went: “a land of traffic,” “a city of merchants.” It did not carry the twig to the wilderness. It carried it to a place where everything has a price tag.</p><p>The picture in this verse is short, quick, and very quiet. No blood, no fire, only one tender twig leaving its tree. This is how exile begins: the first thing taken is often the one with the most future.</p><p>The word “carried” also deserves a pause. The eagle did not destroy the cedar. It only took away the most tender twig. The things that matter most are often not knocked down. They are quietly taken away.</p><p>Landing: some losses do not happen with a bang. One morning you find that the most precious twig has been broken off by someone else. The prophet wants you to see this picture first, because God’s word never skips over your pain.</p>

<p><strong>The riddle is solved, and the answer is a treaty.</strong></p><p>“Don’t you know what these things mean?” This sentence carries a little sigh. The people were not unable to understand; they did not want to understand. So God spells it out piece by piece: the first eagle is the king of Babylon, the tender twig that was broken off is the king and princes of Jerusalem, and another man was taken from the royal family, with whom a covenant was made and an oath sworn.</p><p>Look at how God describes this arrangement: the kingdom is “brought low,” unable to lift itself up, and “by keeping his covenant it might stand.” This is a small, inglorious position, but one that can survive. Not high, yet there is a way forward.</p><p>That is to say, the condition for survival is simple: keep the covenant. Not striving, not turning the tables, only holding on to what you said.</p><p>Zedekiah in history was exactly this kind of king, one who could “survive.” His position was not high, but God did not ask him to be high. He asked him to hold on to one thing.</p><p>Landing: we often complain that a low position is unfair. But sometimes that is precisely the way of life God leaves a person in the middle of judgment. What is truly dangerous is not being low, but refusing to be low and tampering with the covenant.</p>

<p><strong>The broken covenant suddenly had a new owner.</strong></p><p>For more than ten verses, the covenant belonged to “the king of Babylon”: the oath the king swore, the covenant the king made. God places a political breach of promise within the realm of devotion, because to Him the two have never been separate.</p><p>At the end it is still that line: “Then you will know that I, Yahweh, have spoken it.” God is not trying to win. He wants to be known. His judgment is the way His spoken word is heard.</p><p>If a person despises the One by whom he swore, what he despises is more than the other party. After these words, the listeners could no longer say, “This is only politics.”</p><p>Landing: every “I will” and “I promise” and “I swear before God” that you have said, God remembers. Not to catch you, but because He Himself is a covenant-keeping God.</p>

<p><strong>The same action, in a different hand.</strong></p><p>Look back at verse 4: the eagle “cropped off” the topmost of the cedar’s young twigs. <p><strong>Four opposites: all in the causative stem (Hiphil)</strong></p><p>הִשְׁפַּלְתִּי, הִגְבַּהְתִּי, הוֹבַשְׁתִּי, הִפְרַחְתִּי: all four are Hiphil, “I cause …” The subject every time is אֲנִי יְהוָה.</p><p>The last sentence, דִּבַּרְתִּי וְעָשִׂיתִי, has two perfects: “I have spoken, and I have done it.” The perfect here declares certainty, as if the seal had already been stamped.</p><p>There is no real grammatical dispute here.</p>
@@7163@@
<p><strong>The highs and lows of the world are not decided by eagles.</strong></p><p>The prophet closes with four pairs of opposites: high becomes low, low becomes high, green becomes dry, dry begins to flourish. All four verbs are “cause,” and the subject every time is “I, Yahweh.”</p><p>The two great eagles seemed to control everything: one carried off a king, the other promised a source of water. Only here do we find that from beginning to end they were only moving about on marks in God’s script. The real hand had been above the eagles all along.</p><p>The last sentence is especially weighty: “I, Yahweh, have spoken and have done it.” In Him, speaking and doing have no seam between them. Between a person’s words and actions there is often a crack, and that is exactly the sin of this chapter.</p><p>An eagle can break off, an eagle can plant, but an eagle cannot make a dry tree flower. Only One can do that. This is why the chapter ends not with a warning but with a Name lifted high.</p><p>Landing: if you have ever felt like that tree, broken off and left to dry, hear this sentence: “It is I who make the dry tree flourish.” Do not rush to say, “I am not worthy.” This is His work, not your report card.</p>

I. A Riddle, Two Great Eagles (17:1–6)
@@7434@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. I know a sister who always carries two power banks in her bag. Her phone is at full charge, and she still brings them. I asked her, “You have outlets at home and at the office. Why two?” She laughed and said, “I don’t know, I just don’t feel at ease.” Later I found that she is not the only one who does not feel at ease. In our pockets, our accounts, our contact lists, we all keep a few spare “power banks.” Today we read Ezekiel chapter 17. God gives a riddle, and in the riddle are two great eagles and one vine. Please don’t rush to guess the answer. First ask yourself one question: does this tree look like me?
@@7435@@
At the beginning of this chapter God says to the prophet: “Son of man, tell a riddle, and speak a parable to the house of Israel.” Notice that God did not tell him to write an indictment or to read out a verdict, but to tell a riddle. Why? Because the listeners had long been used to treating the prophet’s words as about someone else. When they heard judgment, they nodded and thought: that is right, those people really should hear this. A riddle is different. A riddle makes you think for yourself, and you have to put yourself into it to solve it. God is very patient. He would rather spend a little more time and wait for people to see themselves.
@@7436@@
The first scene of the riddle is a great eagle, with great wings, long feathers, full of feathers, brightly colored, which came to Lebanon. It did not chop down the cedar. It only flew up, broke off the very topmost, most tender twig, and carried it to a land of traffic and set it in a city of merchants. Look at this detail: what was taken away was not the whole tree but the one twig with the most future. To a tree, the tender twig is tomorrow. What the eagle took was tomorrow, and it put it into a city where everything has a price.
@@7437@@
Then the eagle took some of the seed of the land and planted it in fruitful soil, placing it beside many waters. This is very good treatment: good soil, many waters. I think of the potted plants displayed in a shop window, watered on time, given just the right sunlight, trimmed neatly, and customers who walk by all praise how pretty they are. But their roots are limited by a pot. The text says that this twig grew gradually and became a spreading vine of low stature. The cedar reaches toward the sky; the vine crawls along the ground. More important is the direction: its branches turned toward the eagle, and its roots were under the eagle. Whoever the roots are under, that tree belongs to him.
@@7438@@
Please stop for a moment and ask one question: did this vine die? No. It was alive, with branches and sprigs, and the word the text uses is “spreading.” From the outside it lacked nothing. This is the hardest part of this riddle: a person can look like he is doing very well in other people’s eyes while his roots are in someone else’s hands, and he himself may not even have noticed.
@@7439@@
Some of those who heard this riddle were already far away, and some were still in Jerusalem. Every day they were counting: what is the next step, and whom shall we rely on? This riddle was aimed right at those calculations.
@@7440@@
If I don’t understand this riddle, does it mean I am not spiritual enough?
@@7441@@
No. A riddle is meant to be thought over slowly. It is not an exam. When God told the prophet to “tell a riddle and speak a parable,” the listeners also had to think for a while, and even the explanation of the riddle does not come until after verse 11. So please be at ease: not understanding is not a sign of being unspiritual. It is the normal process of reading Scripture. This chapter’s riddle really needs only three pictures: an eagle breaks off a tender twig, a tree is planted in good soil, and a root bends toward a second eagle. You can set aside who the eagles are for now, and ask yourself: where have my own roots bent toward? At your next small group meeting, you do not need to hurry to give an answer. Just say: one picture I saw today is this. Being able to see one picture is already reading this riddle.

II. The Vine That Lacked No Water: Light from the Original (17:7–10)
@@7443@@
The second great eagle came, with great wings and many feathers. The text says: this vine bent its roots toward him, and shot out its branches toward him, from the beds of its plantation, that he might water it. But look at verse 8: it was planted in good soil by many waters, that it might produce branches, bear fruit, and be a good vine. It was already beside many waters. It was not thirsty. It bent over not because it had no water, but because it felt uneasy. This is very much like a student the night before an exam: the book is clearly finished, yet he wants to flip through one more reference book and sign up for one more cram class. It is not that it is not enough. He does not dare to believe it is enough.
@@7444@@
There are three words in the original in these verses that you can take home in thirty seconds. The first is “root,” the Hebrew שֹׁרֶשׁ (shoresh), which appears again and again in this chapter in forms like “its roots” and “his roots”: the roots were under the eagle (verse 6), bent its roots over (verse 7), pull up its roots (verse 9). The roots are the part of a tree that should move least. Branches can be pruned and leaves can fall, but when the roots move, the whole tree is no longer steady. The real question of this riddle is not whether the tree grows well, but where the roots are placed.
@@7445@@
The second is “bent over,” כָּפְנָה (kafnah), verse 7. This word appears only this once in the Old Testament. Some dictionaries connect it with “hunger, longing,” and the CUV translates it “bent over.” Put the two meanings together and the picture appears: the roots bent over not because they were dying of thirst, but because the eyes were hungry, and when they saw water somewhere else, the heart itched. The most common unease is not lack, but “seeing that others have.”
@@7446@@
The third is “east wind,” רוּחַ הַקָּדִים (ruach haqqadim), verse 10. The east wind is a hot wind blowing in from the wilderness, and in one night it can scorch tender leaves dry. What is interesting is that רוּחַ also means “wind,” “breath,” and “spirit.” In this chapter the wind is not an accident of weather. It is an instrument in God’s hand. The last sentence is: it will wither in the beds where it grew. Not in the wilderness, not before the enemy, but in the bed where it was planted and thought itself safest. Put the three words together: the root (shoresh) bent (kafnah), the east wind (ruach) blew, and it withered in the safest place.
@@7447@@
So the question was never “Is there water beside me?” but “Where are my roots facing?” When you get home tonight, you can ask yourself a small question: recently, in what matter did I clearly already have something in my hands, and yet go looking for another channel?
@@7448@@
Does going to a doctor, a counselor, or a friend for help also count as bending my roots toward somewhere else?
@@7449@@
No. What chapter 17 rebukes is turning to other backers after breaking a covenant and despising an oath. It is not all seeking of help. Seeing a doctor when you are sick, talking to a counselor or a pastor when you are sad, asking a friend when money is short: these are channels of water that God gave, not a second eagle. You can tell the difference with three questions. First, when I seek help, am I at the same time abandoning what I have said to God and to others? Second, does this help bring me closer to God or farther from Him? Third, am I seeking help in His hand, or leaving Him to seek a guarantee? If you are sick, depressed, or hurt, please do not let this chapter make you afraid to ask for help. Seeking professional help is often exactly God’s provision.
@@7450@@
III. Solving the Riddle: Despising the Oath and Breaking the Covenant (17:11–21)
@@7451@@
The riddle was told. What does God say? First He sighs: don’t you know what these things mean? They were not unable to understand; they did not want to understand. So God spells it out piece by piece. The first great eagle is the king of Babylon. He came to Jerusalem and took the king and the princes to Babylon, and he chose one man from the royal family and made a covenant with him and brought him under an oath. God specially explains this kingdom’s situation: it was brought low, unable to lift itself up, and could stand only by keeping the covenant. This kingdom had only one way to live, and that was to keep the covenant.
@@7452@@
What did this king do? He rebelled against the king of Babylon, sending ambassadors into Egypt that they might give him horses and many people. He wanted horses, he wanted an army, he wanted to live without relying on this covenant. The second great eagle is Egypt. God asks again and again: will he prosper? Will he escape? This is not a comment on the international situation of the time. It asks one man: if you break your own oath, can you still have peace? Pharaoh, with his mighty army and great company, would not help him. Many people and great power are not the same as security.
@@7453@@
The text says it again and again, like someone tapping a finger on the table: he despised the oath and broke the covenant. “Despise,” the Hebrew בָּזָה (bazah), is to treat something with weight as if it had no weight. “Break,” הֵפֵר (hefer), is to snap what was joined together. The oath and the covenant were treated as wrapping paper that could be thrown away at will.
@@7454@@
The most crucial turn is in verse 19. This oath was originally sworn by a small nation to a great one, and by ordinary reckoning it was a matter between one person and another. But God says: he has despised the oath sworn by me, and broken the covenant made by me. My oath, my covenant. Because that oath was sworn by God’s name. This sentence is speaking to us today as well: what have we said by God’s name? The vows in a marriage, the promises made to God, the “I will” said to brothers and sisters, the service we agreed to take up. God heard. He does not come to catch our mistakes. He wants us to know that the words we speak carry weight in His eyes.
@@7455@@
Some will ask: if the oath was sworn under pressure, does it still count? This is a real question, and there are different views in the church. Some say that since the oath was sworn by God, it must be kept. Some say God rules over the nations and used Babylon to discipline Judah, so breaking the covenant was resisting what God was doing. And some say that whether an oath sworn under pressure is valid deserves further thought. The position of these notes is this: verse 19 puts the weight on the oath “sworn by God.” It does not endorse any oppression, and it does not tell anyone to stay in a covenant that hurts them. If you have been bound by a vow or a promise, or deeply hurt by someone who broke a covenant, please first seek safety and talk to someone you can trust. This chapter speaks of those who despise God’s name. It does not tell the wounded to endure more wounds.
@@7456@@
The swords and snares that follow are very heavy words. We should read them honestly, and not make them bigger than they are. What they mean is: once you leave the covenant, no backer can save you. The effect of this chapter should be quietness, a greater willingness to return to the Lord before whom we swore and made covenant, not panic. God’s purpose in all this is stated clearly in verse 21: then you will know that I, Yahweh, have spoken it. What He wants is for people to know Him.
@@7457@@
I was hurt by someone who broke a covenant, or I was deeply wounded in a relationship. Is chapter 17 telling me to keep enduring?
@@7458@@
No. Please hear this clearly first. What chapter 17 rebukes is a man who despised an oath sworn by God and broke the covenant to look for other backers. It does not ask the wounded to stay in an unsafe situation and endure more. If your safety is under threat right now, please first find someone you can trust, a pastor, or professional help, and protect yourself. This is wisdom, not unbelief. God is the God who watches over the wounded, and He does not despise your tears. You do not need to hurry to forgive, and you do not need to hurry to say, “I have let it go.” You can take it slowly and hand over one thing today: Lord, I cannot yet speak of this wound; please be with me first.
@@7459@@
IV. “I Will”: The Tender Twig God Planted Himself (17:22–24)
@@7460@@
If the story stopped here, no one could stand. But God’s word was not finished. Verse 22: I will also take some of the lofty top of the cedar and plant it. I will crop off from the topmost of its young twigs a tender one, and I will plant it on a high and lofty mountain. Did you hear it? This sentence is almost exactly like the first eagle: the same tree, the same action, take, crop off, the topmost, a tender one. Only one thing is different: this time the one acting is God Himself. The original says “I” twice (אָנִי): I will take, I will plant. In the place where human schemes have all been used up, God says: all right, it is my turn.
@@7461@@
The first time, the eagle planted the twig in a low-lying field, and it grew into a vine along the ground. This time God plants the twig on a high mountain, and it produces boughs, bears fruit, and becomes a good cedar, and birds of every kind will dwell in its shade, in the shade of its branches. I think of a summer afternoon under an old tree: some people are cooling off, some are playing chess, and a child has fallen asleep leaning against the roots. That is a tree that no longer begs others for water. It has become the shade for others. The direction of grace is not more and more in need, but more and more able to give.
@@7462@@
Who is this tender twig? There are several readings in the church: some say it is a king of David’s house in the future; some say it points to the Messiah, Jesus Christ; and some say it is the remnant renewed by God. These notes do not settle it, do not decide for you, and do not use this topic to judge those who hold a different view. All three readings affirm the same thing: this tree was planted by God’s own hand, and God Himself made it grow.
@@7463@@
The last verse is the close of the whole chapter: all the trees of the field will know that I, Yahweh, have brought down the high tree, have exalted the low tree, have dried up the green tree, and have made the dry tree flourish. I, Yahweh, have spoken and have done it. Four actions, and the subject is God every time. We thought the two eagles were deciding everything, one taking away a king and one promising a source of water. Only here do we see that from beginning to end they were only flying through God’s hand. This is not predicting who will fail and who will flourish. It is saying: the One who plants the tree is someone else.
@@7464@@
I am like a tree planted in a place I did not choose, and I often feel pushed around by people and circumstances. Does God still watch over me?
@@7465@@
He does. The answer this chapter gives you is not “try harder,” but “the One who plants the tree is someone else.” The vine was planted in good soil, and also planted in a place it did not choose itself. That is a real situation. But the text tells us at the end that eagles, wind, and the great powers of the world are only flying through God’s hand. You can slowly practice praying like this: Lord, I have been put in this place and I had no choice; please keep my roots in Your hand, whether this is good soil or a hard place. If this helpless feeling of being pushed around has gone on for a long time and is affecting your sleep, appetite, or mood, please talk to a pastor or a trusted companion, and if needed, ask a professional to walk with you for a while.
@@7466@@
The first deviation is directly matching the two great eagles to some modern country or power. The text speaks of Babylon and Egypt at that time. The moment we name a modern country, we are either endorsing someone or condemning someone. We want the congregation to see more clearly that God rules over all things, not to become more afraid or more eager to take sides.
@@7467@@
The second deviation is preaching the “tender twig” as having only one answer, and criticizing those who hold a different view. A king of David’s house, the Messiah, a renewed community: there are people in the church who hold each of these. These notes do not settle it, and they leave room for other readings. We want the congregation to see together that God planted it with His own hands, not to compete over who has the right answer.
@@7468@@
The third deviation is preaching the covenant and the oath as meaning that a person must keep any vow to the death, including in situations that hurt people. Chapter 17 rebukes despising an oath sworn by God. It does not tell people to stay in an unsafe relationship. We want the wounded to be protected, not to be told to endure more.
@@7469@@
The fourth deviation is preaching the swords and snares of verses 16 to 21 as if God delights in punishment and exists to frighten the congregation. That is the consequence of leaving the covenant, not God’s hobby. We want the congregation to return to the Lord more quietly, not to fear the world more.
@@7470@@
The fifth deviation is preaching the vine as meaning “never look outside for help,” and opposing seeing a doctor, a counselor, or getting help. The mistake in this chapter is relying on others after breaking a covenant, not proper seeking of help. We want the congregation to dare to seek the help they should have, not to become lonelier because of this.
@@7471@@
God says “I will.” Does this mean I need to do nothing and just wait?
@@7472@@
No. God says “I will” at the point where human schemes are used up and human methods do not work. It is not saying that people may be lazy. Look at the “I will” in verse 22: before it comes a long story, a king who broke the covenant, a tree that bent its roots, a people who tried every means. “I will” is what He says to the one who has come to the end. So before we reach the end, we are still responsible: to carry out what we have said, not to bend one more root, and to be honest before God. And when there is nothing we can do, then we quietly say: Lord, it is Your turn. These two things do not contradict each other: when we have done the part we should do, we can entrust the rest in peace.
@@7473@@
The first question: we are very used to “keeping a way out.” When we agree to something, we say only part of it. “I’ll try,” “Let me see,” “We’ll see when the time comes” are phrases we know well. This is a kind of wisdom for protecting ourselves, and it often does keep people from getting hurt. But chapter 17 reminds us that some words are spoken by God’s name. Marriage vows, the confession at baptism, promises made to God, saying to someone “I will be with you”: these words cannot be “we’ll see.” You can do one small thing: check one thing you promised someone and have not yet done, and take one small step this week; or say honestly: I can’t do this one, I’m sorry. An honest withdrawal is better than a silent breaking of a promise. If the promise itself would hurt you or others, please first talk it over with a pastor or someone you trust, and do not hold on alone.
@@7474@@
The second question: we are very used to “relying on connections,” and count “who we know” as more real than “whether we have prayed.” When looking for a job, we first ask whether anyone has connections. When a child starts school, we first ask whom we know. When the church wants to hold an event, we first think of which well-known person to invite to lend support. Relationships between people are not wrong in themselves; they are also channels God gave. The problem is the order: when I find people first and only then remember God, my roots have already quietly bent. You can check yourself this way: for this decision I need to make, what did I first say to God? Before you go looking for people, spend ten minutes putting this matter before God, and write down the date and the names. Then go and find people.
@@7475@@
I promised someone something, and now I find I cannot do it. What should I do?
@@7476@@
First tell two things apart. First, was this promise made “by God’s name,” or was it an ordinary agreement? Marriage, baptism, and vows made to God carry the most weight; an ordinary agreement should also be treated honestly. Second, why can’t you do it? Is it because I am lazy and no longer want to, or because the situation has truly changed, or because continuing would even hurt someone? If it is the former, please go to the other person and say: I promised you before, and I haven’t done it. I am very sorry, and I would like to make it up in this way. If it is the latter, please do not decide alone. First talk to a pastor or a trusted elder, and let others help you see clearly. Either way, do not use silence in place of an explanation. Saying it honestly is the first step of not despising an oath, and also the first movement of putting the roots back into His hand.
@@7477@@
VII. Living Out 321: Put the Roots Back into the Hand of the One Who Plants the Tree
@@7478@@
Jesus is my Role Model. However you read that tender twig, Jesus is the One who never bent His roots toward anywhere else. When He was hungry in the wilderness, He faced the most real kind of unease: no bread, no guarantee, no one to back Him up. He said: man shall not live by bread alone. His roots were always in the Father’s hand. Those who follow Him learn His pattern: they do not move their roots away just to have one more backer.
@@7479@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 17 not to find out who the eagles are, but to let God’s word examine me: whose hand are my roots under right now? Do the words I have spoken carry weight before God? Every time I want to find another channel, I will first open this chapter and ask myself one sentence: will it prosper?
@@7480@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. When we feel uneasy, the Holy Spirit does not urge me to find one more safeguard. He brings me back to the water and makes me quiet. Every night you can ask yourself: today, whose hand were my roots in? Then wait a little, and let Him shine His light.
@@7481@@
Let Jesus be King. Whoever the roots are under is king. Every time I want to find another backer, I am really asking: who is my king? A person who lets Jesus be King no longer calculates who the next eagle will be. He is quiet, and waits for the One who plants the tree.
@@7482@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The tender twig grew into a cedar, and the credit is not the tree’s but the hand of the One who planted it. If we can stand, if we have a little shade to give to others, we should not say, “I finally did it on my own.” All the glory goes to the Lord who planted with His own hands.
@@7483@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is planted by Him, not relying on human schemes and not relying on the help of a great nation. Its fruit is to become a shade where birds dwell, a place of rest for people. A God-centered community is a group of people who remind one another “put the roots back into His hand,” and in such a community no one needs to find one more backer for himself.
@@7484@@
Does “let Jesus be King” mean I don’t need to make plans, save money, or think about a way out?
@@7485@@
No. The wise people in the Bible also prepare, store up food, and plan. These are not the second eagle. The difference is whether the plan is made “in His hand” or “away from Him.” A person who has made Jesus King can still save money, buy insurance, and plan next year, but while doing these things he has one sentence in his heart: this is a channel He gave me, not my backer. If the plan fails, he can still be quiet, because his roots are not in the plan. You can practice it this way: before every big arrangement, say to God: this is my plan, please change it; if You do not agree, I am willing to let it go. This is selflessness: not having no plans, but having no hand that I grab hold of for myself.
@@7486@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, the Same Bed
@@7487@@
The proud type says: “I can arrange things myself. I don’t need to rely on anyone, and I don’t need anyone to tell me.” The text says to him: will this vine prosper? Pride is leaving a person’s own position and grabbing God’s power, wanting to take God’s place and arrange every channel oneself. To return to one’s position is to admit: the water is from Him, and it is enough. You can start by saying one sentence to God: “Lord, please change my arrangements.”
@@7488@@
The fearful type says: “If I don’t leave a few more ways out, what if God doesn’t take care of me?” This tree bent its roots in exactly this “what if.” God says: I will, I myself will plant. The fearful type needs to learn to replace “what if” with “He has already said, ‘I will.’” You can begin with one smallest thing: this week, prepare one fewer backup plan, and hand it over to God.
@@7489@@
The shame type says: “I broke covenants before, and I said words that did not count. I am already the dry tree.” The text does not stop at withering in the end. God says: I will plant a tender twig on a high and lofty mountain. Shame says, “You have nothing left.” The gospel says, “I will plant again.” You do not need to make yourself good first. Today, just put your roots back into His hand.
@@7490@@
The wounded type says: “I was betrayed, abandoned. How can you ask me to believe in oaths again, to believe in God again?” God does not blame you for being wounded. He Himself is the One whose oath was despised. You can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story. The companion should not hurry to say “you must forgive,” but only say: “I am here, I am with you.”
