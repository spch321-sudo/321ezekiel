<p><strong>① How should “Yahweh Is There” be read?</strong></p><ul><li>The name is יְהוָה שָׁמָּה: “Yahweh is there”</li><li>The ending ָה on שָׁמָּה usually marks direction, “toward there,” but here it is read as “there”</li><li>It echoes 1:1, where the glory appeared by the River Chebar; the glory has returned, and the place of God never leaves again (43:7)</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: the name of this city is not the name of a place; it is a sentence: Yahweh is there.</strong></p></blockquote>
@@21817@@
<p><strong>What this book finally says is not how big the city is, but that he is there.</strong></p><p>All the measurements before this, all the gates, all the lands, all the proportions, finally come down to one sentence: “from that day the name of the city shall be, ‘Yahweh is there.’”</p><p>This is the end point of the whole book. At the beginning, Ezekiel was by the River Chebar and saw the glory; in the middle, the glory left the temple and went east; in chapter 43, the glory came back. Now the last sentence of the book does not say the glory comes back, but that he will not go away again.</p><p>In the original it is two words: יְהוָה שָׁמָּה, “Yahweh is there.” The name of the city is not that of a person, a king, or a tribe, nor of any achievement, but of a presence: he is there.</p><p>This is the sentence the exiles most needed to hear. They had lost the temple, lost the city, lost the land. If one name could answer all their questions, it is this one: he is there.</p><p>At the end of the Bible there is also a city. We do not need to sort out which comes first and which comes after; we only need to see the likeness between the two pictures: what people finally want is not a city, but the One in the city.</p><p>Landing it: if your life right now is called “loss,” “waiting,” or “not yet,” you can give it another name, not one you made up but one he gave: Yahweh is there. Your name can be like this too.</p>

I. The Roll Call: The Seven Northern Tribes (48:1–8)
@@22200@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. On the first day of school, the teacher stands in front of the classroom holding a list and calls the names one by one. When your name is read, you raise your hand and say, “Here.” It is a very small word, but you know that from this moment there is a place for you in this classroom. If the names go by and yours is never called, you keep sitting there, anxious inside. Today we read the last chapter of Ezekiel, and the last lesson of the whole book. In this chapter the prophet hears God calling the roll.
@@22201@@
First let us set the background. Chapters 40 to 48 are the prophet’s vision of the new temple and the new land. How to read this large section, the church has different views: some read it literally, some symbolically, some spiritually. These notes set the views side by side, do not set a timetable, do not presume when the temple would be built or when sacrifices would be offered, and do not judge brothers and sisters who see it differently. We only ask: what does the prophet want us to see? In this chapter, he wants us to see a list of names, and one name.
@@22202@@
Verse 1 says: these are the names of the tribes. Please notice, “by name.” From the far north, from the way of Hethlon to the entrance of Hamath, that is Dan’s portion; next to Dan, from east to west, is Asher’s portion; then Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, Reuben, and finally Judah. Seven names, one after another, each with its own portion. Every portion runs “from the east side to the west side,” strip after strip, from the border on this side to the border on that side. Not one tribe is left outside the list.
@@22203@@
Think about how the exiles felt when they heard this list. They had lost the temple, lost the city, and lost the land; some of the tribes’ names had long since stopped being mentioned by anyone in this world. Now, one by one, God read them out: Dan, Asher, Naphtali. God had not forgotten. The order on this list is not a ranking, not who is more honored or who is more spiritual; it is a position on a map, not a place on a report card. The point is: every name has a portion.
@@22204@@
I always feel that I have “no share,” like a bystander in the church. What can this chapter say to me?
@@22205@@
First, accept that this feeling is real, and do not rush to say “you should be content.” The exiles had felt this too. Second, please notice what the text does: God “calls the roll”; he does not wait for people to come and fight for a place. The order of dividing the land is not by who is the most gifted, but by God, according to what he swore, dividing it one by one. Third, you can do one very small thing: write your name on a piece of paper and say to God, “Lord, you remember me.” If your sense of loss comes from bereavement, from leaving, or from being pushed out, please first find someone who can walk with you, and do not hold it up alone. Do not hurry to be comforted either. Saying it out loud is the first step from standing at the edge to walking onto the list.
@@22206@@
II. In the Middle, His Portion (48:9–22)
@@22207@@
Verse 8 goes on to say that next to Judah shall be the offering which you shall offer. From here, a special piece of land appears in the center of the map. Verses 9 and 10: the offering you offer to Yahweh shall be twenty-five thousand in length and ten thousand in width. This holy offering shall be for the priests. The sanctuary of Yahweh shall be in the middle of it. Please notice: this land is not at the edge, not in a corner, not in the far north or far south, but in the middle of the whole map. Of the twelve tribes, seven are in the north and five in the south, and in the middle is his portion.
@@22208@@
This central land has three things. First is the priests’ land, with the sanctuary in it; second is the Levites’ land, equal in size to the priests’ land; third is the city, in the middle. Verse 11 says this land is for the sons of Zadok, the sanctified priests, those who kept my instruction. When the children of Israel went astray, they did not go astray as the Levites went astray. Please notice the point of this sentence: it is “kept my instruction,” not “some family has it by birth.” A place near God is not by family background, but by faithfulness, and also by grace.
@@22209@@
The words of verse 14 are weighty: they shall sell none of it, nor exchange it, nor shall the first fruits of the land be alienated, for it is holy to Yahweh. This land is not something people can trade or transfer, and not something people can use to prove themselves. This is not asking people for money, and not telling everyone to give up all their savings; it is saying that the center cannot be sold. We may swap many things in our lives back and forth, but there is one center that cannot be swapped.
@@22210@@
Then verse 15 says: the five thousand left over in the width, in front of the twenty-five thousand, shall be for common use, for the city, for dwelling and for pasture lands. The city shall be in the middle of it. Please notice the two words “common use.” It is not holy land, it is common use: where people live, where they farm, where they build houses, where they get on with life. Yet this city where people get on with life lies right beside the holy land. Verse 19 says: those who labor in the city, out of all the tribes of Israel, shall cultivate it. The workers are farming, and the field is part of the offering. Daily life has not been put outside the sanctuary.
@@22211@@
Verse 21 has one more detail: on both sides of the offering, the remainder shall be for the prince. The prince is at the sides, and the holy place is in the middle. This is a very quiet picture: the center of the map is not the prince, but the presence of God. It reminds a person that however much authority he has, the center is not his place. This is exactly what we keep saying in this study: let man be man, and let God be God.
@@22212@@
Is “not sold, not exchanged” asking believers to give all their property to the church?
@@22213@@
No. What verse 14 speaks of is that one piece of offering land that is holy to Yahweh, which may not be sold, exchanged, or handed over to others; it speaks of the center not being transferable, and does not say that everyone must give up all their property. If someone uses this verse to ask people for money, or to make poor people feel they have not given enough, that is a misuse. A better question to ask is: what is there in my life that I once handed to God and later quietly took back? It may be a period of time, a talent, or a decision. You can honestly say to the Lord: this one thing, I once handed to you and later took back; please help me put it back in your hands.
@@22214@@
III. The Five Southern Tribes (48:23–29)
@@22215@@
Verse 23 says: as for the rest of the tribes, from east to west, Benjamin, one portion. Next come Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, and Gad, one after another, each again from east to west, each next to the one before, none missed. The list began in the north, and when it reaches the south, it is still a list. God does not remember only one end; he remembers the other end too.
@@22216@@
Verse 28 says the south border of Gad runs from Tamar to the waters of Meribath Kadesh, to the brook, to the great sea. Verse 29 puts a seal on this whole allotment: this is the land which you shall divide by lot to the tribes of Israel for inheritance, and these are their several portions, says the Lord Yahweh. Casting lots means that it was not won by people’s own striving, but divided by God. Every name on the list is in his hand, not in people’s hands. This map is a promise spoken to the exiles; it is not any map of today, and we do not use it to endorse any claim to borders.
@@22217@@
Does “every tribe has a portion” guarantee that I will get the position, wealth, or success I want?
@@22218@@
No. The “has a portion” of this chapter means that God remembers; it does not mean that God guarantees everyone success. Chapter 48 does not say that everyone will get what they want, and does not say that life will have no hardship; what it says is: your name is on the list, and God has not forgotten you. If this verse is preached as a prosperity theology, it is misused. We can understand it this way: your position in this world may not be one you chose, and may not be what you wanted, but in his eyes this position is not an accident. You can say to the Lord: this position, which I did not choose, I hand to you; please be with me here.
@@22219@@
IV. Light from the Original: Name, Gate, There (48:30–35)
@@22220@@
In verses 30 to 34, the text turns to the city. The city is square, four thousand five hundred cubits on each side, three gates on each side, twelve gates in all. Verse 31 says: the gates of the city shall be named after the tribes of Israel. The north side has three gates: the gate of Reuben, the gate of Judah, the gate of Levi; the east side has three gates: the gate of Joseph, the gate of Benjamin, the gate of Dan; the south side has three gates: the gate of Simeon, the gate of Issachar, the gate of Zebulun; the west side has three gates: the gate of Gad, the gate of Asher, the gate of Naphtali.
@@22221@@
Please notice two things. First, Levi has a gate. We remember that the Levites did not receive a piece of land like the other tribes; their share is in the offering, before God. But on the city wall, Levi’s name is also carved on a gate. Those without land also have a gate. Second, twelve names stand on the wall of the same city: some who had much rivalry, some who had been sold to another place, some who had gone astray, some who had no land. These names are not in comparison; they are together. They are not given labels; they stand in a circle, surrounding the city in the middle.
@@22222@@
There are three original words in this chapter worth a closer look. The first is “name,” in Hebrew שֵׁם, and verse 35 says וְשֵׁם הָעִיר, the name of the city. When we read the Bible, we often overlook one thing: in Hebrew, a name is not only what someone is called, but the essence of that person, that place, that being. At the very end of the whole book, God gives this city not a measurement or a title, but a “name.” What the city is depends on what he calls it.
@@22223@@
The second is “gate,” in Hebrew שַׁעַר, and verse 33 says שְׁעָרִים שְׁלֹשָׁה, three gates. A gate is a place for going in and out, a place where people come home. In ancient times, the city gate was also where people sat down, spoke, and judged cases. Twelve gates, and every gate has a name. The meaning of this picture is: those who enter the city come in through a gate with a name, not through a hole that nobody knows. When you come in, someone knows whose family you belong to.
@@22224@@
The third is “there,” in Hebrew שָׁמָּה, the last two words of verse 35, יְהוָה שָׁמָּה, Yahweh is there. This is the last sentence of the whole book. This word is very small, just “there”; but please think about it: in the first half of Ezekiel, the glory leaves (chapters 10 and 11), God’s presence rises up from the temple, and the exiles’ greatest fear in their hearts was: God has gone. The whole book went around in a circle, and the very last word is this “there.” God has come back, and he has settled down.
@@22225@@
The last sentence of the text says: “from that day the name of the city shall be, ‘Yahweh is there.’” The name of this city is not Israel, not David, not the achievement of any person; it is he himself. The exiles had lost the temple, lost the city, lost the land; hearing this sentence, perhaps they asked only one question: in my loss, where is God? The answer is: he is here; from that day on, his name is the name of this city.
@@22226@@
But we must be honest: “Yahweh is there” is not a magic spell, and not a slogan. If someone is in sickness, in bereavement, in a long wait, and you say to him, “You must believe that God is right here,” but you do not sit down with him, and do not allow him to weep, that sentence becomes light. The Bible is a whole book; it first lets people see the pain of the glory leaving, and only then lets them hear “there.” So the effect of this promise is not to make people cheerful, but to make people patient, and to make them love him more.
@@22227@@
It is very hard for me to feel God beside me right now. What does this chapter mean for me?
@@22228@@
First, please do not rush to tell yourself “I should feel something.” The exiles also went a long time without feeling him. This chapter does not tell you to feel first and then believe; it says that God’s name is “there,” that this is his name, not your feeling. You can make it an honest prayer: Lord, I cannot feel you right now, but your name is “there,” and I say this name aloud to myself. If you are in bereavement, in pain, or in a long stretch of weakness, please find someone who can be with you; you do not have to say much. And please do not let yourself be pushed to “see it right away.” Sometimes the first shape of presence is simply someone sitting down, saying nothing.
@@22229@@
Why does the whole book end with a name, and not with a victory or a plan?
@@22230@@
Because the question of this book from beginning to end is “where is God?” The glory leaving was the exiles’ deepest pain; the glory returning is their greatest hope. If the ending were a victory, a system, or a plan, the heart would still ask: will he go away again? But a name is different: a name is a relationship, a promise, himself. The end point of the whole book is not what we have gained, but that we are with him. This also reminds us that our own lives should not end with achievement, but end with presence. What is your goal today? Is it achievement, or presence?
@@22231@@
The first deviation is taking the borders and allotments of chapter 48 and using them to endorse any claim to land, border, or conflict today, or to identify some nation or people. This was a promise spoken to the exiles, not a modern map. We want to make the congregation love the Lord more and be more patient, not more eager to fight.
@@22232@@
The second deviation is preaching chapters 40 to 48 as prophecy that can be fitted into a timetable, pointing out when the temple and the city will appear, or directly equating this city with the New Jerusalem of Revelation and using that to set an order of events. The two places have images that echo each other, but they are not a chronology. We want to make the congregation hope calmly, not count the days in panic.
@@22233@@
The third deviation is preaching the order of the tribes’ positions, the position of the sons of Zadok, or the names of the twelve gates as who is more honored and who is more spiritual, or using them to compare gifts and offices in the church, even endorsing any class or putting labels on any tribe. The text speaks of “kept my instruction,” of faithfulness and grace, not family background. We want the church to have less sense of rank, not more comparison.
@@22234@@
The fourth deviation is preaching “every tribe has a portion” as a guarantee that everyone will get the position, wealth, or success they want; or preaching “not sold, not exchanged” as a demand that believers give all their property, used to pressure people. This is God’s remembering, not a guarantee of prosperity, and not pressure to give. We want poor people not to feel they have not given enough, and we want people not to feel used.
@@22235@@
The fifth deviation is preaching “Yahweh Is There” in 48:35 as an easy slogan, or saying to someone in hardship, sickness, bereavement, or exclusion, “You must believe that God is right here,” without sitting down with them; or using “Levi has a gate” to urge those without a role to be content, and not letting them express their loss. We want to listen first, be present first, see first, and then comfort, and not push people to be strong.
@@22236@@
If someone says, “Just say ‘Yahweh is there,’ and all your problems will disappear,” how should I respond?
@@22237@@
You can gently say: this sentence is true, but it is not a magic spell. The whole book asks us to first see the pain of the glory leaving, and then hear him coming back. God’s presence does not guarantee that there will be no suffering; it guarantees that we are not alone in suffering. If someone uses this sentence to stop another’s tears, we should gently say: let him weep for a while first. You can apply this principle to yourself too: when you have no strength to say this sentence, you do not need to force yourself. Let others say it for you, let others sit beside you. Presence is sometimes not a sentence, but a person.
@@22238@@
First question: we are used to using “position” to prove ourselves. In Chinese society, titles, job ranks, and seating order are often a person’s business card. It is the same in the church: who is an elder, who is a deacon, who stands on the platform, who sits in the back row. So some people keep fighting for a position, and some feel that without a position they have no worth. Chapter 48 gives us another picture: every tribe has a portion, every gate has a name; the center is not anyone’s position, but God’s presence. You can begin like this: this week, at a gathering, ask yourself one sentence: am I using position to prove myself? If there were a chance to give my seat to someone else, would I be willing?
@@22239@@
Second question: we are used to putting God in the “holy” place and our days in the “common” place. Sunday at church is holy; Monday to Saturday in the office, the kitchen, the school, the factory is common. But verse 15 says the land “for common use” lies right beside the holy land, and the city is in the middle. Daily life has not been put outside the sanctuary. You can practice like this: this week, in one ordinary thing (before work, while cooking, on the way to pick up the children), stop for three seconds and say in your heart: Yahweh is here. This is his city too.
@@22240@@
What does “let Jesus be King” have to do with “giving the center back to him”?
@@22241@@
The connection is very direct. In the map of chapter 48, the center is the offering, his portion; the prince is at the sides, not in the center. To let Jesus be King is to admit that the center is his, not mine. We often say with our mouths that Jesus is King, but when the days are lived, the one sitting in the center is still ourselves: my plans, my face, my position. Selflessness is not having nothing; it is giving the center back to him, so that my name on his city wall is enough. Every morning you can say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to sit in the center of today; what I am most afraid of having no place for today; and today I give the center back to you.
@@22242@@
VII. Living Out 321: Give the Center Back to Him
@@22243@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He said: “He calls his own sheep by name, and leads them out.” (John 10:3) He is not shouting from far away at a flock; he calls them one by one, by name. In chapter 48 the gates have names and the land has portions, and he is the same: he calls us one by one. He is the shepherd who both searches for us himself and remembers us himself. Those who follow him also learn to remember other people’s names.
@@22244@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 48 not to solve a map, but to hear the last sentence: “Yahweh Is There.” The whole book of Ezekiel goes from the glory leaving to the glory returning, to the water flowing out from beneath the temple, and finally to his settling down. The Bible is my Standard means: I let the direction of the whole book become my direction, that is, returning to his presence.
@@22245@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. He promised us: “Behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age.” (Matthew 28:20) It is the Holy Spirit who makes us remember, in the everyday city, that he is here; it is also the Holy Spirit who stays with us when we cannot feel him. We do not need to live by feelings; we live by his name.
@@22246@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is giving the center back to him. I do not sit in the center, and I do not use a position to prove myself. In 48:8–10, the center is the offering, his portion; to let Jesus be King is to be willing to give the most central position to him. Let man be man, and let God be God.
@@22247@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The name of this city is not the achievement of any person; it is “Yahweh Is There.” If we have a little fruit, we should not carve our names on our own walls; if we are remembered, we should not say “I am important,” but say: he remembers me. All the glory goes to the Lord who calls names.
@@22248@@
Build God’s system. A community that belongs to God is a city with names: everyone has a portion, everyone has a gate. First life, being remembered; then relationships, in the same city together; then ministry, going in and out through the gates. Its name is not the name of any one person, but “Yahweh Is There.”
@@22249@@
The proud type says: my position was earned by my own effort, and I should sit in the center. The text says to him: the center is the offering, his portion, and the prince is at the sides. Pride is leaving one’s position as a human being, grabbing God’s authority, and wanting to take God’s place. The proud type should learn to give the center back to him. You can begin by saying to the Lord: Lord, the center is yours.
@@22250@@
The fearful type says: what if I am forgotten, and left off the list? The text says to him: the land of the tribes, according to their names, one by one, none missed. The fearful type does not need to first be sure whether he deserves it, but only to hear this “by name”: he remembers you. You can begin with one small thing, and say to the Lord: Lord, I hand you this unease.
@@22251@@
The ashamed type says: I once went astray and did wrong, and I have no right to stand on the city wall. The text says to him: twelve names stand on the wall of the same city, and not one has been taken away. Shame says, “You do not deserve a gate,” and the gospel says, “Your name is here.” The ashamed type does not need to make himself good first, but only to draw near that gate today.
@@22252@@
The wounded type says: I have given so much for God, yet I feel like the forgotten one. God does not rebuke your sense of loss; the Levites did not receive land like the others, but they had a gate. The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story; those who accompany should not hurry to say “you should be content,” but only say: I am here, I am with you.
@@22253@@
First, every night before bed, spend one minute saying to the Lord, “Yahweh is here.” Do not ask for a feeling; simply acknowledge that he is here. And write down the date.
@@22254@@
Second, write down one position you did not choose (work, family, church), and beside it write: “This is where he put me.” You do not need to hurry to leave, and you do not need to blame yourself.
@@22255@@
Third, find someone you feel has been forgotten or left outside, and send one message: “I thought of you and prayed for you.” Only a greeting, no advice; say his name and the date.
@@22256@@
Fourth, pick one everyday thing (going to work, cooking, the school run) and say in your heart: “This is your city too.” Put the everyday beside the sanctuary, and say which thing it is.
@@22257@@
Fifth, think of one thing you once handed to God and then quietly took back, and this week put it back in his hands; write down which day, and ask a companion to remind you.
@@22258@@
I declare: Yahweh remembers my name; I am on his list; I have a portion.
@@22259@@
I declare: the center is his; I will no longer use position to prove myself; let Jesus be King.
@@22260@@
I declare: it is enough that my name is on his city wall; I also remember others’ names and am with them in the same city.
@@22261@@
I declare: my days are his city; Yahweh is here; all the glory goes to the Lord who calls names.
@@22262@@
The simplest thing is to take a sheet of paper today and write down your name, and beside it write: Yahweh remembers this name. Then write the name of someone you feel has been forgotten, and pray for him for one minute. Put this paper where you will pass every day. Each time you see it, say two names: his, and yours. This action is not asking you to get better right away, nor asking you to change anything; it is only saying to yourself: we are all on the list. At the next small group meeting, you only need to say: this week I said one person’s name. You do not need to say anything more.
@@22263@@
Chapter 48 begins with a list and ends with a name. It begins with the twelve tribes, from north to south, each with a portion; in the middle is his portion and the everyday city; at the end are twelve gates, twelve names, and the name of the city: Yahweh Is There. From a list to a city, from a city to a name: this is the last journey of the whole book of Ezekiel.
@@22264@@
Looking back over the whole book: the glory left, and came back; for his own name, he made people know that he is Yahweh; he himself became the shepherd, gave a new heart and a new spirit, and made dry bones live; water flowed out from beneath the temple, and even the deadest places came alive. In the end he settled down, and the name of this city is himself. That God came to us in Jesus; he calls his own sheep by name, and he also promised to be with us always.

So please remember the three main points of this lesson: every tribe has a portion, every gate has a name, and the name of this city is Yahweh Is There. 1:1 says “the thirtieth year, the fourth month, the fifth day,” but does not explain from when it is counted; 1:2 then gives the date, “the fifth year of King Jehoiachin’s captivity.” The dates of the book are broadly in order, but there are places out of order (for example, 29:17 is the twenty-seventh year). 1:3 also says he was the priest, the son of Buzi, who received visions of God in the land of the exiles by the River Chebar.
@@22285@@
The prophet was thirty years old
@@22286@@
Some think “thirtieth year” refers to Ezekiel being thirty years old, exactly the age when priests began their service (Num 4:3); he should have been serving in the temple, but was called in a foreign land.
@@22287@@
The thirtieth year of some era
@@22288@@
Some think it is counted from some calendar or event (such as after Josiah’s reform), a way of recording the year, not an age.
@@22289@@
The two do not conflict
@@22290@@
Others think the verse deliberately leaves a blank: whichever it is, it says that God comes to his servant at the most unexpected time and in the most unexpected place.
@@22291@@
These notes read according to the CUV and its order, marking the date of each chapter; for “the thirtieth year” the views are set side by side, and none is made a condition of faith. Whatever the prophet’s age, the point is 1:3: Yahweh’s word came expressly to him, and Yahweh’s hand was there on him in the land of exile.
@@22292@@
Do not let readers feel that “the year must be sorted out before this book can be read”; reading this book does not require solving it first.
@@22293@@
In chapter 1, each of the four living creatures has four faces and four wings, wheels within wheels, and a throne above the expanse; in chapter 10 the prophet says, “This is the living creature that I saw under the God of Israel by the river Chebar; and I knew that they were cherubim.” How chapters 1 and 10 are to be read together, and what the forms stand for, has been explained in many ways through the ages.
@@22294@@
God’s presence and the movement of the throne
@@22295@@
The point is that the throne can move: God is not a God trapped in the temple; his glory can come to the land of exile, and it can also leave the temple.
