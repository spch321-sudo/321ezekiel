@@12987@@
At the very end of the chapter there is one small promise: “In that day I will cause a horn to sprout for the house of Israel, and I will open your mouth among them.” Ezekiel’s tongue had been stuck to the roof of his mouth, and for a long time he could not speak. Now God says he will speak. This is God’s way. When he takes away the reed, he does not leave you out in the wind. He receives you with his own hand. First he takes away what could not hold you up, and keeps the One who truly can. First he empties your hands, then he lets a horn sprout, then he opens your mouth.
@@12988@@
What should I do if the person I “leaned on” has died or gone away?
@@12989@@
This is a very deep loss, and it cannot be settled with one sentence like “just lean on God.” First, please do not blame yourself by asking whether you leaned on that person too much. To love someone and to lean on someone is part of being human. What the passage says is this: put your “whole weight” on God alone. Second, you do not have to be strong right away. You can say to God: Lord, I am very empty right now, and I do not know what to hold on to. Then find someone you trust and sit with them for a while. You do not need to say much. Third, if this loss keeps you from sleeping or eating, or you have thoughts of hurting yourself, please seek help from a pastor and from a professional at the same time. When God takes something away, he receives us with his own hand, but he often does it through the hands of the people around us as well.
@@12990@@
The first deviation is to take the staff of reed in Ezekiel 29:6–7 and preach it as “you leaned on the wrong thing,” using it to blame people who were hurt after leaning on family, doctors, or friends. This chapter says that the one to whom we should give our whole weight is God. It is not putting the wounded on trial. We want the wounded to be comforted when they finish listening, not accused.
@@12991@@
The second deviation is to take the “forty years” of verses 11–13 and use it to count days, draw up a timetable, or calculate some modern event. The church has both literal and symbolic readings of this number. These notes set them side by side and do not use the number to calculate anything. We want the congregation to trust the Lord who has the final word more, not to be more anxious about dates.
@@12992@@
The third deviation is to match Egypt’s desolation to a modern country, pointing at it and saying, “This is God’s judgment,” or using this chapter to judge other people’s disasters. These notes do not name any modern country. We want the congregation to look at themselves more humbly, not to judge others more quickly.
@@12993@@
The fourth deviation is to preach Nebuchadnezzar’s wages in verses 18–20 as “God likes war,” or to find excuses for violence and invasion. The passage says that God rules history. It does not praise killing. That God uses someone does not mean he approves of his methods. We want the congregation to revere God’s sovereignty more, not to think less of human suffering.
@@12994@@
The fifth deviation is to turn “lean on God” into “do not use any person or thing,” telling people to refuse doctors, insurance, savings, and friends. The problem with the reed is putting all your weight on it. We need to distinguish “using” from “leaning on,” so that the congregation is more thankful and also more free, not more isolated.
@@12995@@
I have heard that the prophecy in chapter 26 does not seem to match this one. Will this shake the reliability of the Bible?
@@12996@@
This is an honest question. You can bring it here, and you do not have to pretend you do not have it. Chapter 26 says Nebuchadnezzar would break through Tyre, but chapter 29, verse 18 says he received no wages from it. The church has several readings. Prophecy has layers, first partial and then complete. Or this passage in chapter 29 is a later supplement by the prophet. Or it shows that prophecy in history is not a mechanical clock. People hold each of these, and none of them denies God’s word. These notes set the views side by side and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. You can first hold on to the clearest sentence: “because they worked for me.” God is not afraid of being questioned. The text itself puts this difficulty in front of us.
@@12997@@
The first problem: we are used to treating a “backer” as security. Chinese society talks about connections and networks, about “I know so-and-so,” and in daily life this often really helps, and we do not need to look down on it. But when we put all our security on one backer, the moment that person falls, leaves, or turns on us, our whole self collapses, and we feel ashamed and cannot easily tell anyone. Here Ezekiel 29:6–7 says that this pain is real, and God sees the injury to the shoulders and the loins. You can begin like this: find someone you trust, and say in one sentence whom or what you have leaned on most recently, and that you are willing to put a little less weight on it.
@@12998@@
The second problem: we are used to saying “I built this with my own hard work.” Chinese people value diligence and hard work, and generation after generation has built a family’s fortune brick by brick. This is a virtue. But when hard work turns into “so it is all my own credit,” we have unknowingly said the same sentence as Pharaoh. You can examine yourself like this: this month, was there even one time when you received a result and sincerely said to God, “This is what you gave”? If not, pick one good thing you received today and say it out loud: this was given by the Lord. Then tell a family member. Thanksgiving is giving the river back to the Lord of the river.
@@12999@@
I am afraid that “letting Jesus be King” means giving up everything. What about my job, my family, and my property?
@@13000@@
Letting Jesus be King does not mean giving up everything. It means giving the last place back to him. Your job, your family, and your property are still what God has entrusted to you, and you should manage them well and love them well. Pharaoh’s mistake was not that he had a river, but that he said he had made the river. You can try a small exercise: list the three things you care about most, and say to the Lord, one by one: this is what you gave me to look after, and it is not my king. With some things you will feel relieved after saying it. With others your heart will tighten as you say it. That tightening is where your weight is. You do not need to blame yourself. Just tell the Lord honestly, and let him slowly take over.

VII. Living Out 321: Putting Our Weight Back on Him
@@13002@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He had authority, yet he said, “the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing.” Every day he put his weight on the Father. He is not a reed. He is the one who can hold. We who follow him learn his pattern: having power, and also leaning; having achievements, and also saying, “The Father gave it.”
@@13003@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 29 not to judge Egypt, and not to count forty years, but to let God’s word ask us: Who made my river? How much weight have I put on my staff? Behind every word of judgment there is the sentence “then they will know that I am Yahweh.” That is God’s invitation.
@@13004@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. The Spirit leads us, when it hurts, to ask not only “Why did it break?” but also “Whom am I leaning on?” Take a quiet moment each day and ask: Lord, on which staff did I put my weight today?
@@13005@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of the source position of “this river is mine, I made it.” It is not throwing away the staff, but giving the king’s place to him. Savings, connections, ability, and position can all be used, but none of them is my king.
@@13006@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we are held up, let us not say, “I finally held on.” Everything is grace from the Lord who made the river, in Jesus. If we stand, the glory goes to the Lord who never breaks.
@@13007@@
To build God’s system. A God-centered community acknowledges that both history and daily life are in his hand. Each person is faithful in their own place, and no one or no institution is treated as a pillar that cannot fall. In such a community we do not need to hold everything up alone, and we are not held up by others as if we were reeds. Life first, then relationships, then ministry. Only when our life leans on the right One will our relationships and ministry not snap at the first touch.
@@13008@@
What does “building God’s system” have to do with the staff of reed in this chapter?
@@13009@@
It is very direct. A system means that the whole order of life is in God’s hand: the river is his, and I am the one being fed. A system that God has not built often depends on one person, one sum of money, or one position, and that is simply a bigger reed. You can examine yourself like this: in your home, your small group, or your service, is there any place where everything would collapse if one particular person fell? If so, you do not need to blame that person or yourself. You can do one very concrete thing: share a little of that person’s load, and prepare another companion. A God-centered system lets everyone lean on God with peace of mind, and no one is forced to be someone else’s crutch.

I. Alas for the Day (30:1–9)
@@13396@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. In the village where I lived as a child, on the day before every typhoon, the village head would ride an old bicycle and knock on every door, one household after another. He did not phone, and he did not rely only on the loudspeaker. He stood at the door of each home and called out names: Old Wang, tie down the chicken coop in the back. Xiuqin, clear the drain at your house. We children thought he was very nagging, but the adults listened quietly. Later I understood that he was not coming to scare anyone. A man willing to knock door to door does so because he keeps every household in his heart. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 30, and God does the same. Before the clouds press down, he knocks on the door first.
