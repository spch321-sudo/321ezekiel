I. Shepherds Who Only Feed Themselves (34:1–10)
@@15366@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Please imagine a huge shopping mall on a Saturday afternoon, crowded with people, the music loud. Suddenly an announcement comes over the speakers: Attention, please, there is a five-year-old child in a yellow jacket who cannot find his mother and father on the third floor. At that moment the child is really doing only one thing: standing where he is, crying. He does not know which way to go. He only knows that someone will come to find him.
@@15367@@
Chapter 34 is a story of “someone coming to find them.” But its beginning is not gentle. In verse 2 God tells the prophet to prophesy against the shepherds of Israel: “Woe to the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves! Shouldn’t the shepherds feed the sheep?” This is a very direct question: the shepherd’s work is to feed the sheep, and even a child understands this.
@@15368@@
In verses 3 and 4 God lists, one by one, what the shepherds did: “You eat the fat. You clothe yourself with the wool. You kill the fatlings, but you don’t feed the sheep.” Then come five “haven’ts”: you haven’t strengthened the diseased; you haven’t healed that which was sick; you haven’t bound up that which was broken; you haven’t brought back that which was driven away; you haven’t sought that which was lost. All five are very concrete acts of care, some for the body of the sheep, some for where the sheep have ended up. The last sentence is heavier: “but you have ruled over them with force and with rigor.” What should be cared for is not cared for, and what should be managed is managed very strictly.
@@15369@@
Verses 5 and 6 describe the fate of the sheep. Because there was no shepherd, the sheep were scattered; once scattered, they became food for all the animals of the field. “My sheep wandered through all the mountains, and on every high hill,” and then comes the sentence that hurts the most: no one searched or sought. Please notice that God calls them “my sheep.” The sheep are his, not the shepherds’. The shepherds are only those who have been entrusted.
@@15370@@
In verses 7 to 10 God says: I am against the shepherds, and I will require my sheep at their hand. This is a heavy word, but look at its purpose: “cause them to cease from feeding the sheep. The shepherds won’t feed themselves any more. I will deliver my sheep from their mouth, that they may not be food for them.” The direction of God’s judgment is to stand on the side of the sheep who are being eaten.
@@15371@@
Here we must make one thing clear: the conclusion of this chapter is not to find out who the bad shepherds are. We do not use this chapter to attack any church leader, nor to put labels on anyone. The point of the whole chapter is not “finding the bad shepherds,” but “God himself is coming.” If you have been hurt by someone who led you, this chapter stands on your side first: your wound is real, you do not need to forgive right away, and you do not need to go back to where you were right away. If you are leading others, you do not need to be crushed by this chapter either: the shepherd is also a sheep, and also needs to be shepherded.
@@15372@@

@@15373@@

Verses 5 and 6 say that the sheep were scattered, and no one searched, no one sought; what God says is “my sheep,” so they were not thrown away, they were kept in God’s heart. Just remember: God himself is coming to find you, and you do not need to get well first before he is willing to come. If this hurt leaves you sleepless or low for a long time, please also seek help from both a pastor and a professional.

II. I Myself Will Be the Shepherd of My Sheep (34:11–16)
@@15375@@
Verse 11 is the turning point of the whole chapter, and it has only one word: “Behold.” “For the Lord Yahweh says: ‘Behold, I myself, even I, will search for my sheep, and will seek them out.’” Before, the shepherds did not seek; now God says, I myself. This “myself” is the key to the whole chapter. He does not send another shepherd, he does not issue an order; he sets out himself.
@@15376@@
Verse 12 says that as a shepherd seeks out his flock in the day that he is among his scattered sheep, so God will seek out his sheep, and deliver them out of all places where they were scattered in the cloudy and dark day. Please notice “cloudy and dark”: the place where a sheep got lost is often not a place of beautiful scenery, but a day of thick fog when no one can see the way. God does not wait for the sky to clear before he comes; he goes in to find them on the darkest day.
@@15377@@
In verses 13 and 14 God says three things: I will bring them out, I will gather them, I will feed them. To bring out is to lead them from the places where they were scattered; to gather is to bring them together from all places; to feed is to let them lie down beside good pasture and watercourses, and to rest in a good fold. Here it speaks of a return to the land, and readers through the ages have read it differently: some see it as the return of those who were exiled; some see a greater, spiritual return; some hold both. These notes set out the different views side by side, do not make any one of them a condition of faith, do not name any modern country, and do not draw up a timetable. The point is the subject: I will bring out, I will gather, I will feed. The subject is God.
@@15378@@
Verses 15 and 16 are the most beautiful sentences of this passage: “I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep, and I will cause them to lie down.” Then he takes up, one by one, the five things the shepherds before did not do: “I will seek that which was lost, and will bring back that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick.” Before, there were five “haven’ts”; here are God’s “I will.” You can put the two passages together and read them slowly, and you will find that God did not finish scolding the shepherds and leave; he took up the debt the shepherds owed, and paid it himself, one item at a time.
@@15379@@
The last sentence of verse 16 must be read carefully: “but I will destroy the fat and the strong. I will feed them in justice.” This does not say that people who have strength or resources are guilty in themselves. What the text blames is what comes next: thrusting with the shoulder and pushing the weak with the horns. The purpose of God’s judgment is to protect the oppressed sheep, and the weight of the ending is salvation, not destruction.
@@15380@@
If God himself comes to seek me, do I still need the church and pastors?
@@15381@@
Yes, but your eyes need to be placed in the right place. Verse 11 says that God himself comes to seek and to shepherd; verse 23 says again that he will set up a shepherd over them. God’s shepherding often comes through people, but people are not the source; they are servants who have been entrusted. So you can see it this way: the pastors in the church are those to whom God entrusted you to be looked after together; if they do their duty, you should give thanks, and if they fall short, remember that the one who shepherds you is always God himself. If you have been hurt, you can slowly find a safe small group or a trustworthy companion, and there is no need to rush back to the large gatherings.

III. Light from the Original Language: רֹעֶה, דָּרַשְׁתִּי, בְּרִית (34:2, 11, 25)
@@15383@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth a close look. The first is “shepherd,” the Hebrew רֹעֶה (ro‘eh). Verse 2 says: “Shouldn’t the shepherds feed the sheep?” This word is the participle of the verb “to shepherd.” Its meaning is simple: a shepherd is “one who shepherds.” His name is his work. Some have received the name “shepherd,” but have done only the thing of “being shepherded,” only feeding themselves. Thirty seconds turned into life: whether you are a parent, a group leader, or a manager, is the one thing behind your name still being done?
@@15384@@
The second is “seek,” the Hebrew דָּרַשְׁתִּי (darashti). Verse 11 says: “I myself … will search for my sheep.” Please read it together with verse 6: verse 6 says “no one who searched,” and that “searched” is the same root דָּרַשׁ, meaning to pursue, to inquire, to care. Before, there was no one to darash; later God says, I darashti. This word is also often used for “seeking God,” meaning that for people to seek God is something permitted; and this chapter lets us see that God also “seeks” people, and does so earlier. Thirty seconds turned into life: you do not need to worry that you are not seeking well enough, because you were sought first.
@@15385@@
The third is “covenant,” the Hebrew בְּרִית (berit). Verse 25 says: “I will make with them a covenant of peace.” What follows is שָׁלוֹם (shalom), peace. This is not a paper with no fighting on it; shalom is whole, complete, lacking nothing. To make a covenant is for God to stake himself on it: I am your God, and you are my sheep (verse 31). Thirty seconds turned into life: your security is not built on your own performance, but on the one sentence he spoke in the covenant.
@@15386@@
Join the three words together and you have the road of this chapter: there is a name called shepherd (ro‘eh), but the work of shepherding is not done; so God himself says, I darashti, I myself will seek; and then he makes with the sheep a berit shalom, a covenant of peace. To shepherd, to seek, to covenant: these are the three verbs of this chapter.
@@15387@@
The word “shepherd” is so simple. Why does God rebuke the shepherds with such heavy words?
@@15388@@
Because the name “shepherd” is itself a trust. The רֹעֶה of verse 2 literally means “one who shepherds,” that is, his name and his work are one thing. What God rebukes is not that they lacked position, but that the name remained while the work was gone. They ate the fat and wore the wool, yet did not feed the sheep. This happens not only to church leaders, but to everyone who has been entrusted: parents, group leaders, managers, teachers. This chapter does not ask us to cross-examine others, but lets us first ask ourselves: what name do I carry, and am I doing what that name requires? You can choose one name, and this week do just one small thing for it.

IV. Between Sheep and Sheep, My Servant, a Covenant of Peace (34:17–31)
@@15390@@
In verse 17 God’s word turns to the flock: “As for you, O my flock, the Lord Yahweh says: ‘Behold, I judge between sheep and sheep, the rams and the male goats.’” Before, God rebuked the shepherds; now he turns around and speaks to the sheep. Because the problem is not only above, but also between sheep and sheep.
@@15391@@
Verses 18 and 19 give a very down-to-earth picture: you fat sheep, does it seem a small thing to feed on the good pasture, that you must tread down with your feet the residue of your pasture? And to drink the clear waters, that you must foul the residue with your feet? As for my sheep, they eat what you have trodden with your feet, and drink what you have fouled with your feet. Imagine a buffet table: someone piles his plate full, cannot finish it, and then pours the leftover soup into the shared water jug, and the people behind can only drink from that jug. This is a very quiet kind of oppression: not with a knife, but with taking it for granted.
@@15392@@
Verse 21 says again: “you thrust with side and with shoulder, and push all the diseased with your horns, until you have scattered them abroad.” People with strength are not necessarily bad people, but strength that is not governed will press on others. This passage does not ask us to put the label “fat sheep” or “lean sheep” on people, nor does it ask those who have gifts and resources to be ashamed of what they have. The question is: how do I use my strength? Is my strength protecting the lean sheep, or pushing them aside? Verse 22 gives the conclusion: I will save my flock, and they will no more be a prey, and I will judge between sheep and sheep. The end of the judgment is salvation.
@@15393@@
In verses 23 and 24 God says: “I will set up one shepherd over them, and he will feed them, even my servant David.” Who is this “David”? Readers differ: some read him as a king of David’s house in history; some read him as the Messiah who comes from David’s descendants, the Jesus we believe in; some read him as a symbol of an ideal shepherd. These notes set out the different views side by side, do not make any one of them a condition of faith, and do not use this question to win an argument. The text itself says it very clearly: “I, Yahweh, will be their God, and my servant David prince among them.” God is still God, and the servant is among the flock; he did not set himself up, and he did not replace God.
@@15394@@
From verse 25 on is the warmest passage of the whole chapter: “I will make with them a covenant of peace, and will cause evil animals to cease out of the land. They will dwell securely in the wilderness, and sleep in the woods.” The wilderness and the woods were originally the least safe places for sheep; now they can sleep there. Next come rain, trees bearing fruit, the land yielding: all of these are everyday blessings. Verse 27 says that God will break the bars of their yoke and deliver them out of the hand of those who made slaves of them, and “then they will know that I am Yahweh.” Please notice this order: first being delivered, and then knowing. Knowing God comes from the experience of having been delivered by God.
@@15395@@
Verse 31 is the last sentence of this chapter: “You my sheep, the sheep of my pasture, are men, and I am your God.” It begins with “Woe” and ends with “You are my sheep, and I am your God.” The weight of this chapter is not in the rebuke, but in this one sentence of belonging: you are his sheep, and he is your God.
@@15396@@
Who exactly is “my servant David”? How should I understand it so that I do not end up arguing with others?
@@15397@@
This is a place where readers differ. Some read him as a king of David’s house in history; some read him as the Messiah who comes from David’s descendants; some read him as a symbol of an ideal shepherd. These notes set out the different views side by side, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. You do not have to pick a side before you can be shepherded. You can first hold on to the parts the text itself says clearly: first, God is their God; second, the servant is among them, and did not set himself up; third, this shepherd’s work is to shepherd, not to feed himself. If there are different views in your small group, please first ask: does this view make me love the Lord more and become more humble, or does it make me want to win? You do not need to win an argument; what matters is that you let the shepherd shepherd you.
@@15398@@
God says he will “destroy the fat and the strong.” Is this too harsh? Are all people with ability and resources a problem?
@@15399@@
No. What the text blames is the behavior described in verses 18 to 21: trampling the pasture, fouling the water, and pushing aside the lean sheep with shoulder and horns. Having gifts and resources is not in itself a sin, and the text does not teach us to put the label of fat sheep or lean sheep on people. The purpose of the judgment is to protect the oppressed sheep, and the end is salvation: verse 22 says, I will save my flock, and they will no more be a prey. So instead of asking “who is a fat sheep,” it is better to ask: today, whom did my strength protect? Whom did it push aside? If you have been pressed down, there is no need to rush to take this passage as a reason for others to suffer; you do not have to carry it out for God, and you do not have to forgive first before you can be accepted.
@@15400@@
The first deviation is to take the “shepherds of Israel” of Ezekiel 34:2–10 and point at some church leader and say he is a bad shepherd. The conclusion of this chapter is not to find the bad shepherds, but that God himself will be the shepherd. We want the congregation to trust that shepherd more, not to have one more reason to criticize.
@@15401@@
The second deviation is to take “I myself will search” in Ezekiel 34:11–16 and teach it as meaning that people do not need the church, do not need pastors, and do not need anyone’s help. God often shepherds through those whom he has entrusted; he is the source, and people are servants. We want the congregation to rely more on God, and also to treasure one another’s care more, not to become more isolated.
@@15402@@
The third deviation is to take the “fat sheep” and “lean sheep” of Ezekiel 34:17–22 and teach that everyone with ability or resources is guilty, or to put labels on people. What the text blames is pressing the weak with strength, and the end of the judgment is salvation. We want those who have more to be more willing to protect others, not to be more ashamed or more guarded.
@@15403@@
The fourth deviation is to take “my servant David” in Ezekiel 34:23–24 and teach it as the one answer, and then demand that others accept it, or argue with people over it. Readers have different views, and these notes set out the different views side by side, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. We want the congregation to know the shepherd better, not to become more argumentative.
@@15404@@
The fifth deviation is to take “the mountains of Israel” in Ezekiel 34:13–14 and teach it as asking people to calculate times, or to develop hostility toward a certain people or country. The subject of this passage is God: I will bring them out, I will gather them, I will feed them. We want the congregation to trust the shepherd more, not to become more anxious or more panicked.
@@15405@@
The first problem: we are very used to “respecting teachers and honoring the Way,” and also very used to “not troubling the pastor.” Sheep do not dare to say they are hurt, for fear of being seen as disrespectful; shepherds also do not dare to say they are tired, for fear of being seen as not spiritual enough. The result is that the hurt sheep leave quietly, and the weary shepherds hold on quietly. The five “haven’ts” in 34:4 are partly the stories that no one said out loud. You can begin like this: find someone you can trust, and say in one sentence where you are right now: I am a little tired, or, I am a little hurt. No defending, no dressing it up, and no need to tell the whole story.
@@15406@@
The second problem: we are very used to “the capable do more,” and also very used to “elders eat first.” People with strength find themselves standing in front, using their shoulders to shield everyone from the wind; but the same shoulders can sometimes, without noticing, push the slower ones off to the side. You can check like this: this month, was there one time when I let someone weaker than me sit first, speak first, eat first? If not, starting from the next gathering, pick someone sitting in the corner and go over to him.

I am a small group leader, and I often feel that I am not being well shepherded myself. Can I still shepherd others?
@@15408@@
You can, because the shepherd is also a sheep. Verse 31 says: “You my sheep.” The leader’s first identity is a sheep. If you feel that you are not being shepherded, you can do three things: first, tell God the truth: Lord, I am tired, and I also need to be shepherded. Second, find a companion, not someone under you, but someone with whom you can speak truthfully. Third, this week, set aside a time without any responsibility, just to be shepherded by God, for example: quietly read the passage of verse 23, or lie on the grass and do nothing. For a shepherd to feed only himself is wrong; for a shepherd never to be shepherded is also not God’s intention. If your weariness has already affected your emotions and your life, please talk with a pastor or someone you trust.

VII. Living Out 321: Let the Good Shepherd Seek Me, and Let Me Be a Sheep Who Follows Him
@@15410@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He said: I am the good shepherd; the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. He sought the lost sheep, and when he found it, he carried it on his shoulders and went home rejoicing. The five things of Ezekiel 34:16, seeking the lost, bringing back the driven away, binding up the broken, strengthening the sick, one by one became real in Jesus. Those who follow him also learn to treat people this way.
@@15411@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read Ezekiel 34 not to judge who the bad shepherds are, but to let God’s word set ourselves straight: Have I fed only myself? Have I trampled the pasture and fouled the water? Behind every rebuke there is an invitation; the invitation of this chapter is verse 15: I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep, and I will cause them to lie down.
@@15412@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lets us see the stray sheep near us, and it is the Holy Spirit who makes us willing to walk over, instead of waiting for people to come find us. Every day, be quiet for a moment and ask one sentence: Lord, which sheep today do you want me to go and look in on?
@@15413@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of two things: the self-sufficiency of “I do not need to be shepherded,” and the loneliness of “no one looks after me.” To let Jesus be King is to admit: I am the sheep, and he is the shepherd; verse 24 says that his servant is prince among them, and God is still their God.
@@15414@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we have been found again, we do not say “I finally found the way”; it was the shepherd who found me. If we are leading, we do not say “I raised them”; it is the shepherd who is shepherding. All the glory goes to the one who himself is the shepherd of my sheep.
@@15415@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is a flock shepherded by the shepherd and also looking after one another: the diseased are strengthened by someone, the sick are healed by someone, the broken are bound up by someone, the lost are sought by someone; those with strength use their strength to protect others, not to push them aside. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: first be found by the shepherd, and then we can be good shepherds.
@@15416@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “he is my shepherd”?
@@15417@@
The relationship is very direct. In the Bible, shepherd is often another name for king: verses 23 and 24 speak of shepherding and of ruling together. If Jesus is my shepherd, I do not need to look for water myself or find pasture myself, and I do not need to prove to others how well I can walk. Many times we say with our mouths that Jesus is King, but when we get tired, our first reaction is to hold on by ourselves. You can practice like this: every morning, say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to hold up by myself today is this; what I am most afraid of being seen today is this; today I place them in your hands. Over time you will find that being shepherded is not weakness, but a kind of peace of heart.

VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Shepherd
@@15419@@
The proud type says: “I can find pasture myself. I do not need to be shepherded, and I do not need anyone’s care.” The text says to him: “You my sheep, the sheep of my pasture.” Pride is leaving man’s position and grabbing God’s power, wanting to take God’s place as the shepherd. Returning to one’s position is admitting: I am the sheep, and he is the shepherd. First say one sentence to God: “Lord, I am willing to be shepherded by you.”
@@15420@@
The fearful type says: “If I do not hold myself up, no one will look after me; if I am alone, I will be eaten by the wild animals.” God says: I will deliver my sheep from their mouth. The fearful type needs to learn to exchange “I must hold myself up” for “I lie down in his fold.” Start with the smallest thing: today, hand one worry over to the shepherd.
@@15421@@
The shame type says: “I am a lost sheep. I have gone too far, and he will not come to find me.” God says: “I will seek that which was lost, and will bring back that which was driven away.” Shame says “you have gone too far”; God says “I myself will seek.” The shame type does not need to walk back first; just stop hiding, and let him find you.
@@15422@@
The wounded type says: “The one who led me hurt me. I do not want to trust any shepherd again.” God did not tell you to forgive right away, and he did not tell you to go back right away. He says: “I will bind up that which was broken.” The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you, and tell your story; those who accompany should not rush to say “you must forgive,” but only say: “I am here, I will be with you.”
@@15423@@
First, every day spend three minutes reading verse 15, and say to God: “You are my shepherd, I am your sheep, and today I lie down in your pasture.” Mark it on the calendar.
@@15424@@
Second, write down one thing in which you have recently “scattered,” without polishing it, and write down whom you are willing to let know, and say his name.
@@15425@@
Third, find someone who has recently been weaker or quieter, and this week, with a phone call, a cup of tea, or a meal, tell him: I thought of you, I am here. Write down the name and the date.
@@15426@@
Fourth, check your strength: this week, is there one thing in which you can let a weaker person go first, sit first, eat first? Write down which one and which day.
@@15427@@
Fifth, if you are leading others, find a companion and tell him: I also need to be shepherded, and set the time for your next meeting.

The simplest thing is to take your phone today, find someone who has been very quiet lately, and send one sentence: I thought of you. How have you been? It does not need to be long, it does not need to make a point, and you do not need to expect an answer right away. This is a very small step of “I will seek that which was lost” in verse 16, taken in you. Then do one thing for yourself: tonight before you fall asleep, say to God: You are my shepherd, I am tired today, please shepherd me. In this way, listening to the sermon becomes footsteps. At the next small group meeting, just say: this week I looked for someone; no details are needed, and one name and one date are enough.
@@15433@@
What is most distinctive about Ezekiel 34 is that it begins with one word, “Woe,” and ends with one sentence, “You are my sheep, and I am your God.” It begins with shepherds who only feed themselves, and sheep wandering on the mountains with no one to seek them; in the middle God himself says, I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep; at the end is the covenant of peace, and the sheep lie down securely in the wilderness. From the shepherds’ failure to God himself coming, from wandering to lying down, this is a God who seeks lost sheep.
