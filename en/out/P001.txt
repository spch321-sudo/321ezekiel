@@10@@
The deepest thought in the heart of an exile is often not “I am so miserable,” but “God is not here.” The temple is far away, the glory stayed back in the old place, and we have been thrown onto a muddy bank where no one sees us. Perhaps you are sitting beside your own River Chebar right now, feeling that your situation does not deserve God’s presence. The answer of Ezekiel chapter 1 is not a word of comfort but a picture: the heavens opened. In the land of exile, beside a channel dug by human hands, glory comes down. The throne is still there, the rims of the wheels are full of eyes, and wherever the Spirit is, everything moves together. God did not wait for people to return to the temple before he appeared. He came himself. And the one who sees can do only one thing: fall on his face.
@@11@@
Glory is not tied to the temple. In the land of exile God opens the heavens to people whose road has run out; the one who sees him first falls to the ground, and only then hears him speak.
@@12@@
Beside the River Chebar, the heavens opened for Ezekiel. He saw a stormy wind, a great cloud, four living creatures in the fire, and wheels within wheels, full of eyes; wherever the Spirit went, they went. Above the expanse was a throne, and on the throne a likeness as the appearance of a man, with the brightness of a rainbow all around. As soon as he saw it, he fell on his face, and he heard the voice of one speaking.
@@13@@
God’s glory is not tied to the temple. In the land of exile he opens the heavens; the throne is still there, and wherever the Spirit is, everything moves together.
@@14@@
The heavens were opened from above (נִפְתְּחוּ, passive, perfect); no one climbed up to them. He comes to those whose road has ended. We do not have to put ourselves in order first to qualify to be found by him.
@@15@@
Whoever sees the glory falls down first. Selflessness is not working hard to make yourself small; it is that after seeing him, you no longer stand there holding everything up. Only the one who is on the ground can hear him speak.

<p><strong>① The “thirtieth year”: thirty years of what?</strong></p><ul><li>The prophet’s age: he was thirty, the age at which priests began their service</li><li>Counted from some religious reform, or from the year the book of the law was found</li><li>Counted from some international event, in parallel with the dating in verse 2</li></ul><p>The text itself does not say; verse 2 gives another, clear date.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: set them side by side; do not make any of them a condition of faith.</strong> For the position of the whole book, see the issue “Thirty years of what?”: what this verse truly says is “the heavens were opened,” not the number thirty.</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>The heavens opened, not in the temple but at a riverside in the land of exile.</strong></p><p>The first verse has already told us his situation: “I was among the captives.” He came from a priestly family and should have been serving in the temple in Jerusalem. Now he sits beside a canal on the plain of Babylon. The temple is far away, the altar is far away, and even the direction of prayer seems to face a ruin.</p><p>The verb נִפְתְּחוּ is Niphal, passive: the heavens “were opened.” He did not climb up, and he did not ask; the sky above split open of itself. The “visions” that follow are plural, a whole series of sights.</p><p><strong>He did not go looking for God; God came to the place where he was sitting.</strong></p><p>Notice that he does not say, “I was in Jerusalem.” He puts himself “among the captives,” not pretending to be an exception. God’s vision first comes to someone who honestly admits his situation.</p><p>Landing it: do you also think that God’s presence has to wait until things improve, until you move back to “the right place”? The riverside that makes you feel most forgotten may be exactly where the heavens are about to open.</p>

<p><strong>הָיֹה הָיָה (the doubled “came”)</strong></p><ul><li>An infinitive absolute plus a finite form of the same root, a common Hebrew way of strengthening a statement</li><li>Some stress “it really happened”: the word truly came</li><li>Some stress “force”: the manner of its coming cannot be ignored</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: both together.</strong> The word is not the prophet’s own idea but an event that came from outside, and the place where it came was the land of exile.</p></blockquote>
@@54@@
<p><strong>The word of God found a priest who had no temple to stand in.</strong></p><p>Verse 2 does not use the king’s regnal year but counts by “King Jehoiachin’s captivity.” This dating is bitter: their time is counted from the day of loss.</p><p>Verse 3 uses the third person, “Ezekiel,” and then in the second half, “was there on him,” the text turns back to “I.” The same man is “he” when the word of God names him, and “I” when the hand of God lays hold of him.</p><p>“Yahweh’s word” and “Yahweh’s hand” appear side by side. These are the two movements of the whole book: the word comes, the hand lays hold. The word lets a person understand; the hand lets a person stand. In the many years to come, Ezekiel would often speak words nobody wanted to hear, and the only thing that held him up was this hand.</p><p>One thing is easy to miss: the one to whom this word came is called “priest.” All his life he had been preparing for service in the temple. Now the temple cannot be entered, and God changed his service into another kind: opening his mouth to speak.</p><p>Landing it: when God chooses someone, he does not look at whether that person is in “the right position”; he looks only at whether the word has somewhere to land. The land of exile is also a place where he speaks. You do not have to change where you are now before you qualify to hear.</p>

<p><strong>What is חַשְׁמַל?</strong></p><ul><li>Amber</li><li>An alloy of gold and silver (electrum)</li><li>A shining metal, material unknown, indicating only the color of the light</li></ul><p>This word appears only three times, all in Ezekiel, so other passages cannot be used for comparison.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the point is the “as it were,” not the material.</strong> The prophet uses כְּעֵין, “like the appearance of.” He is describing what he saw, not naming a mineral.</p></blockquote>
@@57@@
<p><strong>What he saw first was not God, but a storm.</strong></p><p>A stormy wind, a great cloud, fire, coming from the north. To a captive, the north is not a good direction; the armies that destroyed the nation came down from there. The first thing he saw was the very kind of thing he feared most.</p><p>Then the style changes. “Flashing fire” uses the Hithpael: the fire rolls and grips itself, needing no firewood. Around the fire is brightness, and in the middle of the fire is the light of glowing metal. The further toward the center you look, the less it looks like disaster and the more it looks like glory.</p><p>Notice the words again: “like,” “as it were,” “as the appearance of.” From this verse on they keep appearing. The prophet is not describing God; he is confessing: my language is not enough.</p><p>So the first feeling this verse gives is fear, but the second is being drawn in. God’s presence is often like this: first it makes a person unable to stand steady, then it makes him unable to look away.</p><p>Landing it: the storm that comes into your life is not necessarily coming to destroy you. You cannot tell what direction it came from, but at the center of the storm may stand the Lord you thought had left. Do not hurry to say “What is this?” First look, and then look a little longer.</p>

<p><strong>① What does “straight feet” mean?</strong></p><ul><li>The legs are straight pillars with no knee to bend, so they go forward without turning</li><li>It describes standing upright and straight</li></ul><p><strong>② Who are these living creatures?</strong></p><p>Here they are called only “living creatures.” Later, in chapter 10, when the prophet sees the same likenesses, he says plainly that they are cherubim.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: for now, say only “living creatures.”</strong> When the prophet looked, he did not know the name; when we read, we look along with the prophet first and do not hurry to attach a label.</p></blockquote>
@@60@@
<p><strong>Every detail of the four living creatures says: these are not here to play.</strong></p><p>They have the likeness of a man, wings, the hooves of a calf, and the hands of a man. They are not monsters; they are put together as “the sum of created things”: the intelligence of man, the height of wings, the strength of an ox’s hoof, and yet under the wings there are still a man’s hands.</p><p>Those hands are hidden below the wings, and that is not decoration. Beneath God’s throne the living creatures do not fly on their own; they serve. They do not use their hands to grab at anything; the hands are under the wings and are not stretched out.</p><p>The legs are straight and the bronze is burnished. This is a straightness, an obedience, a lack of crookedness, as if the whole body had only one direction.</p><p>It is also worth pausing at the fact that the likeness of a man comes first. In the sum of created things man has not been thrown away; those who have been carried off and looked down on still have a place in God’s vision.</p><p>Landing it: this picture is not for you to remember the details. It is for you to see what truly belongs to God looks like: there is power and wisdom, yet no one clutching his own strength. Are your hands stretched out to grab, or hidden under the wings, waiting to serve?</p>

<p><strong>Whose spirit is הָרוּחַ (“the spirit”)?</strong></p><ul><li>God’s Spirit, guiding the direction of the living creatures</li><li>The living creatures’ own spirit (will, vitality)</li><li>A wind, simply indicating direction</li></ul><p>This verse has the definite article, which points to a particular one; verse 20 then says “the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels.”</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the point is not to sort out whose spirit it is, but “wherever the Spirit was to go, they went.”</strong> The direction is not decided by the living creatures.</p></blockquote>
@@63@@
<p><strong>Four faces, and not one looks back.</strong></p><p>Man, lion, ox, eagle: each is the peak of one kind of creature. Man is the crown of creation, the lion is king of beasts, the ox is the best at bearing burdens, the eagle flies the highest. Together these four are like the whole created world standing beneath the throne.</p><p>But the point of the text is not the faces but the direction. The same word for “face” also means “front.” In front of each living creature is one direction, so they “went straight forward” and had no need to turn around.</p><p>Verse 12 gives the reason: “Wherever the spirit was to go, they went.” They do not consult one another or look back at the road, because the direction is with the Spirit and not with themselves.</p><p>Four faces face four directions, yet none blocks another. Every direction has someone watching it, and every front has a road.</p><p>Landing it: we often turn around halfway, because we are not sure it is right. These living creatures are not smarter than we are; they have handed over the direction. Today’s direction: did you set it yourself, or are you waiting for the Spirit?</p>

<p><strong>The fire does not burn the living creatures from outside; the fire walks among them.</strong></p><p>Burning coals, torches, lightning. The prophet uses every kind of fire he can think of, and still says “like.” Notice the preposition: the fire is not outside the living creatures; it “went up and down among” them.</p><p>In the Old Testament fire is often God’s presence, and also judgment. Here both are present: the fire has brightness, and it also has lightning. He is the one who gives light, and also the one who purifies.</p><p>The living creatures running back and forth like flashes of lightning show speed, and also response. I. The Heavens Opened at the Riverside (1:1–3)
@@387@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever moved house? I know a brother who was transferred to work in another city. On the first night, with cardboard boxes stacked to the ceiling and no bulb yet in the socket, he sat on the floor and ate a boxed meal by the light of his phone. He said the most painful thing at that moment was not tiredness but a thought that popped up: is my God left behind at the old home? The old church, the old seat, the old pastor, all far away. We have all had this thought to some degree. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 1, and the main character is a man who was moved away, and not by his own choice, but driven out.
@@388@@
Look first at where verse 1 puts him: “among the captives by the river Chebar.” Please notice the last words: among the captives. He is not in the temple, not beside the altar, not in any “right place.” He is standing among a crowd with no choice. Verse 2 adds a mark: it was the fifth year of King Jehoiachin’s captivity. Even the king had been brought. David’s house, too, was sitting in this foreign land. No one could say, this is someone else’s suffering.
@@389@@
Verse 3 carries great weight: “Yahweh’s word came to Ezekiel the priest, the son of Buzi.” He was a priest’s son, born to be ready to serve in the temple. Now the temple was far away, and the road to service was cut. His life was like a tree pulled up by the roots and left lying on someone else’s soil. And right then God’s word came to him, and God’s hand was on him. Not after he had stood steady again, but while he was still lying on the muddy ground.
@@390@@
About the “thirtieth year” in verse 1 we must say honestly: thirty years of what? Some say the prophet was thirty, which is exactly the age priests began their service; some say it is a dating counted from a certain great event; and some say the text does not explain, because verse 2 already gives a clear year. Each has its reasons. We set them side by side and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. A person’s faith should not be built on whether he can solve a number. What this verse truly wants you to see are the last words: the heavens were opened.
@@391@@
Please write this sentence down: God’s word does not come to those who are prepared; it comes to those whose road has run out. Your situation today may not be as far away as the River Chebar, but you may also have a corner where you feel “unworthy”: too messy, too bitter, too shabby. The text does not say these situations are good things; exile is not good. The text says only one thing: these situations cannot stop him.
@@392@@
My situation right now is terrible. Is God really here?
@@393@@
This question is honest and does not need to be pushed down first. Chapter 1 does not tell the captives to have faith first and then see the heavens open; the heavens opened first. You can begin like this: do not ask “Is he here?” but only “If he is here, where is here?” Then write your situation in one sentence; the place, the day, and the people can all be there. Then say to God: I am here now; please let me see you. A prayer like this can be very small and very short. If your trouble is deep, for example the loss of a loved one, serious illness, or a long time without seeing a way out, do not force yourself to speak beautifully, and please also let a pastor or someone you trust walk with you for a while.

II. A Stormy Wind and a Great Cloud: The Prophet Can Only Say “Like” (1:4–14)
@@395@@
Next comes the hardest part of this chapter to read: a stormy wind, a great cloud, fire, and four living creatures, each with four faces and four wings. Some people get to verse 4 and close the book. I once asked a five-year-old to draw his grandmother. He stopped halfway and said, “Grandma is not like this. Grandma is like...” and then he could not say. Here the prophet is like that child. Count them: through this whole passage he keeps saying “like”: like shining metal, like burning coals, like torches, like a flash of lightning.
@@396@@
So we set a rule: no drawing diagrams, no measuring the wheels, no looking for a match for every detail. Verse 10 says that the four living creatures had the face of a man in front, the face of a lion on the right, the face of an ox on the left, and the face of an eagle at the back. Some say this speaks of the most honored, the strongest, the hardest-working, and the highest-flying among created things, all before God. Some say it is the appearance of those who stand in attendance in heaven. Others say the text does not explain and there is no need to guess. We listen to all of these readings, but we do not add one word to the text. There is only one thing the text itself says clearly.
@@397@@
Verse 12: “Each one went straight forward: where the spirit was to go, they went. They didn’t turn when they went.” This idea appears again and again in this chapter: verse 9 says they did not turn when they went, verse 12 says it again, and verse 17, speaking of the wheels, says it once more. When the text repeats, it is saying to us: remember this.
@@398@@
Think about one “turning” from yesterday. The Spirit said this way, my mouth said yes, but my feet went the other way; God said do not be afraid, but my heart kept circling that matter. Turning is the motion we have practiced the most. But the living creatures went straight forward. This is not because they have no feelings. Look at them: legs straight, the sole of the foot like the sole of a calf’s foot, sparkling like burnished bronze, running back and forth like a flash of lightning. That is the freest appearance there is, because they are not wrestling with God; they are standing in his heart.
@@399@@
There is one more detail: verse 13 says the fire went up and down among the living creatures, and lightning went out of the fire. This is not a fire burning at random; it is a living light moving back and forth among them. To a people who thought God had been left behind in the old place, this is world-turning news: the temple does not move, he moves. His glory is not tied to one building.
@@400@@
I cannot understand the four living creatures and the wheels at all. Have I read this chapter for nothing?
@@401@@
Not for nothing. The prophet himself keeps saying “like” and “as it were”; even he did not say it completely. To read this passage you do not need to understand every face and every wheel first. You can grasp just three things. First, the glory of this God is moving and is not tied to one place. Second, they go straight forward and do not turn. Third, the result for the one who sees is that he falls on his face. If you want to see various interpretations, you can read together with a pastor or a fellow reader, but first put these three things in your heart. Not understanding does not mean you are not spiritual; the prophet also fell down when he saw. You do not have to understand everything at once. Today, just take these three things and read Ezekiel 1:26–28 once more.

III. Light from the Original: Opened, Spirit, Fell (1:1, 20, 28)
@@403@@
This chapter has three original words you can take home in thirty seconds. The first is “opened,” נִפְתְּחוּ; verse 1 says “the heavens were opened.” This verb is passive, and it is perfect: the heavens were opened, and they stand open. The text does not say who climbed up and pried them open. It was opened from above, not pushed from below. So we do not need to ask God to come closer to us; we only need to admit: the heavens are already open.
@@404@@
The second word is רוּחַ (ruakh); verse 20 says “the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels.” This word is at once spirit, wind, and breath. The “stormy wind” of verse 4 is רוּחַ, and the “spirit” of verses 12 and 20 is also רוּחַ. The same word: when it blows in, it is wind; when it leads the way, it is Spirit. So the Spirit is not an abstract idea; it is the One who moves, leads, and has direction. The reason the wheels could go was not that the wheels were big or fast, but that the Spirit was in them.
@@405@@
The third word is וָאֶפֹּל (wa'eppol); verse 28 says “I fell on my face,” literally “and I fell.” Its root נָפַל is also used for a person falling to the ground and for a city wall collapsing. He did not bow gracefully; his knees gave way and his whole body went down. This is not a technique; it is a reaction. Someone who sees the glory does not need to be taught how to fall down.
@@406@@
Join the three words, and this is the journey of the chapter: the heavens were opened (נִפְתְּחוּ), the Spirit leads within it (רוּחַ), and the man fell down (וָאֶפֹּל). Do not reverse the order: it is not that people fall down first and then the heavens open; the heavens open first, and then people fall down.
@@407@@
“Spirit” in the original is also “wind.” What help is that for my daily life?
@@408@@
The help is very practical. Wind is something you cannot see, but you can see what it moves. You cannot see the Spirit, but you can see this: a person who used to be very calculating suddenly is willing to yield; someone who used to be afraid suddenly is willing to speak. This is the Spirit leading the way. So each morning you can ask one question: where is the wind today? That is, in which matter do I feel a gentle push, and yet want to turn away? Then choose one and follow it. It need not be big; a phone call or one apology is enough. This kind of practice is the first step of “wherever the Spirit goes, I go.”
