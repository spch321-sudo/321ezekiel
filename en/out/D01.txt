@@0@@
<strong>The place he is sent is a people who will not listen.</strong><p>“I send you.” In the original this is a participle, and it is going on right now: he is sending you. This is not a consultation, and it is not a test.</p><p>Sent to where? To the “rebels.” That word appears twice in just two verses, and it is tied to the fathers: “even to this very day” means that from the old days until now it has never been broken.</p><p>What is more startling is that the ones he is sent to are “children.” They are not outsiders. They are the children of the family. Their faces are hard, and their hearts are stiff.</p><p>God gave Ezekiel no guarantee that they would listen. He gave him only one line to say: “This is what the Lord Yahweh says.”</p><p>The phrase “even to this very day” is also worth pausing over. The fathers transgressed, and the children went on transgressing. This was not a momentary slip. It was a road handed down along the family line. Ezekiel stood at the end of this road, to speak to one generation after another of rebellion. What he faced was not a group of people who could be reasoned with, but a wall of “impudent and stiff-hearted.”</p><p>Putting it into practice: the people you are sent to may be the ones closest to you, the ones who listen to you least. Your responsibility is not to make them change. It is to say “the Lord says this,” and not “I say this.”</p>
@@1@@
<strong>The food God gives begins bitter.</strong><p>A hand was stretched out. It was not a voice giving orders. It was a hand. In the hand was a scroll. The picture is quiet, and very near.</p><p>The scroll was spread out before him, written on both sides. There was no blank space where he could add something of his own, and no place he could skip. What was written on it was lamentations, mourning, and woe.</p><p>This is what Ezekiel was to eat. It was not victory. It was not blessing. It was a nation’s lament. The prophet had to eat their sorrow first, and only then could he speak to them.</p><p>God’s word is not always sweet. Sometimes what goes in first is tears.</p><p>Please notice that the text does not say he went and took it himself. It says, “He spread it before me.” The spreading was in God’s hand, and Ezekiel only watched. He could not choose which side to read first, because it was written within and without. A commission like this left him no room to bargain. It left him only one position: to stand before the scroll and listen until God had finished reading it to him.</p><p>Putting it into practice: if you have been entrusted with a message you do not want to say, do not rush to find a nicer version. First let his hand spread the scroll before you, and then see what he wants you to do.</p>
@@2@@
<strong>What was written was lamentation, yet it was sweet in his mouth.</strong><p>In chapter 2, the hand held out the scroll, full of lamentations, mourning, and woe. In this chapter God says: eat it, cause your belly to eat, and fill your bowels with it.</p><p>The prophet did as he was told. He said, “It was as sweet as honey in my mouth.” This was not because the content had changed. The words on the scroll had not been wiped out. It was because it was given by him, because it was his word.</p><p>Please notice the order: first eat, then go and speak. If the word has not yet gone into the belly, it cannot come out of the mouth.</p><p>This is also the first quiet reminder of this chapter: whoever serves others must first be fed himself.</p><p>The phrase “fill your bowels” is not a light one either. God did not want him to taste a mouthful. He wanted him to eat until he was full. If the word stays only at the edge of the mouth, what is spoken is light. If the word goes into the belly, the one who speaks carries the weight of that word. Later, when Ezekiel stood before the exiles, what he brought was not material in his hand, but what he himself had swallowed.</p><p>Putting it into practice: when you read his word recently, was it “looking up information” or “eating”? Even if what you read today is a word you do not want to hear, let it go in first, and then see what taste it has in your mouth.</p>
@@3@@
<strong>The hardest to hear is not a foreign language, but the language of your own home.</strong><p>God said: I am not sending you to a people of a strange speech. If it were that, you could say: the language is not shared, there is nothing I can do.</p><p>The people he sent him to speak the same language, and understand every word he says. They have no excuse for not listening.</p><p>The second half of verse 6 is sharper still: if I sent you to foreigners, “they would listen to you.” In other words, not understanding is not the problem. Understanding and refusing to listen is the problem.</p><p>This makes people very uncomfortable, but it is true. We often think people do not believe because we did not speak well enough or clearly enough. Sometimes that is not the reason.</p><p>Think about those who do understand: they know every word he says, and they know who those words point to. There is no translation to help them put it off, and no misunderstanding to plead their case. This makes the prophet’s work more like a patient standing, waiting not for people to understand, but for people to be willing to turn around.</p><p>Putting it into practice: have you lost heart over the person near you who always understands but never responds? Before you lose heart, think: perhaps what this person is refusing is not you, but him.</p>
@@4@@
<strong>The hardness God gives is so that the prophet will not fall before hard people.</strong><p>God did not tell them to become soft. He first made the prophet hard. He said: I have made your face hard against their faces, and your forehead hard against their foreheads. The same word “hard” is used of them, and of him.</p><p>This is a very strange grace. Usually we ask God to make us tender and loving. Here what he gives is a strength that cannot be knocked down.</p><p>The picture is also concrete: a diamond, which can cut stone; harder than flint. It is not for striking people. It is for standing.</p><p>The point is in the order: his hardness comes first, and “Don’t be afraid” comes after. The strength is given by him. It is not something you trained up.</p><p>Seen this way, a forehead like a diamond, harder than flint, is not for winning an argument. What he had to face was one familiar face after another, and when those faces darkened, a person would easily shrink back. God set this scene before the prophet ahead of time: the looks would come, and the dismay would come too, but he did not have to take a single step back because of them.</p><p>Putting it into practice: do you feel you are not strong enough? Perhaps what you should ask for is not to become strong, but for him to give you a forehead that can stand, while you keep a tender heart.</p>
@@5@@
<strong>First receive it in your heart, then speak it out.</strong><p>“Receive in your heart all my words that I speak to you.” The order in verse 10 is: heart first, ears after. Eating the scroll was the first step, putting the words in the heart is the second, and only then comes the going.</p><p>This “going” is also concrete: to the exiles, to “the children of your people.” They are not strangers far away. They are his own countrymen, who are also in exile.</p><p>They suffer the same as he does. The prophet did not speak to them from a height. He was with them in the land of captivity.</p><p>“Receive in your heart and hear with your ears” puts two actions side by side. What the ears hear is sound. What the heart receives is weight. He wanted Ezekiel to have both, not to be only a loudspeaker.</p><p>The commission that follows even states the outcome ahead of time: “whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse.” Said before he went out the door, this lifts a burden off him in advance: how many listen is not his report card. What he has to do is tell them “This is what the Lord Yahweh says,” without leaving out a word.</p><p>Putting it into practice: the words you are going to speak, have you first said them to yourself? If you have not yet received his word in your own heart, do not rush to speak it to others.</p>
@@6@@
<strong>Behind him was a voice, and the voice said: glory is to be blessed.</strong><p>The Spirit lifted him up. He did not see anything. He only heard: behind him a great rushing, and then someone said: “Blessed be Yahweh’s glory from his place.”</p><p>He heard this sentence on the way, while being lifted up and carried away. The road ahead was the land of captivity, the rebellious house, a scroll of lamentation. Yet the first thing he heard was praise.</p><p>This is an arrangement of order: praise first, then the task. Before the prophet was sent to a place of bitterness, he first heard glory itself being blessed.</p><p>Wings touching one another, wheels turning: his glory is not still. It moves. The prophet could not see the whole of it. He could only hear the sound.</p><p>This voice came from behind him. The prophet could not turn his head, and could not see who was speaking. He could only listen on the road while the Spirit lifted him. The wings of the living creatures touched one another, the wheels turned, and all the movement of heaven went with him, together, toward the land of captivity. He did not set out alone.</p><p>Putting it into practice: before the place you least want to go, let yourself stop for a moment and listen to how he is worthy to be blessed. Strength often begins from there.</p>
@@7@@
<strong>The one who is sent is bitter in heart, yet his hand is strong.</strong><p>Ezekiel did not pretend to be very spiritual. He said, I went in bitterness, in the heat of my spirit. The text leaves this honest feeling just as it was.</p><p>But the other half of the same verse says: Yahweh’s hand was strong on me. The bitterness did not stop his hand. And his hand did not take away the bitterness.</p><p>Then he sat down among the exiles and sat there overwhelmed for seven days. He did not speak first, and he did not hurry to work. He sat first, as they did.</p><p>This is a very deep kind of presence. Before saying anything, sit beside the one who is suffering, and say nothing.</p><p>Tel Aviv was where the exiles lived, by the River Chebar. He did not speak from far away. He went in and lived there. The Spirit brought him here, and he sat among these people “overwhelmed for seven days.” His bitterness and their bitterness met in the same place.</p><p>Putting it into practice: if someone near you is in the bitterest time, perhaps what you most need to do is not speak, but sit down. The text does not ask you to explain his pain. It has only the verb “sit.”</p>
@@8@@
<strong>He did not ask whether he was willing. He said: I have made you.</strong><p>After seven days, the word came to him. These seven days were not sat in vain. He had first sat in the bitterness, and only then was he entrusted with this office.</p><p>“Watchman” is one who stands on the city wall. His work is not to fight, and not to repair the city. It is to watch, and when he sees danger, to blow the trumpet.</p><p>Please notice the two verbs: first “hear,” then “warn.” He must hear it from his mouth before he has any right to speak for him.</p><p>This is the hinge of the whole chapter, and also the definition of Ezekiel’s role in the whole book: he is not a leader, and not a priest. He is one who stands on the height, placed there by him.</p><p>The office of “watchman” is a very familiar picture in a city: someone stands guard on the wall so that the people can sleep. A watchman does not stand there because he is brave. He stands there because he has been appointed to stand there.</p><p>When God says “I have made you,” that is his initiative. What Ezekiel has to do is to “hear the word from my mouth.” The danger he sees is not one he imagined. It is a warning that God has already spoken. The watchman’s eyes are opened through his word.</p><p>Putting it into practice: have you seen someone near you walking toward danger, and hesitated? You are not being asked to judge him. You are being asked to speak. The watchman’s failure is silence.</p>
@@9@@
<strong>What he requires is the silence, not the other person’s refusal to listen.</strong><p>This passage is heavy to read. “I will require his blood at your hand.” But please read it all the way through, because it has two sides.</p><p>The first side: if you see and do not speak, and that person dies in his sin, the responsibility is yours. The second side: if you speak and he still does not turn back, you have saved yourself, and the responsibility is no longer yours.</p><p>Please notice that the watchman does not have to answer for the other person’s decision, only for whether he speaks or not. For the prophet this is a great release: the result is not in your hand.</p><p>But it is also a great challenge: you can no longer use “he won’t listen” as a reason not to speak.</p><p>This passage must also be read under his heart: he takes no pleasure in anyone’s death. He sent the prophet not to add to a person’s guilt, but to give people a chance to turn around.</p><p>What is required is the account of “dying in his iniquity,” which involves the weight of a life. He has placed one person’s life and death on another person’s tongue.</p><p>Putting it into practice: have you kept from speaking because you are afraid of being rejected? Speaking is not to prove you are right. It is to give that person one chance to turn around.</p>
@@10@@
<strong>Even the righteous need to be warned.</strong><p>It is easy to think that the watchman’s task is only to speak to the wicked. Verse 20 goes on to say: the righteous too.</p><p>A person walking in righteousness can also go astray. He said: I lay a stumbling block before him. This is a very serious picture: even the crooked path is under his eyes.</p><p>But the last verse is the gospel: “If you warn the righteous man, that the righteous not sin, and he does not sin, he will surely live.” The word “live” in the original is doubled: live, surely live.</p><p>The purpose of warning, from beginning to end, is these two words. It is not to expose, not to condemn, but to let people live.</p><p>The line “his righteous deeds which he has done will not be remembered” makes the heart tremble. The road a person has walked all his life can be put on his scale again because of one turning away from righteousness into iniquity. This is not that he is stingy. It is that he is unwilling to treat a moment’s turning as a small matter that does not count. So the watchman’s warning is, for the righteous, a timely check.</p><p>Putting it into practice: the brother or sister near you who looks so steady may need a word of reminder. A reminder is not criticism. A reminder is a kind of love, and its purpose is that he may live.</p>
@@11@@
<strong>The glory is not in the temple, but on the plain where he was commanded to go.</strong><p>God said: arise, go out into the plain, and I will talk with you there. He did not ask why. He only arose, and went.</p><p>When he reached the plain, the glory was standing there, like the glory he had seen by the River Chebar. The glory had not left him. The glory was waiting for him at the place he was sent to.</p><p>His reaction was the same as in chapter 1: he fell on his face. He had passed through seven days of dismay, and had heard the watchman’s commission, and still he fell down. This is not his weakness. People who see the glory simply cannot stand.</p><p>Please notice the word “stood”: it was the glory that stood there, not the prophet. The one standing was he.</p><p>The plain is an open place, with no temple walls and no crowd of exiles. He first called him out from the crowd, to stand alone before him. What follows, the muteness, the shut door, the symbolic acts, all begin from this open place. The glory “stood there,” as if waiting for him.</p><p>Putting it into practice: do you think he is only in places you know? Perhaps he is already standing and waiting for you on the plain where you are commanded to go and do not want to.</p>
@@12@@
<strong>The glory had just met him, and next came: go in, and shut the door.</strong><p>In the previous verse he had just fallen on his face. In this verse the Spirit entered into him again and set him on his feet. It is the same grace as in chapter 2: he did not stand up by himself.</p><p>After he stood up, the first command was: “Go, shut yourself inside your house.”</p><p>No one would have expected that the prophet sent to the people would have as his first task to be shut in, unable to go out and walk among them.</p><p>This is a very different kind of ministry: not going out, but being drawn back in. In some seasons he draws you inside, not to leave you idle, but to prepare you.</p><p>“They will put ropes on you” does not say who. It may be people, or it may be circumstances, but the result is the same either way: he “will not go out among them.” Just before, he had sat among the people for seven days, and now he must be separated from them.</p><p>A shut door did not change his office. It changed only the way he spoke. The word had not stopped. It only had to move to another place, take another posture, and wait again for him to speak.</p><p>Putting it into practice: if you are in a season of being restricted, unable to do what you want to do, do not conclude at once that you have been cast off. When he shut you in the house, the glory had just stood before you.</p>
@@13@@
<strong>He first took away his voice, and then let him be his mouth.</strong><p>The tongue sticks to the roof of the mouth. This is a very concrete picture: he could not speak. God made him mute, not as a punishment, but to take his words away and leave only God’s words.</p><p>Look at the dividing line: he would “not be able to correct them.” He could no longer rebuke in his own name, could not offer opinions, could not debate.</p><p>Then comes a condition: “But when I speak with you.” When he speaks, he opens his mouth. The mouth is not his. It is lent to him.</p><p>What he says is also only one sentence: This is what the Lord Yahweh says. Those who hear, let them hear; those who refuse, let them refuse. And again the same closing: a rebellious house.</p><p>The most special thing in this passage is that both muteness and speaking are decided by him. Ezekiel did not decide for himself to be silent, and did not decide for himself to speak. When he was silent, people saw a prophet who could not speak; when he opened his mouth, what people heard was only “This is what the Lord Yahweh says.” Both say the same thing: this man’s mouth does not belong to himself.</p><p>Putting it into practice: what you most need to learn may not be how to speak better, but when not to speak. Wait for him to open your mouth, and then that one sentence will carry weight.</p>
@@14@@
<strong>The prophet is to play a sandbox game, but this game is not funny.</strong><p>He said: take a tile, lay it before yourself, and portray on it Jerusalem. Then lay siege against it, build forts, set camps, and plant battering rams.</p><p>What the exiles cared about most was the city of their homeland, which they thought would not fall. The prophet’s tile was a very small battlefield model laid on the ground.</p><p>The picture is simple. So simple that a child could understand it. He did not first use a long speech. He told the prophet to use his hands, and told people to look with their eyes.</p><p>The word “you” is repeated. The prophet is not a bystander. He is the one who portrays, builds, casts up, and sets. With his own hands he carved out, stroke by stroke, what they did not want to believe.</p><p>A tile is an ordinary thing, a piece of clay one can hold in the hand and lay before oneself. He chose the most ordinary material to set out the greatest matter. The smaller the thing, the more it forces people to come close and look. And once they came close, they found it was the Jerusalem they knew.</p><p>Putting it into practice: some words, if you only say them, no one listens, and you have to live them out. Sometimes he will ask you to see for yourself first, so that others can see. Are you willing to see first?</p>
@@15@@
<strong>Sin is not a thought. It is a weight to be carried.</strong><p>This verse has a very strong word: “bear.” This word is also used elsewhere for “forgive.” It means “to lift up.” To lift up can mean to carry, and it can also mean to take away.</p><p>The prophet lay on his left side and laid the iniquity of the house of Israel on himself. It was not a figure of speech. It was his body, lying there day after day.</p><p>He said: I have appointed the years of their iniquity to be to you a number of days. One day for a year. The consequence of sin has a length. It does not pass in a moment.</p><p>Please notice: this is a sign on the prophet’s body, not the prophet’s merit. He is not a savior. He is only a body borrowed to make a show.</p><p>Three hundred ninety days is not a numbers game. It is days, one after another, that cannot be skipped. Every day the prophet lay there said: all these years, he has seen. Sin is not unrecorded. It is only that he records it slowly, and with patience.</p><p>Putting it into practice: when you see another person’s iniquity, do you want to blame first, or to stop first and think about the weight being carried behind it? Sometimes real shepherding begins by lying down beside the weight.</p>
@@16@@
<strong>He was bound by God, and became a sign that could not turn over.</strong><p>The prophet uncovered his arm and set his face toward the besieged city. This movement is a prepared posture, like a person rolling up his sleeves to get to work.</p><p>But the point of this verse is not in how he moves, but in how he cannot move: “I put ropes on you, and you shall not turn yourself from one side to the other.”</p><p>This is an echo of Ezekiel 3:25: they will put ropes on you. Here it is changed to: I put ropes on you. The one who binds him is God.</p><p>Notice that his prophecy is said to be “toward Jerusalem.” Yet in the whole passage there is not one word spoken. He prophesies, and what he uses is his body.</p><p>Think of that picture: a man lying on his side, his arm uncovered, his face toward a city drawn on a tile, not moving at all. People passing by looked, and without hearing anything he said, knew that something was wrong. No pulpit, no cheering crowd, only a man bound by ropes, setting the future of the whole city before the eyes of all.</p><p>Putting it into practice: is the situation in which you are restricted perhaps ropes he has put on you? Whether illness, age, family, or circumstances, sometimes he is not asking you to go out, but to become a sign in this very place.</p>
@@17@@
<strong>In the days of the siege, even eating is by weight.</strong><p>Six kinds of grain in one vessel: wheat, barley, beans, lentils, millet, and spelt. Not fine food, but what a besieged person can find at hand.</p><p>This is what the prophet had to eat. And it was by weight, and by time. Water was the same.</p><p>In ordinary days people eat by appetite, and eat when they want. In this chapter even what to eat, how much, and when were all fixed.</p><p>This is teaching what a siege is: not a sudden disaster, but ordinary life tightened day by day. Every meal is a reminder.</p><p>The numbers speak too: bread of twenty shekels a day, and water by measure, the sixth part of a hin. These were calculated amounts, just enough not to die, not enough to be full.</p><p>The most disturbing part is “from time to time you shall eat” and “from time to time you shall drink.” In the bitterness of a siege, even time is under control. People no longer eat and drink by feeling, but look at the measure and count mouthful by mouthful.</p><p>Putting it into practice: when you eat today, have you thought that this meal was given? Those who eat more than enough may need to remember that there are people weighing out their bread right now.</p>
@@18@@
<strong>They thought they would be taken away, but did not know that even the food would be unclean.</strong><p>He told the prophet to bake bread with dung from man, before the eyes of all. This command is hard to hear, because it is meant to make people uncomfortable.</p><p>In his law, uncleanness separates people from him. So this act says: those driven out among the nations will eat unclean food in an unclean place.</p><p>He said: this is where I will drive them. It is not fate. It is his hand. And at the same time he is saying: I am still here, and I am still watching.</p><p>Please notice the way of it: “in their sight.” He wants people to see, not to cover it up. The prophet’s humiliation became a mirror for the people.</p><p>The prophet did this before the eyes of all, and verse 13 goes on to speak of “the nations.” Those driven out are not only the people of one city, but the steps of a whole nation, which will go out to the nations. The bread the prophet baked there was already an announcement of the whole road of exile to come.</p><p>Putting it into practice: when he exposes, he also accompanies. If you are in a very undignified situation, do not say at once: he has left. What the text says is: I drive you out, and I watch you.</p>
@@19@@
<strong>What he breaks is the staff that people lean on.</strong><p>Verse 16 gives the meaning: he will break the “staff of bread” in Jerusalem. People’s days are held up by a staff, and bread is one of those staffs. He said: I will break it.</p><p>The result is not immediate starvation, but something slower and more tormenting: eating bread with fearfulness, drinking water in dismay. Even eating and drinking become something with fear in it.</p><p>Then: “dismayed one with another.” The suffering of a siege is not only an empty stomach. The way people look at one another changes too.</p><p>The last line is the heaviest: “pine away in their iniquity.” That is the explanation of the three hundred ninety days on the prophet’s body: what he lay there, weighed, and ate was a rehearsal of all this.</p><p>The phrases “with fearfulness” and “in dismay” take a meal apart. The bread is still bread, and the water is still water, but the one who eats and the one who drinks are no longer the people they were. The food may still be there, but peace is not. What he breaks is really people’s hold on tomorrow.</p><p>Putting it into practice: what is the staff you lean on? Work, savings, health, reputation? This is not to frighten yourself. It is an invitation to think: if one day it were broken, what would you have left in your hand? The answer should be him.</p>
@@20@@
<p><strong>The “staff of bread” is broken.</strong></p><p>Verse 16 says, “I will break the staff of bread” (מַטֵּה־לֶחֶם). Bread is like a walking stick, and people lean on it to walk. When the stick is broken, it is not that people get thinner. It is that they cannot stand.</p><p>God’s picture is arrows: the arrows of famine, evil animals, pestilence, bloodshed, the sword, laid out one after another like a list. Later 14:21 will bring up the four calamities together; this is the first full listing.</p><p>The word “bereave” is one God himself used. He did not avoid its weight. If you have ever lost a child, this passage is not spoken against you. It speaks of the consequence of a whole people leaving God for a long time. It is not any one person’s individual suffering, and it is certainly not a rebuke to the bereaved.</p><p>The last line of the passage is “I, Yahweh, have spoken it.” This row of calamities is not by chance. It is a word he himself bears. If the listener still wants to push all this onto luck, this sentence blocks that road.</p><p>Putting it into practice: this chapter has no way out, not because God has no way out, but because God wants people to see first that the staff they lean on can be broken. Only when the other staffs break are we willing to turn back and take hold of the God who has been holding out his hand all along.</p>
@@21@@
<p><strong>God’s first question is not “Do you know?” but “Do you see?”</strong></p><p>He told him to lift up his eyes and look north. There, at the north gate of the altar, the idol stood at the entrance to the place of sacrifice.</p><p>“Do you see?” This sentence appears four times in this chapter. God is not testing him. He is inviting him. He wants the prophet to see, because he does not want to bear these things alone.</p><p>In verse 6, “that I should go far off from my sanctuary” is the heaviest line in the whole chapter. People think what they do affects only themselves, but it affects the place where God dwells.</p><p>Then God says: there are still greater ones. God did not rush to give a verdict. He first took him to look again. This was not to shame him, but so that one day the prophet could say: I really know, I have seen.</p><p>Verse 5 says the idol was “northward of the gate of the altar, in the entry.” This was not something hiding in the dark. It stood in the place everyone had to pass in and out, so that everyone who came to sacrifice looked up and saw it first. What the prophet was called to see was not a faraway foreign land, but the doorway his own people walked past every day.</p><p>Putting it into practice: is there a part of your own condition that you have never been willing to open your eyes and look at? When God asks “Do you see?”, it is not to frighten you. It is to have you be honest. Starting from that honesty, he can go forward with you.</p>
@@22@@
<p><strong>There is a hole in the wall, and behind the hole a door. People hide their most private things behind the wall.</strong></p><p>From outside everything looks as usual. The court, the doorway, the wall are all where they should be.</p><p>Yet God said: dig. He did not directly show what was behind the wall. He had the prophet do it with his own hand. This was not to torment him. It was to teach him: sometimes the truth takes a little effort before it can be seen.</p><p>That door led to a room that ordinary people did not know about. God said, “Go in, and see.” He did not describe the sin to him. He wanted him to see it with his own eyes. That way, what he said later would not be second-hand.</p><p>Verse 7 says only “a hole in the wall,” and in verse 8, as soon as he dug, “I saw a door.” A wall is meant to separate. Now there was first a hole in the wall, and then a door, so this path had clearly been walked before. What could not be seen from outside had long since become a regular place inside. The instruction in verse 9 is simple: “Go in, and see the wicked abominations that they do here.” Notice the last word, “here.” It is not somewhere else. It is at the gate of God’s own court, the place nearest to him.</p><p>Putting it into practice: every person has a wall in the heart. In the wall there is a hole, and behind the wall a door. Are you willing to let God’s hand dig it open? It is not to be rebuked, but because what is behind the wall can be dealt with only in the light.</p>

<p><strong>Judgment begins beside the altar.</strong></p><p>At the very start of this chapter, it is not the prophet speaking. It is God’s voice, crying out loudly and entering the prophet’s ears.</p><p>Six men came with weapons. The seventh was clothed in linen, with an inkhorn. Linen is the cloth of priests, and the inkhorn is the tool of a scribe. This man had no weapon in his hand.</p><p>Six men and one man stood beside the bronze altar. That is the place of sacrifice, where people bring their sin and seek forgiveness. Now judgment begins from there.</p><p>This picture has a quiet weight. Judgment did not come from the wilderness. It came from God’s house. Because the house is the place where people most ought to hear his voice, and also the place where people least ought to say to him, “You do not see.”</p><p>Verse 1 says the voice cried “in my ears with a loud voice.” The prophet did not find it out with his own eyes. God put the word directly into his ears, and shouted it loudly. Those called were “those who are in charge of the city,” each with a “destroying weapon in his hand,” and verse 2 says they came from “the way of the upper gate, which lies toward the north.” The north gate is exactly the direction where the idol stood in chapter 8. The same direction: in the previous chapter the prophet saw sin, and in this chapter judgment came.</p><p>Putting it into practice: this chapter is not meant to make you afraid, but to make you serious. God’s house is not a place exempt from judgment. The nearer you are to him, the more you ought to hear his voice. When you hear it, turn to him.</p>

This sentence makes us think of another one. In John 17:4, Jesus said to the Father: “I glorified you on the earth. I have accomplished the work which you have given me to do.” On the cross he said: It is finished. The one who walked through all the sighing did it according to the Father’s command, one thing after another, until it was done. We do not say that the man clothed in linen is Christ, because the text does not say so; but this man’s quietness and faithfulness are like a mirror that lets us see the true Lord, who obeyed completely.

<p><strong>The house was filled with the cloud: the nearest, and also the last, appearance of the glory.</strong></p><p>The cloud filled the inner court, the brightness filled the court, and the sound of the wings was heard even to the outer court, like the voice of God Almighty when he speaks.</p><p>This is a picture that makes people tremble. The glory did not hide. It appeared in full. You can imagine it: the whole house, from inside to outside, was in his light and his sound.</p><p>Yet this picture is on the eve of judgment. This makes us understand: God’s appearing is not always comfort. The clearer his glory, the less place there is for sin to hide.</p><p>The name “God Almighty” here points to the sound of the wings. It is not the content of what was said, but the power of the sound. What the prophet heard was a sound far beyond what a person can bear.</p><p>The order in verse 3 is also worth noting: “when the man went in,” the cherubim stood on the right side of the house, and the cloud filled the inner court. Verse 4 says the glory “mounted up from the cherub, and stood over the threshold of the house.” The glory had moved from its original place and stopped at the doorway. Inside the house was full of cloud, while the courts outside were filled with “brightness”: cloud inside, light outside, layer upon layer, from inside to outside. The glory was moving outward. This is the first step of its leaving the house.</p><p>Putting it into practice: what can be filled is you, filled by him. Your house, your home, your life can all be filled with his glory. The question is: when he wants to fill, have I prepared to make room?</p>

In Luke 13:34, Jesus stood in front of the same city and said: “How often I wanted to gather your children together, like a hen gathers her own brood under her wings, and you refused!” When the glory left, the Lord’s heart did not leave. He is the Lord who waits at the door and under the wings for people to turn back. If we want to be like him, we should not shut the door first when others leave.

The first deviation is to take Ezekiel 11:11, “This will not be your cauldron,” and preach it as a general “God will protect you and nothing will happen to you.” The cauldron here is where people put their security in the place of the city and the temple. God’s word takes away this false security. It is not a promise that you will not meet any disaster. We want the congregation to trust him more, not to be more sure that a cauldron is protecting them.
