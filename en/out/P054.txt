@@18305@@
In verse 3 there is a man standing at the gate, his appearance like bronze, with a line of flax and a measuring reed in his hand. The line measures length, and the reed measures straight. This is a patient man. He does not persuade, and he does not argue. He simply measures, one thing at a time. In verse 4 the first words he speaks are the key to the whole chapter: “Son of man, see with your eyes, and hear with your ears, and set your heart on all that I will show you; for you have been brought here so that I may show them to you. Declare all that you see to the house of Israel.” Please notice the order: first see, first hear, set it on your heart, and only then speak. We often do it backwards. We speak first, then add a little looking, and listening, not to mention. God first brings you there, then lets you see, and then lets you speak.
@@18306@@
Is this temple literal, symbolic, or spiritual? Do I have to choose one first?
@@18307@@
No. The temple of chapters 40 to 48 is not exactly the same as Solomon’s temple, nor as the temple built after the exile. Through the centuries there have been three main readings. Some think it is a temple that will actually be built in the future. Some think it describes, in words that exiles could understand, the fullness of God’s presence with his people. And some think it is fulfilled in Christ and the church. These notes set them side by side, and do not assume any timetable for building a temple or offering sacrifices, nor do they judge brothers and sisters who see it differently. The prophet himself says only “like,” and he did not finish explaining the vision first. If you are in a foreign land and have lost your home, you do not need to settle how to read this temple before God can comfort you. See first, hear first, set it on your heart. That is enough.
@@18308@@
II. Gate After Gate, One Reed (40:5–37)
@@18309@@
From verse 5 come the long measurements. The temple has a wall all around, and the man measures the wall with the reed: one reed thick and one reed high. Then he comes to the gate facing east, goes up its steps, and measures one threshold of the gate, one reed, and the other threshold, one reed; he measures the lodges, the porch, and the gateway. As you read on, verse after verse, you may think: so many numbers, what do they have to do with me? Please read it once more and you will find that the text is doing something very special: it repeats. After the east gate is measured, the north gate has “its length and its width” the same as the first gate; the south gate is “according to the former measures”; and the south, east, and north gates of the inner court are also “according to the former measures.” The repetition itself is speaking.
@@18310@@
What is the repetition saying? There is no favoritism. The north gate is like the east gate, and the south gate is like the north gate; the gates of the outer court have seven steps, and those of the inner court have eight, and in every direction the rule is the same. This temple is not open only to people from one direction, and there is not a path only for those who come through the main gate. I think of the school playground when I was a child. Every lane of the running track was the same width, because if they were not the same width, the race would not be fair. God’s house is like this too: the way is prepared by him, the measures are measured by him, and no one can rely on their own connections to enter a closer place than anyone else.
@@18311@@
There are also some details we cannot understand. For example, the “closed windows” in verse 16: the original is uncertain, and different scholars read it differently. We do not quarrel over it, and we do not invent a meaning for it. What we can be sure of is this: the gates are guarded, the windows are designed, and the posts have carved palm trees. All of these say the same thing: his holiness is real, and his presence is ordered. When we meet something we cannot understand, we do not need to feel unspiritual. We can leave a place for “I am not sure” and keep walking. Sometimes honestly saying “I am not sure” brings us closer to the text than forcing an answer.
@@18312@@
Finally, I want to ask you a very everyday question: is there a door that you have not walked through for a very long time? It may be a church, a person you once hurt, or a prayer you can no longer say. Every gate in chapter 40 has been measured, with steps, a porch, and guardrooms. This means: God has prepared the way in, and not carelessly. If you feel that the road is too far, please look only at the first step.
@@18313@@
Chapter 40 has so many numbers that I cannot get through it. Can I skip them?
@@18314@@
You can read slowly, and you do not have to finish at once. The test is simple: can this number be turned, within thirty seconds, into how a person lives today? If not, leave it in the study. But please do not skip the “repetition”: the north, south, and east gates are all “according to the former measures,” and this itself is an important message: God shows no favoritism. You can read it this way: at each gate, ask only one question: what is this gate telling me about what kind of God he is? Or you can read only verses 5 to 16, and with a pencil circle every “one reed” and “according to the former measures” that repeats, and then say: Lord, you have measured, and you show no favoritism. Read this way, the numbers are no longer a code, but a comfort.
@@18315@@
III. The Priests’ Rooms and the Sons of Zadok (40:38–49)
@@18316@@
From verse 38 the camera moves into the gateway, where there are rooms, and the priests wash the burnt offering there. Verses 39 to 43 speak of tables made of cut stone, a cubit and a half long, a cubit and a half wide, and a cubit high, prepared for the burnt offering, the sin offering, and the guilt offering. The picture in this part has flesh and blood, and I will not enlarge it. If you have been hurt, you do not need to force yourself to imagine the details. You can stop at just one sentence: sin is faced, and God has prepared a way. This is also the meaning of the whole temple: he does not tell people to hide from sin; he prepares a place where sin can be faced.
@@18317@@
Verse 44 says that beside the north gate of the inner court there are rooms for the singers. Verses 45 and 46 say that the room facing south is for the priests who keep the charge of the house, and the room facing north is for the priests who keep the charge of the altar. They are the sons of Zadok, from among the sons of Levi, who come near to Yahweh to minister to him. Please notice that in this temple there is not only an altar, but also rooms for the singers. Some are set apart to sing, some to keep watch, and everyone has a room of their own. What God prepares is not only ritual, but a people who have a place and a name.
@@18318@@
Here “the sons of Zadok” appear, and different interpreters also see differently who they are. This chapter only names them first; chapter 44 will look at them again, and we will leave it until then. For now I only want to remind you of one thing: this verse must not be used to shut ordinary brothers and sisters outside the door, and no one should be made to feel like an outsider. The qualification for service is God’s invitation, not a person’s bloodline. If you have been hurt in church, or feel pushed to the edge, this verse is not fixing your place; it is saying: God has prepared rooms, and he has also prepared names.
@@18319@@
In verse 47 he measures the inner court, a hundred cubits long and a hundred cubits wide, a square, and the altar is in front of the temple. In verses 48 and 49 he brings the prophet to the porch of the temple, measures the posts of the porch, and the porch itself; there are steps up to the porch, and beside the posts there are pillars, one on this side and one on that. So at the end of this chapter we are already standing at the door of the temple. We have not gone in yet, only stood at the door. But this is enough, because the prophet knows: he is not one who was left outside, but one who was brought here.
@@18320@@
The temple has the burnt offering, the sin offering, and the guilt offering. How can this fit with Christ’s offering “once for all”?
@@18321@@
This is an honest question, and different interpreters read it differently. Some think these offerings are commemorative in the future, and do not conflict with the completeness of Christ’s once-for-all offering. Some think it uses language that could be understood at that time to describe the fullness of God’s presence with his people. And some think it has already been fulfilled in Christ. These notes set them side by side, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. However you read it, it does not weaken the completeness of Christ’s once-for-all offering, nor make anyone feel that they must add something to their salvation. Hebrews says clearly that Christ offered himself only once. So when you read this passage, you can stop at just this sentence: “sin is faced, and God prepares the way.” If you have been hurt, you do not need to look at the pictures in this passage.
@@18322@@
IV. Light from the Original Language: Set Down, Set on the Heart, Keep (40:2, 4, 45)
@@18323@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth a close look. The first is “set down” in verse 2, a causative form of the Hebrew נוּחַ (וַיְנִיחֵנִי). The prophet says: he set me down on a very high mountain. The root meaning of this word is “to give rest” and “to make stop.” In Genesis 2:15, when God “put” Adam in the garden of Eden, the same word is used. So the prophet is brought back not only to change places, but to be put down and settled. The thirty-second transformation is like this: the one thing that wears you out most today, you do not have to climb up with it yourself. He will set you down.
@@18324@@
The second is “set on the heart” in verse 4, the Hebrew שִׂים לֵב. שִׂים means to place, and לֵב is the heart. Literally it says: put the heart on it. We often say “remember,” but this word is more like putting something on the table where you see it every time you pass. God said to the prophet: all that I show you, see with your eyes, hear with your ears, and set your heart on it. First see, then hear, then set it on your heart, and only at last speak. If you read one verse of Scripture today, do just one thing: write it on a piece of paper and put it where you pass every day.
@@18325@@
The third is “keep” in verses 45 and 46, the Hebrew שָׁמַר, and שֹׁמְרֵי is its participle, those who keep. The same word, in Genesis 2, is where Adam “keeps” the garden of Eden. To keep is not to stand guard; it is to preserve well what God has entrusted, and not let it be damaged. Someone keeps the temple, and someone keeps the altar; what each keeps is different, yet all of it is entrusted by God. What are you keeping today? A home, a child, a job, a small group? It does not need to be big. It only needs to be what he has handed to you.
@@18326@@
Join the three words together, and you have the order of this chapter: he sets you down on the mountain, so that you may rest; then he tells you to set on your heart what he shows you; and finally he hands one thing to you to keep. A person does not have to prove himself first to be set down; does not have to grab the word first to hear first; and does not need a big position to have something to keep.
@@18327@@
Is “see with your eyes, hear with your ears, set it on your heart” only for very spiritual people?
@@18328@@
No. Verse 4 was spoken to the prophet, and it is also an invitation to everyone who is willing to set it on their heart. You do not need a special vision or a special voice. You only need a passage of Scripture and a little quiet. You can practice like this: each day choose a short passage, read it once, then listen to it once with your ears (read it aloud, or listen to a recording), then choose one sentence to set on your heart, and think it over once more before sleep. Do not prepare a sermon, do not prepare a sharing, and do not tell anyone yet. If someone says you are “not spiritual enough,” remember that the subject of verse 4 is him: “you have been brought here so that I may show them to you.” Since he has brought you, he will show you.
@@18329@@
The first deviation is to treat chapters 40 to 48 as a blueprint from which a timetable for building the temple can be worked out, and to hint that some date or event is about to happen; or to do arithmetic with every cubit and every reed and preach it as a mysterious code. The text gives no date, and does not ask anyone to calculate. We want the congregation to value God’s presence more, not to calculate more anxiously.
@@18330@@
The second deviation is to teach only one reading and call the other readings unbelief. In the church, different traditions understand this temple differently: literal, symbolic, spiritual fulfillment. These notes set them side by side and make none of them a condition of faith. We want brothers and sisters who see it differently to respect one another, not judge one another.
@@18331@@
The third deviation is to preach “see with your eyes, hear with your ears” as if only people with special spiritual power can see and hear. It was spoken to the prophet, and it is also an invitation to everyone who sets it on their heart. If a sermon makes ordinary believers feel that they are “not qualified,” it must be changed. We want people to be more willing to draw near to God, not to feel smaller.
@@18332@@
The fourth deviation is to frighten people with the pictures of the burnt offering, sin offering, and guilt offering, or to hint that Christ’s one sacrifice is not enough, or that people must do something more to make it enough. Hebrews says Christ offered himself once. We want people to be more sure of the completeness of salvation, and first of all to protect those who have been hurt, not enlarging the pictures.
@@18333@@
The fifth deviation is to preach “the sons of Zadok” as if only a certain family or a certain kind of office-holder is qualified to serve, or to preach the gates, steps, and walls as if the church should build walls to separate different people. What the text says is: the way is prepared by God, and the place is given by God. We want people to feel invited, not excluded.
@@18334@@
I read it in one particular way. Will others say I am wrong? How should we get along?
@@18335@@
They may. Because through the centuries this temple has had three kinds of readings, literal, symbolic, and spiritual, and each takes the text seriously. You can get along this way: first, say your reading and explain why you read it so, and do not say others “do not believe.” Second, listen to the other person finish, and then ask: what makes you sure when you read it this way? Third, find the shared center: God with his people, the glory returning (43:1–5), the river flowing out from under the temple (47:1–12), and the city whose name from that day is “Yahweh Is There” (48:35). Fourth, if someone becomes emotional, stop first and pray for one another. True unity is not reading it the same way; it is loving the same Lord.
@@18336@@
The first question: we are very used to picking up a “ruler” to measure people. We compare grades, salaries, which school the children get into, how many people a church has. From childhood we have been measured, and later we learn to measure others, and after measuring, the heart feels not comfort but unease. In chapter 40 there is a reed, but the one holding it is the man standing at the gate, not the prophet. The prophet only sees, hears, and sets it on his heart. You can check yourself like this: this week, did I even once, in my heart, take my own ruler and measure someone? If so, the next time, pray silently first: Lord, you have measured; I will see first, I will hear first.
@@18337@@
The second question: people who have left home are very used to “not going back.” They cannot go back at the new year and the festivals, and slowly they stop asking when they will go back; the distance from family grows wider and wider, and once they are used to it, they no longer feel the pain. The prophet in 40:1–2 is just such a person, yet God brought him back. You can begin like this: this month, make a phone call to a family member or friend you have not been in touch with for a long time. Do not talk about big things; just say one sentence: I thought of you. To someone who is in a foreign land, or has lost family or position, please do not say, “God’s plan is perfect, you must be strong.” Just say: I am with you.
@@18338@@
I have been in a foreign land so long that I have grown used to not going home. Is that wrong?
@@18339@@
It is not wrong; it is a very real weariness. The prophet in 40:1 had also counted twenty-five years in a foreign land. Habit is often there to protect ourselves, so that we do not keep hurting. But God did not blame him for the habit; God simply brought him back. You can do this: do not decide yet whether to move back, but first do one small thing, for example ask yourself every day one sentence: what do I miss most? Tell the answer to God. Then find someone you trust and say it: I miss home, only I no longer dare to say it. If this habit has already affected your sleep or appetite, or brings thoughts of harming yourself, please find a pastor or a professional counselor as soon as you can. You do not have to carry it alone.
@@18340@@
VII. Living Out 321: See First, Hear First, and Only Then Speak
@@18341@@
Jesus is my Role Model. John 5:19 says: “the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing.” He first saw, first heard, and only then acted. The order of 40:4 was lived out in Jesus. Those who follow him learn his pattern: they do not grab the word, do not grab the position, but first see what the Father is doing.
@@18342@@
The Bible is my Standard. When we read chapter 40, it is not to solve the puzzle of the temple’s measurements, but to let the text finish speaking for itself: he brings us (40:1–2), he has measured (40:5–37), he has prepared rooms and names (40:44–46). If the way we teach it makes people more anxious and more calculating, then it is not this chapter. If it makes people more willing to draw near to him, then it is in line with the text.
@@18343@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. Verse 1 says: Yahweh’s hand was on me, and he brought me to the land of Israel. The prophet did not walk there himself; the Spirit took him. Every day we can say quietly: Lord, today let your Spirit lead me; I will see first, I will hear first.
@@18344@@
Let Jesus be King means handing over the “ruler” in our hands (40:3). We are used to measuring others by our own standards, measuring God’s work, and being quick to explain every measurement for God. Those who let Jesus be King know that the one who measures is him, and my work is to follow, to set on my heart what he says.
@@18345@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory means not rushing to say: this is what I saw. The prophet says “like”; he leaves the glory to the one who measures. If we have received something, we should not say “I see more clearly than others,” but say: all the glory goes to the Lord who brought me here and showed me.
@@18346@@
To build God’s system is to be a community that has walls, gates, courts, and also rooms and names (40:5, 44–46). What God prepares is an ordered presence: each person has a place, and there is no favoritism. Life first, we are brought back by him; then relationships, with rooms and names for one another; and ministry last, we open our mouths to speak. A fellowship like this is a small piece of God’s system on earth: measure the gate first, then talk about service.
@@18347@@
What does “let Jesus be King” have to do with “handing over the ruler”?
@@18348@@
We often say “Jesus is Lord,” but when something happens, our first reaction is to pick up our own ruler and measure: is this person qualified, is this worth it, is this decision right? Verse 3 says the one holding the reed is the man standing at the gate, not the prophet, and not me. To let Jesus be King is to admit that the one who measures is him. You can practice like this: every morning, write on a piece of paper the one person or matter you most want to measure, and then say to him: this one I will not measure for now; I will see first, I will hear first. After a while you will find that when you no longer measure people with a ruler, your heart is more at peace, and also has more love.
@@18349@@
The proud type says: “Let me say my view first; I understand better.” The text says to him: all that I show you, see with your eyes, hear with your ears, and set it on your heart. Pride is leaving a person’s position and grabbing God’s power, wanting to do with our own hands even the measuring of God’s work. To return to one’s position is to admit: the one who measures is him, and I am the one who was brought here. First say one sentence to God: “Lord, I will see first, I will hear first.”
@@18350@@
The fearful type says: “I cannot understand so many measurements; am I not spiritual enough?” The text lets him see that the prophet himself said only “like,” and that God, speaking to the exiles, did not require them to finish explaining the vision first. The fearful type should learn to change “I am afraid I cannot understand” into “I will look at one small passage first.” You can begin with one verse, and not ask for all of it.
@@18351@@
The ashamed type says: “I have been away from God’s house too long; I do not deserve to go in again.” Every gate in chapter 40 has been measured, with steps and porches; the road is prepared by God, not won by you. Shame says, “you have been left outside,” and the gospel says, “I brought you here.” The ashamed do not need to get themselves in order first; they only need to step onto the first stair.
@@18352@@
The wounded type says: “I was hurt in church; this temple has nothing to do with me.” You do not need to go back right away. The gates and rooms of this chapter are saying: God has prepared a place, and he has also prepared a name. A wounded person can slowly find someone who truly loves you and say what you are willing to say. The one who accompanies should not rush to say “you must forgive,” but only say: “I am here; I am with you.”
@@18353@@
First, spend five minutes each day reading only one short passage of Scripture: see with your eyes, hear with your ears, choose one sentence to set on your heart, and think it over once more before sleep. Do not tell anyone yet, and write down the date.
@@18354@@
Second, write down an occasion where you often speak up first, and before you walk in the next time, pray silently: “Lord, I will see first, I will hear first.” Say which meeting it is, and who you will sit with.
@@18355@@
Third, choose a family member or friend you have not been in touch with for a long time, and this week make a phone call or send a message with only one sentence: “I thought of you.” Say his name and the time.
@@18356@@
Fourth, each time you pass through a door (the door of your home or your office), pause for one second before going in and think: this is a road that has been measured. Write down which door it is.
@@18357@@
Fifth, before you prepare to lead or share, first set the passage on your own heart once, and then decide what to say; say which meeting you are going to lead.
@@18358@@
I am usually very busy and have no time to be quiet. How do I start “see first, hear first”?
@@18359@@
You do not need an hour first, and you do not need a very quiet room. You can start with five minutes, tied to something you do every day, such as while the water boils, when you get into the car, or before washing the dishes. In those five minutes, read only one short passage and set only one sentence on your heart. If you forget one day, do not blame yourself; come back the next day. You can also find a companion and ask each other one sentence: what is the sentence you set on your heart today? With a companion there is a reminder, and with a reminder it is not easy to stop halfway. To see first and hear first is not an extra assignment; it is a place from which the rest of your day can be set down.

Cherubim and Palm Trees
@@18392@@

<p><strong>What is carved on the wall is not scenery; it is the gate of the garden of Eden.</strong></p><p>The whole temple, from the ground to above the door, is carved all over with cherubim and palm trees. <p><strong>First called “altar,” then called “table.”</strong></p><p>This verse is very short, yet it has a very quiet turn: it first says “altar,” and at the end the man says: “This is the table that is before Yahweh.”</p><p>The altar is the place of offering, where people hand things over. The table is the place of sitting down to eat, where people are hosted. The same thing, two names. Offering and sharing a table are placed in the same piece of furniture.</p><p>And it is made of wood. Its corners, its surface, its sides are all wood. Wood is a material that decays, yet it is placed before the most holy place. God did not ask for the most costly material; what he asked for is this position: “before Yahweh.”</p><p>This gives us a gentle hint: in our relationship with God there is “offering,” and there is also “sharing a table.” If only the former is left, it becomes a debt; if only the latter is left, it becomes casual. He placed them together.</p><p><strong>Landing:</strong> Today, what will you offer to God, and what will you sit down before him to receive? Do both.</p>

<p><strong>Even on the doors, Eden is carved.</strong></p><p>The last part speaks of the doors. The temple and the most holy place each have doors of two leaves; each leaf is again two leaves, which can fold and also turn. A door like this is not pushed all the way at once; it is opened layer by layer.</p><p>What is more special is that the doors are also carved with cherubim and palm trees, the same as on the wall. That is to say, wherever you look from, you see the same picture: the tree of life, and those who guard the tree of life.</p><p>This chapter begins with measuring the wall and ends with carving the door. All the way down, the prophet is led, from the temple to the inner temple, from the side rooms to the wall, one thing after another. The last arrangement is very simple: you reach the door, put out your hand and push it, and what your fingertips touch is the tree and the cherubim.</p><p>This is a temple that does not yet have the glory, yet everywhere it has the marks of Eden. When the home is prepared, it waits for him to come in.</p><p><strong>Landing:</strong> What is carved on the door of your home, on the door of your heart, right now? Today, before your hand pushes open a door, pause a moment, and pray for what is on the other side of the door.</p>
