I. Another’s Gate Is Broken, and I Will Be Replenished (26:1–6)
@@11527@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. At the mouth of the lane there are two breakfast shops, selling much the same things, one on the left and one on the right, and since they were young the two owners have been competing over whose egg pancake smells better. One day, the owner of the shop on the left collapsed in his shop and was taken to the hospital. The metal shutter came down, with a note stuck on it: closed for now. The wife of the owner of the shop on the right stood at her own door and glanced at that note. The first thought that came up in her heart was not “Is he all right?” but: tomorrow morning’s crowd will be mine. She meant no harm, and she did not say it aloud, and even she was startled. But that thought was real. Today, when we read Ezekiel chapter 26, the text is about this very thought, only the lane has become a coast, and the breakfast shop has become a city called Tyre.
@@11528@@
After chapter 25 finished with four neighboring nations, chapter 26 pulls the camera to the seashore and speaks only to Tyre. Tyre was a merchant city that lived off the sea; fleets, harbors, trade routes were all her capital. Verse 1 records a date: “In the eleventh year, in the first of the month.” We do not need to calculate anything from this date; we only need to notice one thing: God’s word came on a recorded day; it was not a sentence floating in the air.
@@11529@@
Verse 2 is the word of Tyre: “Aha! She is broken! She who was the gateway of the peoples has been returned to me. I will be replenished, now that she is laid waste.” Listen: she did not hate Jerusalem, and she did not clap; she only worked out a sum. Jerusalem sat on the inland highway, the “gateway of the peoples,” through which goods and travelers passed. If the gate was broken, the trade routes would turn toward her. Another’s ruin, in her eyes, was a seat left empty in her own warehouse.
@@11530@@
The words “I will be replenished” are the sharpest. They are not a fact but a calculation, an abacus clicking in her heart. She had not yet obtained anything; she only hoped, hoped that another’s collapse would fill the gap for her. Please notice that this verse does not say she pulled down anyone’s wall with her own hands. She only laughed once, and treated another’s pain as her own opportunity.
@@11531@@
God’s answer begins at verse 3: “Behold, I am against you, Tyre, and will cause many nations to come up against you, as the sea causes its waves to come up.” God first stands before her, and only then begins to say the rest. She had calculated other cities, other fleets, other trade routes; the only one she had not calculated was this One. The sea was originally her home, her wall, her source of wealth; now even the voice of the sea has become the voice of judgment: what she relied on has been sent to deal with her.
@@11532@@
The picture in verses 4 and 5 is very quiet. The walls are torn down, the towers broken, even the dust is scraped away, and she becomes a “bare rock”; she will be “a place for the spreading of nets in the middle of the sea.” No fire, no thunder, only fishermen spreading out a few nets under the sun. A city once full of sails ends up useful for drying nets.
@@11533@@
Tyre was only “thinking”; she did not actually do anything. Why does that count as sin too?
@@11534@@
The “I will be replenished” of verse 2 is in the original a kind of wish, the abacus in her heart, not something already in her hand. What the text rebukes is not what she did, but that in her heart she took another’s falling as a vacancy for herself to fill. The thought is small, but it is a root; if the root is there, branches will sooner or later grow. This is not asking us to panic over every thought that flashes by, but to learn, at the moment it arises, to bring it before God: Lord, I have this calculation in my heart. God can bear that kind of honesty. You do not have to wait until the thought becomes an action before you come to him; the sooner you say it aloud, the easier it is to put down.
@@11535@@
II. Nebuchadnezzar: The One Who Is Brought (26:7–14)
@@11536@@
From verse 7 the wave has a name: “Behold, I will bring on Tyre Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, king of kings, from the north, with horses, with chariots, with horsemen, and an army with many people.” Please look at that verb: “I will bring,” and the subject is Yahweh. Nebuchadnezzar thought he was the one who sent out the army; the text says he is the one who is brought. Even the title “king of kings” is spoken within God’s word: however great he is, he is under sending.
@@11537@@
Next, the camera moves closer step by step: forts, mound, shields, battering engines, axes, so many horses that the dust rises and covers the city, hoofs treading down every street. Every detail is clear, as if someone were standing on the wall watching it taken apart inch by inch. Verse 10 says he enters the gates as men enter a city which is broken open, meaning that this city which looked the strongest already had cracks before them.
@@11538@@
From verse 12, the subject changes from “he” to a nameless “they”: plundering riches, taking merchandise, breaking down pleasant houses, and in the end laying the stones, timber and dust in the middle of the waters. This city that rose by the sea has even its materials given back to the sea. Some read the “many nations” and the “one king” as two things, and some read them as the beginning and continuation of one thing: Nebuchadnezzar is the first wave, and afterward there are many waves, like the sea’s waves one after another. Interpreters understand it differently; we do not need to make any one of these a condition of faith, much less set a timetable. You only need to hold on to this: the word is God’s, and when he has spoken, it counts.
@@11539@@
What cuts deepest is verse 13: “I will cause the noise of your songs to cease. The sound of your harps won’t be heard any more.” Tyre was not only a city of business but a lively city, with singing in the streets, harps, and days that went well. Judgment does not pass over this detail: it is not only that the walls fall, but that the sound of “days that go well” can no longer be heard. Liveliness is not the same as stability.
@@11540@@
The ending of verse 14: “You will be built no more; for I Yahweh have spoken it.” Some read this sentence as saying that this place will never again be inhabited; others read it as a picture of judgment aimed at that city which rose by the sea and thought itself a god, not at a piece of land on the map. These notes take the latter: what is taken away is her form. The weight of this sentence is not on Tyre but on the One who speaks.
@@11541@@
Since Nebuchadnezzar is God’s instrument, does that mean everything he did was right?
@@11542@@
No. The text speaks of God’s sovereignty being greater than any king; even the “king of kings” is under sending. It does not say that his every deed is good. This is not a defense of any violence, and certainly not saying to victims, “you deserved it.” If you have been oppressed or harmed by power, please do not use this chapter to persuade yourself that “God arranged for me to suffer.” God sees, and he is not pleased that you were hurt. What this verse wants us to see is his sovereignty, not that human violence is justified. You may honestly tell God your pain, and you may also seek proper protection and help; you do not have to carry it alone.
@@11543@@
I worry about how exactly “many nations coming up” was fulfilled. Do I need to understand the history first before I can believe?
@@11544@@
No. Bible scholars understand this question differently: some stress Nebuchadnezzar’s one campaign, some stress the history that came later, wave after wave, and some remind us that the text uses picture language, and not every detail needs to be locked onto one battle. These notes set the views side by side, do not set a timetable, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. The point is: God’s word will be fulfilled, and a wall is not the final thing to rely on. However you read it, if this chapter leads you to trust him more and love him more, that is enough. You may set aside the places you cannot understand, and first hold on to what you can: he is the Lord, his word can be trusted, and he is glad for you to return to him.
@@11545@@
III. Light from the Original: I Will Be Replenished, I Am Against You, Pit (26:2, 3, 20)
@@11546@@
This chapter has three words in the original that are worth a close look. The first is “I will be replenished” in verse 2, the Hebrew אִמָּלְאָה (immale’ah). The ending of this word is a wish form, “let me …” So the original sounds more than the CUV like a sentence inside the heart: let me be filled. The verb is also passive, which means: I can be filled only by another’s vacancy. This word gives us a ruler: the next time “this way I will …” comes up in your heart, look at whether what follows that sentence depends on someone else’s gap.
@@11547@@
The second is “I am against you” in verse 3, the Hebrew הִנְנִי עָלַיִךְ (hinneni ‘alayikh), literally “Behold, I, against you.” This is a challenge formula, in which God himself turns and stands before his opponent; Ezekiel uses it especially often when speaking to the nations. You can turn it into a very practical question: in what I am doing and saying now, which side is God on? Once this question is asked, many calculations in the heart will stop.
@@11548@@
The third is the “Sheol” in verse 20, the Hebrew בּוֹר (bor), which in the original is “pit,” a place from which no one turns back. The CUV took the sense and translated it “Sheol.” Tyre will dwell with “the people of old time,” a crowd who long ago lost their names. A city with the greatest name ends up sitting among people with no names. This word reminds us: being remembered by people is not the purpose of life.
@@11549@@
Put the three words together: “I will be replenished” is the calculation in her heart; “I am against you” is God’s answer; “pit” is where the calculation ends up. But please do not forget that after these three words there is still one more sentence: “I will set glory in the land of the living.” Judgment is not the last sentence of the story.
@@11550@@
Can I use “I am against you” to describe God’s attitude toward me?
@@11551@@
Please be careful. This sentence is said by the righteous God to a city that was proud, leaned on its walls, and treated others’ pain as an opportunity; it is not said to the weak, the fallen, or those who are turning back. He has no pleasure in the death of anyone; he is glad when people turn and live (Ezek 18:23, 32). If you are weak and have failed, please do not use this sentence to condemn yourself. You can ask yourself this: am I gripping a wall and refusing to let go, or in my weakness am I willing to return to him? To the former, God will deal with it himself; to the latter, he is waiting for you. The final purpose of this sentence is to make people know him, not to make people fear him.
@@11552@@
IV. The Trembling of the Islands, and Glory in the Land of the Living (26:15–21)
@@11553@@
The sound of Tyre’s fall reached the islands. The islands were her customers, her harbors, her backers. The picture in verse 16 is very concrete: the princes of the sea come down from their thrones, lay aside their robes, strip off their embroidered garments, clothe themselves with trembling, and sit on the ground, trembling every moment. Notice the act of changing clothes: what they take off is the prince’s outer robe, what they put on is “trembling”; terror has become their new uniform.
@@11554@@
Are they really weeping for Tyre? Verse 17 is the lamentation they sing: “How you are destroyed, who were inhabited by seafaring men, the renowned city, who was strong in the sea, she and her inhabitants, who caused their terror to be on all who lived there!” This is the résumé of her whole life. In ordinary days Tyre made people fear her by being strong; when a strong thing falls, the people it sheltered feel the ground under their own feet shake. So verse 18 says the islands tremble and are dismayed. Some sorrow is, in name, for someone else, but what it has touched is really our own fear.
@@11555@@
There is one more thing in this lament that is worth a pause: they asked only “how,” and did not ask “why.” The next time you hear that something big has fallen—a company, a system you thought was stable, a person’s health—do not hurry to ask “how could it be”; you can also ask: Lord, what do you want me to see in this? To ask this is not to find someone to blame, but slowly to lead the heart from panic back to trust.
@@11556@@
In verses 19 and 20, the camera sinks into the water: “the deep on you, and the great waters cover you.” Tyre lived by the sea all her life, and now the sea becomes her grave. Then comes the pit, the place desolate of old. This is a picture for a proud city; it is not passing judgment on anyone’s death, much less explaining your family member’s passing. If you are bereaved, please do not take these verses home to think about alone. What is said here is not about your loved one, and you do not need to look for an answer here.
@@11557@@
But at the end of verse 20 there is a sentence that is different: “I will set glory in the land of the living.” The meaning of the original is that God sets his beauty in the land of the living; some ancient versions read this line as a negative, and interpreters read it differently, but the direction is clear: the eyes move from Tyre to God himself. At the same time that Tyre goes down to the pit, his glory will be set among his people. Verse 21 closes: “Though you are sought for, yet you will never be found again.” Being sought is not the purpose of life; being known by him is.
@@11558@@
I am losing something right now: work, savings, or health. Is this chapter saying that I “leaned on the wrong thing”?
@@11559@@
No. What this chapter accuses is the heart that treats another’s pain as an opportunity and takes a wall for God, not the person who is losing something. That you are suffering does not mean you are Tyre. If you are unemployed, bankrupt or ill, please do not take this chapter back with you to condemn yourself. What you can do is tell God, one by one, the things you are losing, and find someone trustworthy to walk with you for a while. When necessary, also seek professional help: finance, medical care, counseling are all help that God gives. His glory in the land of the living includes the road you are walking now.
@@11560@@
The first deviation is to preach the “many nations coming up” as a timetable predicting some modern nation or event, or to make one reading of the fulfillment a condition of faith. These notes do not set timetables and do not name modern nations; the readings of the different interpreters are set side by side, and the point is that God’s word will be fulfilled. We want to make the congregation trust God more, not panic more.
@@11561@@
The second deviation is to preach “I am against you” as God threatening every weak person. This sentence is spoken to a proud city that leaned on its walls, not to those who have fallen and are turning back. We want to make the weak long to return to him, not fear more.
