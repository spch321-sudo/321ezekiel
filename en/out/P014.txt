@@4657@@
⑤ Teaching “those who stayed in the city” and “those taken captive” as if one group were more spiritual than the other
The inhabitants said, “Go far away from Yahweh,” but God says it was he who removed them far off, and that he himself will be a sanctuary to them there.
Test: Does the message avoid putting the label “far from God” on any group of people?
@@4658@@
⑥ Teaching the “sanctuary for a little while” of 11:16 as “from now on we need not worship together”
The sanctuary goes with the people, but that does not cancel worshiping together with our companions. That he himself is the sanctuary means closer, not looser.
Test: After hearing it, is the congregation more eager to gather and worship with their brothers and sisters, or more distant?
@@4659@@
⑦ Teaching “the glory stood on the mountain to the east” as a forecast of some modern place or event
This chapter names no modern place and sets no timetable.
Test: Does the message contain any present-day place name, country, or date?
@@4660@@
⑧ Turning the stony heart and the heart of flesh into a personality verdict: “You are just too hard”
The stony heart and the heart of flesh are God’s work, not a verdict on a person’s character. Sorrow and numbness are sometimes the protection of a wound, not stubbornness.
Test: After hearing it, can a wounded person still say that they feel numb without being labeled?
@@4661@@
⑨ Using “selflessness” to ask people to let go of the fact that they were hurt
Selflessness is seeing him and then placing our peace back in his hands. It is not telling the victim to swallow injustice, and it is not demanding forgiveness.
Test: Does the speaker first protect people, not press for details, and not demand forgiveness?
@@4662@@
⑩ Using Hebrew (מַעֲלוֹת, יָעַץ and so on) to show off learning
The original language is only for opening up the text. If it cannot be turned into a way of living today within thirty seconds, leave it in the study.
Test: After listening, can the congregation name one thing they will do today?
@@4663@@
⑪ Turning “grace comes first, cleansing follows” into “we may leave sin alone for now”
Cleansing is the response after being gathered (11:18), and verse 21 also says that a heart which clings to idols will bear the consequences.
Test: Does the message have both grace (he gives first) and response (people remove)?
@@4664@@
⑫ Turning this chapter into a horror film and creating panic
The fruit should be endurance and a deeper love for the Lord: between the judgments there is the sentence “I will give them a heart of flesh”; the glory leaves the city, yet it stops on the mountain.
Test: When the congregation walks out, are they more afraid of the world, or do they love the Lord more?

It is being set in a place without familiar rituals, and he went along too.</p><p>Landing: you may have walked into a place with no familiar church, no familiar home, no familiar health. <p><strong>Something worth noticing: the verb form turns a corner here.</strong></p><p>Verse 16 is the wayyiqtol וָאֱהִי, “I (already) am”; verse 17 turns to וְקִבַּצְתִּי, וַאֲסַפְתִּי, וְנָתַתִּי, a run of perfect consecutives, “I will gather, I will assemble, I will give.” Setting the tenses of the two verses side by side shows more clearly that verse 16 speaks of the present and verse 17 of the future.</p>
@@4688@@
<p><strong>God’s promise first brings people home, and only then asks them to clean the house.</strong></p><p>He says: I will gather you from the peoples, I will give you the land. These are all his actions. The one thing people are to do is in verse 18: take away all the detestable and abominable things. They will come “there,” to the given land, and only then set about removing. The land is a gift, and also a responsibility.</p><p>The order is this: grace comes first, cleansing follows. People were not gathered after first cleaning out the idols; they were gathered and given the land, and then had the strength to carry the idols out.</p><p>There is one detail to remember. “Take away,” הֵסִירוּ, has the people as its subject in this verse. In the next verse (19) the same verb has God as its subject: “I will take the stony heart out of their flesh.” The idols outside, people must handle with their hands; the stony heart inside, only he can remove.</p><p>Landing: we often think we must be completely clean before we deserve to be gathered. The order in the text is the reverse: first received back, then cleansed. You can begin right now with one thing you know should be carried away, but do not treat it as a ticket to get in.</p>

<p><strong>God did not say, “You must change your heart.” He said: I will give.</strong></p><p>This is the first promise of a new heart and a new spirit, and it appears in the very chapter where the glory is about to leave the city, between the judgment and the departure. <p><strong>The glory was not driven out. I. The Twenty-Five Men at the East Gate (11:1–4)
@@5005@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. When you were little, did you ever play hide-and-seek? There is a kind of child who is especially adorable: he burrows his head into the blanket, with his bottom and both feet sticking out, and shouts, “You can’t find me!” He truly believes he is well hidden. Why? Because he cannot see outside, so he thinks outside cannot see him. In today’s chapter, twenty-five grown men are doing the same thing, except that the blanket they hide their heads in is not a quilt but a city.
@@5006@@
First, see where the prophet is. Verse 1 says, the Spirit lifted me up and brought me to the east gate of Yahweh’s house. The subject is the Spirit; the prophet is only the one being carried along. In the last chapter the glory had already come to this gate and stopped there (10:19); today the Spirit sets the prophet in the same place. You can think of it this way: God put him beside the gate the glory is about to leave, so that he could see with his own eyes who was still sitting at the gate, and with what thoughts they were guarding this city.
@@5007@@
At the gate are twenty-five men, and two of them are named: Jaazaniah and Pelatiah. Names speak. Jaazaniah means Yahweh hears, and Pelatiah means Yahweh causes escape. Just think how much hope and thanksgiving their parents had when they gave their children these two names. Now the two men sit in the seats of the council, their names saying he hears and he rescues, and at the council table not a word has asked him. A name is faith given by parents, but a person can live out its opposite.
@@5008@@
In verse 2, Yahweh says, these are the men who devise iniquity and give wicked counsel in this city. Please notice that devising is not idle talk by the roadside; it is sitting down to deliberate. In verse 3, God reads out their words: “The time is not near to build houses. This is the cauldron, and we are the meat.” The meaning: the captives were thrown out, but we are still in the city; the meat is in the cauldron, the cauldron protects it, and nothing will happen. The most dangerous thing about this sentence is that it sounds very much like faith. The walls are there, the temple is there, our position is there, so we feel at ease. Yet in that whole sentence there is not one word that mentions God. In verse 4, God’s response is short: son of man, prophesy against them. There are some words that cannot be allowed to go on any longer.
@@5009@@
I have a cauldron of my own. Will God ask me to give up my work, my identity, and all my seniority?
@@5010@@
No. God did not say the cauldron is a bad thing. What he cares about is people putting the cauldron in his place. Work, family, the identity of serving, years of experience can all be good gifts from God, and you can use them well. There is only one question to check: if this thing were taken away tomorrow, what peace would I have left? If the answer is nothing at all, then it has quietly taken a seat on God’s throne. There is no need to rush to throw it away. First say it out loud before him: this is my cauldron. Seeing it is the first step in moving it back to its place, and the most honest one.
@@5011@@
II. God Takes Up Their Metaphor (11:5–12)
@@5012@@
In verse 5, Yahweh’s Spirit fell on the prophet, and as soon as God began to speak he said: what you have said, and what comes into your mind, I know. The word for what comes into your mind is, literally, what goes up, that is, thoughts. Thoughts are like steam rising from a cauldron: no one calls them, they rise by themselves, and you cannot hold them down. God says: this, I know too. This sentence has two sides. To those hiding evil schemes it is a warning; to a person ashamed of his own thoughts it is the comfort of being fully known.
@@5013@@
Thoughts do not stay in the heart forever. Verse 6 says, you have multiplied your slain in this city and filled its streets with the slain. A sentence at the council table can walk into the street and become a person who has fallen. We need not paint the picture any larger. We only need to remember that God looks not only at the result; he begins to look from the thought.
@@5014@@
Verse 7 is the best sentence of this section. They said, this is the cauldron, and we are the meat. God did not deny this metaphor; he took it up and changed it: “Your slain whom you have laid in the middle of it, they are the meat, and this is the cauldron; but you will be brought out of the middle of it.” The cauldron is still the cauldron, but the meat is not you. The meat in the cauldron is the people you have harmed; as for you, I will bring you out of the place you thought most secure. The verb “bring out” is causative: it is not that you leave on your own; it is that I bring you away.
@@5015@@
Verse 8 is heavier: “You have feared the sword; and I will bring the sword on you.” The very thing you fear most is often the very thing you use to prove you are safe. They feared the sword, so they built walls, made alliances, and treated the city as a charm; they did not fear God, so they put their peace in the wrong place. In verses 9 to 11, God says he will deliver them into the hands of strangers and execute judgments among them in the border of Israel, and verse 11 says it once more: “This will not be your cauldron, neither will you be the meat in the middle of it.”
@@5016@@
Verse 12 gives the reason, and says it very plainly: you have not walked in my statutes, nor executed my ordinances, but have done after the ordinances of the nations around you. It is not that they had no religion. They had a temple, a gate, sacrifices; only from Monday to Saturday they lived by the rules of those around them. The most dangerous form of faith is not unbelief; it is believing, yet treating God as a charm you can keep in your pocket.
@@5017@@
God says, “You have feared the sword; and I will bring the sword on you.” Does that mean whatever I fear most will happen?
@@5018@@
No. This sentence was spoken to leaders who refused to turn back; it is not a law that whatever you fear comes. You can be at ease: your fear of illness, unemployment, or loneliness is not a handle God uses against you. What this verse wants us to see is that they built their peace on walls, alliances, and position, not on God, so God removed this false refuge. If you have many fears in your heart, you can tell him honestly and ask him to teach you to place your fear in his hands. If anxiety is already affecting your sleep and your life, please also talk with a pastor or a medical professional.
@@5019@@
III. The Prophet on His Face, God Says “I Will Give” (11:13–25)
@@5020@@
While the prophet was still prophesying, verse 13 records one thing in a single sentence: Pelatiah the son of Benaiah died. The text does not say how he died, nor explain why, and we will not explain it for the text, nor paint a picture, nor guess a cause. We only look at what the prophet did. He fell on his face and cried out loudly: “Ah Lord Yahweh! Will you make a full end of the remnant of Israel?” He did not defend God, and he did not say it was his own business. His whole self fell down, and what he asked was not “Did I say it right?” but “What about the ones who are left?” One who preaches hard words is not cold; he preaches with tears.
@@5021@@
If someone near you has just passed away, you may stop right here, where the prophet lay on the ground. Grief is allowed. You need not hurry to find a cause, and you need not demand that you be strong. Please notice the order of the text: the prophet fell first, and in verse 14 Yahweh’s word then came. God did not rebuke his weeping; he carried the word on. When people are at their softest, God’s word is often clearest.
@@5022@@
Someone close to me has passed away. Reading about Pelatiah’s death makes me very sad. What can I do?
@@5023@@
You may choose not to read it, or read only as far as the prophet on his face and stop there. The text uses only one sentence for Pelatiah’s death and gives you no answer you need to work out, so you do not need to hand in homework for this verse. Borrow the prophet’s posture: fall down before God and say your questions just as they are, without polishing them and without defending anyone. Then find someone you trust, and let him simply sit with you for a while, with nothing needed to be said. If the sorrow is so heavy that it affects eating, sleeping, or daily life, please let a pastor and a professional counselor walk with you together for a while.
@@5024@@
In verse 15, God quotes a sentence from the inhabitants of Jerusalem. They said to their fellow captives: “Go far away from Yahweh. This land has been given to us for a possession.” The meaning: you were moved away, you are far from God; we are still standing on this land, so God is on our side. They turned God’s gift into a reason for shutting others out. In verse 16, God’s answer is unexpected: whereas I have removed all the house of Israel far off among the nations, I will be to them a sanctuary for a little while in the countries where they have come. The inhabitants said they had gone far; God said it was I who put them far away, and I myself will go to be their sanctuary. Not in the temple, not in the city, but in the very place they were put.
@@5025@@
In verses 17 and 18, all the verbs are his: I will gather, I will assemble, I will give. There is only one thing for people to do, to take away the detestable and abominable things, and it is after they have come back there. See the order clearly: grace comes first, cleansing follows. It is not that I must first wash myself clean before God will want me. If it were so, we would never see that day.
@@5026@@
Then we come to verse 19: “I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within you. I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them a heart of flesh.” Please read slowly: give, put, take away, give: four verbs, and the subject of each is he. This is the first promise of a new spirit and a heart of flesh, and it appears in this very chapter where the glory is about to leave the city. Verse 20 states the purpose: that they may walk in my statutes, and keep my ordinances, and do them. “They will be my people, and I will be their God.” The new heart comes first, then obedience; it is not that we are obedient enough and so deserve a new heart.
@@5027@@
In verses 22 and 23, the scene turns. The cherubim lifted up their wings, the wheels were beside them, and the glory of the God of Israel was over them; Yahweh’s glory went up from the middle of the city and stood on the mountain on the east side of the city. The glory left step by step; it did not vanish all at once. The last step: standing on the mountain, facing the city. He has gone, but he has not gone far. In verses 24 and 25, the Spirit brought the prophet to the captives, the vision went up from him, and he spoke to the captives all the things Yahweh had shown him. The words of this chapter were first placed in the hands of those who had been thrown outside.
