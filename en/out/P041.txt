I. A Tree and the Water That Nourishes It (31:1–9)
@@13858@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever had a day when the water was cut off? You get up in the morning, turn on the tap, and nothing comes out. You stand at the sink for a moment, stunned, and only then realize: every day I brush my teeth, cook, and flush the toilet, and I have never once thought about where the water comes from. The water was always there, so we assumed it was simply there. On the day the water is cut off, for the first time we think of the waterworks, the pipes, the reservoir far away, and we remember that none of this is ours. Today’s passage speaks of a tree that forgot where its water came from.
@@13859@@
First, look at the time. Verse 1 says that in the eleventh year, in the third month, on the first day of the month, Yahweh’s word came to the prophet. It was addressed to Pharaoh king of Egypt and his multitude. God asked a question: “Whom are you like in your greatness?” Please notice that God did not say, “You are not great,” and he did not condemn at the first word. He said: come, let us compare. Pharaoh sat by the Nile with the great power of his nation in his hand, and perhaps he thought: who can match me? So God said: very well, I will show you one greater than you.
@@13860@@
In verse 3, what God brings out is Assyria: “Behold, the Assyrian was a cedar in Lebanon with beautiful branches, and with a forest-like shade, of high stature; and its top was among the thick boughs.” Cedar was the most precious timber of the time, and both the temple and the palaces used it. When the prophet opens his mouth, it is not to scold, but to praise: beautiful, shady, tall. It is a picture painted with real admiration. But next comes a sentence that is the center of the whole passage: “The waters nourished it. The deep made it to grow.” Look at each verb: growing was done by the water; becoming great was done by the water. The tree simply stood there, sent its roots down, and received. Verses 4 to 7 say again and again that rivers ran all around it, channels reached the fields, and its roots were by many waters. Every inch of this tree’s height has a source.
@@13861@@
In verses 6 to 9 the picture becomes warmer. Birds make their nests in its boughs, animals give birth under its branches, and “all great nations lived under its shadow.” A strong nation is like a great tree that shelters many people from wind and rain, and this is a real good. In verse 8, God lines up the cedars, the cypresses, and the pines of the garden of God one by one, and says that none could compare. The subject of verse 9 is very important: “I made it beautiful by the multitude of its branches, so that all the trees of Eden, that were in the garden of God, envied it.” The subject is “I,” and that is God. Imagine a manager who has led a team for more than ten years. One day he says to an old friend: I came to see it later. It was not that I was good at leading people; it was that a group of people were willing to let me lead. What he said is just this verse. Your height has water too. Your health is water, your mind is water, and the people you have met are water.
@@13862@@
Is being tall, successful, and influential a sin in itself? Should I try not to stand out too much?
@@13863@@
No. This chapter first spends a full nine verses praising the tree, and God himself says: “I made it beautiful by the multitude of its branches.” This means that being tall is itself a gift, and that growth and service are things we may pursue. The real problem does not appear until verse 10, and it is “his heart is lifted up.” Please distinguish two ways of reading. The first is “do not be too tall, or God will strike you down,” which makes people want to grow but dare not, and live timidly. The second is “you may be tall, but your roots must be beside the water,” and this is what fits the text. Some people go to the opposite extreme and push away every compliment, saying, “Oh no, not at all.” In fact, honest thanks is closer to humility than false modesty. When someone praises you, you can say in your heart: this is from God. Then go on and do the work well.

II. A Heart Higher Than the Treetop (31:10–14)
@@13865@@
Verse 10 has a turn: “Because you are exalted in stature, and he has set his top among the thick boughs, and his heart is lifted up in his height.” All along it has been speaking of “height,” and suddenly it turns to the “heart.” It is the same word, high; said of the tree, it is height on the outside; said of the heart, it is height on the inside. From the outside it looks exactly the same: the leaves are still green, the branches are still thick, and the birds still come to nest. But inside, the water no longer flows. Please notice that the text does not blame the tree for growing too tall. What it blames is that the position in the heart is higher than the position under the feet. You sit in one position, and your heart sits in a higher one. That gap is pride.
@@13866@@
What is pride? It is leaving man’s position, seizing God’s power, and wanting to take God’s place. The roots of this tree were originally beside the water, and at the moment it began to say in its heart, “I grew this tall by myself,” the roots had left the water. You cannot see this from the outside. Every one of us has seen something like it: after a person takes a seat of authority, his tone of voice slowly changes, and his patience in listening slowly changes. When others say, “This won’t work,” he says, “What do you know?” He is not necessarily a bad person; he has only slowly forgotten the water. The position has not changed, but the heart moved out first.
@@13867@@
Verses 11 and 12 are heavy: God delivers it into the hand of the mighty one of the nations, and the foreigners cut it off and cast it away. Please do not make this passage light, and do not push it too far. The text does not describe in detail the scene of that day; it leaves only a result: “His branches have fallen on the mountains and in all the valleys.” Then comes a very bitter sentence: “All the peoples of the earth have gone down from his shadow, and have left him.” In the time of glory, everyone gathered around it; when it fell, they walked away one by one. The guests under the shade came for the shade, not for the tree. If you are standing in a high place right now, this sentence is not meant to frighten you, but to give you a practical reminder: while you can, find a few people who are not under your shade, who are not afraid of you and are not asking anything from you, and who will tell you the truth. These few people are worth more than a hundred rounds of applause.
@@13868@@
Verse 14 is the key to this section: God lets a tree fall “to the end that none of all the trees by the waters exalt themselves in their stature, and don’t set their top among the thick boughs.” The one that fell is one tree, but the ones reminded are all the trees beside it. So another person’s fall is for us to look at, not for us to judge. We so easily say, the moment we see someone fall, “He was just too proud.” But the text asks a different question: you, the other trees beside the water, what about you? Today, is there one thing in which your heart is higher than your position? Do not look for other people’s answers first; look for your own.
@@13869@@
The text says God handed this tree over to the “tyrant” nations to be cut off. Does this mean that everything those people did was right?
@@13870@@
No. This is the place in verses 11 and 12 most easily misunderstood. The text holds two things true at the same time. First, God rules history, and even a strong nation is in his hand; no strong nation can decide its own direction. Second, the text calls these people “the tyrants of the nations,” which means that violence does not become righteousness just because God uses it. God can use unrighteous instruments without approving of the instruments. The later prophets also say that these instruments themselves must face judgment. This is very practical for us: if you were treated unjustly, you do not need to hurry to say, “This is God’s will,” and you do not need to use “God used him” to force yourself to forgive. God’s sovereignty is real, and that person’s conduct must still be answered for before him. You can lay down revenge, because judgment does not belong to us.
@@13871@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Be Like, Lifted Heart, Comforted (31:2, 10, 16)
@@13872@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth a close look. The first is “be like,” the Hebrew דָּמָה (damah), found in verse 2 and in verse 18: “Whom are you like?” The whole chapter has only one question, asked once at the beginning and once more at the end, and this is the word used. Its meaning is to resemble, to compare. You see, God is not asking Pharaoh “Are you great or not?” but “Whom do you liken yourself to?” All our lives we compare: as children we compare grades, as adults we compare salaries, and after we believe in the Lord we can still compare service and compare gifts. This word reminds us: every time we compare, we are deciding which position to stand in.
@@13873@@
The second is “heart lifted up,” the Hebrew רָם לְבָבוֹ (ram levavo), verse 10: “his heart is lifted up.” Literally it means “his heart rose high.” The word רָם is often used to describe the height of mountains, trees, and towers; placed with לֵבָב (heart), it means the position in the heart is moved upward. Interestingly, in the Hebrew verse 10 suddenly changes its voice, from “it” to “you,” as if God draws nearer and nearer, and finally speaks directly to the tree. This lets us see that God does not pronounce judgment from far away; he walks up to you and speaks to your heart. The thirty-second exercise is very simple: ask yourself once a day, where has my heart been lifted to today?
@@13874@@
The third is “comforted,” the Hebrew נָחַם (nakham), the form וַיִּנָּחֲמוּ in verse 16. The range of this root is wide: to comfort, to sigh, to regret, to change one’s mind. So interpreters read the sentence in verse 16, “all the trees of Eden … were comforted in the lower parts of the earth,” differently: some say it means seeing that the strong come down in the same way, and gaining a kind of balance; some say it is rest within sorrow; and some read it as a bitter irony. These notes do not treat any one reading as settled. But whichever reading we take, there is one thing we can be sure of: we must not use another person’s fall to comfort ourselves. True comfort is not in others’ failure, but only at the source of the water.
@@13875@@
Put the three words together, and this is the journey of the chapter: God asks us whom we liken ourselves to (damah), the human heart rises upward (ram levavo), and in the end even comfort (nakham) must be learned again: not in comparison, not in another’s fall, but in returning to the water.
@@13876@@
Since “comforted” can be read in different ways, what should I do when I read it? Could I be reading it wrong?
@@13877@@
No. In the Bible there are some sentences where the original language itself allows different possibilities. That is an honest matter, and nothing to fear. You can do this: first, look at all the readings and know how scholars divide. Second, see which one best fits the center of the whole chapter. The center of this chapter is “God mourns for the fallen tree” (verse 15), so any reading that comes out as “how satisfying that others have fallen” does not fit the whole chapter. Third, do not argue about this verse with brothers and sisters; put it on the shelf of “not yet certain” and go on looking at the parts that are clear. What is clear? The height was given by the water; pride makes the roots leave the water; God mourns over this; and the trees beside it are reminded. These four things are enough for us to return to the water today.
@@13878@@
IV. God Mourns for It (31:15–18)
@@13879@@
Verse 15 is the quietest verse in the whole chapter: “In the day when he went down to Sheol I caused a mourning.” Please pause here. On the day of judgment, God is not clapping his hands. He arranged sorrow for this tree: the deep was covered, the rivers were held back, the great waters stopped, and even the forests of Lebanon mourned for it. The water that once made it grow has now stopped. The one who gave is the one who takes back. This is not cruelty; it is order: a created thing cannot take the Creator’s seat. When the seat is wrongly taken, even the water must stop, to remind us. This is not a triumphant execution; it is a solemn funeral.
@@13880@@
Sometimes we think of God as a cold person, waiting to catch our mistakes. But the God of this chapter has tears. He was the one who made this tree grow, and he knows how it could have lived. So please remember: when you think of your own pride, you do not need to be frightened first; first remember the God who mourns for the proud. He does not want you afraid; he wants you to come back. This is the key to all passages of judgment.
@@13881@@
Verse 17 has one more detail: those trees of Eden “who were his arm, who lived under his shadow in the middle of the nations,” now go down with it. One person’s pride does not take away only himself; it carries the people around him along as well. Think about it: who carries the weight of your temper? Who goes along with your insistence? Among the arms around you, is anyone quietly growing tired? This reminder is not to condemn, but to let us see that our heart is never only our own business.
@@13882@@
In verse 18, God asks the first question again: “To whom are you thus like in glory and in greatness among the trees of Eden?” Then the answer comes out: going down to Sheol, and lying down. The tallest tree and the lowest tree end up lying on the same ground. The end of comparison is level ground. So I would like to invite you to ask in a different way. Do not ask again, “Am I higher than he is, or is he higher than I am?” Ask instead: “Are my roots still beside the water?” Jesus said: “Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.” He is the one who came down first. We do not need to compare who is higher; we only need to return to our position.
@@13883@@
Are “Sheol,” “the pit,” and “those slain by the sword” telling us what the world after death looks like? My loved one has passed away. Could I be reading it wrong?
@@13884@@
Please be assured first: this chapter is not speaking of your loved one. These pictures are poetic lament, used to show one thing: however tall the tree, it must lie on the same low ground. The Bible teaches clearly about death and hope in other places, but this passage is not suited to working out any details of life after death, and still less to judging where a deceased person has gone. If you are grieving, please do not blame yourself because of these verses, and do not let anyone use these verses to condemn your loved one. The point of this lesson is not a map of Sheol, but the question God asks: whom are you like? If you have just lost someone, first let yourself cry and let people keep you company; you do not need to hurry to look for answers in this chapter. When you need it, please find a pastor or a professional counselor to walk with you.
@@13885@@
The first deviation is to teach “the cedar” as “do not be too successful, do not be too tall.” The text speaks of a proud heart, not of height itself; God himself says, “I made it beautiful by the multitude of its branches.” We want the congregation to dare to grow, and also to remember where the water comes from, and not to want to grow yet be afraid to.
@@13886@@
The second deviation is to teach the end of Assyria or Pharaoh as a prophecy about some modern nation or some kind of person. The text is a comparison and a reminder spoken to Pharaoh of that time; it is not a list for us to attach labels to. We want the congregation to look back at their own hearts, and not to point at others and say, “That’s the one.”
@@13887@@
The third deviation is to use chapter 31 to judge some fallen person: “He was just too proud.” God mourns for the fallen tree; he is not gloating. We want the congregation to become more compassionate and pray more for others, and not to be quicker to talk about others.
@@13888@@
The fourth deviation is to use the fallen tree to frighten people, so that they dare not stand in a high position or serve. The fruit of this chapter is patience and a deeper love for the Lord, not panic. We want those who listen to give thanks more quietly, and not to be more afraid of God’s punishment.
@@13889@@
The fifth deviation is to teach “Sheol” and “the pit” as a map from which to work out details of death, or to infer where a certain person has gone after death, or to quote “those slain” to the bereaved so that they feel it was their fault. The text is poetic lament imagery. We want to accompany the grieving first, so that they are comforted, and not judged.
@@13890@@
I have served for many years and now have some results. How can I tell whether my “roots are beside the water” or “my heart has already been lifted up”?
@@13891@@
You can check with three very concrete questions. First, when others criticize me, is my first reaction to want to listen, or to want to argue back? A person whose roots are by the water can listen; a person whose heart has been lifted up has an explanation as the first word. Second, when others succeed, am I glad in my heart, or a little uncomfortable? Third, recently, was there a time when I did something to make others look good, rather than to make myself look good? These questions have no standard answers, and you do not need to force yourself to be fixed at once. Every evening you can spend two minutes listing three things “the water gave,” and say thank you to God. This habit is itself putting the roots back by the water. If you find a problem, talk with someone who will tell you the truth; you do not need to carry it alone.
@@13892@@
The first problem: from childhood we live in comparison. “Other people’s children,” “Mrs. Wang next door’s son came first in the exams again”: we hear such words all our lives, and later we begin to use them ourselves. In the church, comparison puts on a spiritual coat: who prays more, who serves more, whose small group is larger. When we compare, the heart begins to grow upward and the roots go a little farther from the water. The question of 31:18 is just right to use against this: whom are you like? You can begin this way: this week, fast from comparing once. When someone speaks of his results, his children, or his service, do not quietly give marks in your heart; only say: “That’s wonderful, thank God.”
@@13893@@
The second problem: we are used to being “under the shade,” and dare not tell the truth. Respecting elders and submitting to pastors is originally a virtue, but sometimes it becomes a kind of silence: those under the shade see the problem, but because they fear being rude or losing face, they say nothing, until the tree falls. On the other hand, those sitting in high places often use “humility” to block others’ honest words, and a single “Oh, not at all” ends the conversation. Chapter 31 tells us: those in high places need someone to tell the truth, and those under the shade need the courage to say it. You can do this: if you are in a high place, find someone who is not under your shade and ask him to tell you one thing he sees in you, and only listen, without explaining; if you are under the shade, find a suitable time and, in a respectful tone, say one thing you have seen.
@@13894@@
I am the one who is compared and demanded of, and I often feel I cannot breathe under another person’s shade. What does this chapter say to me?
@@13895@@
This chapter is also speaking to you. You are not the tall tree; you are the person living in the shade. There is a sentence in the text that is often overlooked: “All the peoples of the earth have gone down from his shadow, and have left him.” This sentence tells us that God sees those under the shade, and knows who came seeking shade and who came to keep company. If you are pressed down, compared, and demanded of, please do not use this chapter to blame yourself, saying, “Why didn’t you become humble first?” God does not ask you to be strong at once, and does not ask you to forgive at once. You can first say one sentence to God: Lord, you see. Then find someone you can trust, say how you feel, and let someone keep you company once. If this pressure is already affecting your sleep, your emotions, and your daily life, please also seek help from a pastor or a professional.

@@13897@@
Jesus is my Role Model. “Who, existing in the form of God, didn’t consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men” (Philippians 2:6–7). @@13899@@

Quiet yourself for a moment each day and ask one sentence: Lord, where are my roots today? Then pay attention to the small voice in your heart; that may be the water speaking.
@@13900@@
Let Jesus be King. The pride in this chapter puts “I grew by myself” on the throne. @@13902@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is many trees rooted beside the same water (verse 14). God’s people do not compare who is taller, but remind one another where the water comes from. Life first, then relationships, then ministry; the higher a person’s position, the more he needs someone to tell him the truth. Such a community is a small piece of God’s system on earth.
@@13903@@
What is the relation between “Let Jesus be King” and “roots beside the water”?
@@13904@@
They are very close. Having roots beside the water is admitting that your life has a source; letting Jesus be King is admitting that the source has authority. The proud person pulls his roots out of the water, plants them in himself, and says, “I am my own water.” The person who lets Jesus be King puts his roots back in him and says, “Everything I have comes from you.” This is not self-belittling, but entering the limitless: selflessness is not having nothing, but returning to the fullness of that One. You can practice it this way: every morning, say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to rely on myself for today; what position I most fear losing today; today I put them back in your hands. After a while, you will find that having roots beside the water is not poverty; it is very secure.
@@13905@@
The proud type says: “The results I have today, I fought for them myself.” The text says to him: “The waters nourished it. The deep made it to grow.” The proud leave their position, seize power, and want to take God’s place; returning to one’s position is admitting that the water has always been there. You do not need to say it all at once; first say one sentence to God: “This is what you gave.”
@@13906@@

@@13907@@

@@13982@@
<p>The text uses “came” (<em>hayah</em>) to bring in God’s word, meaning that this word happened like an event. There is no real grammatical dispute in this verse; what is worth noticing is the dating format, which is the same as the date format in chapter 31 and places this lament in the same continuous series of messages.</p>
@@13983@@
<p><strong>God’s word first “happens,” and only then is spoken.</strong></p><p>This chapter begins with a date. The prophet does not say, “I had a feeling.” He says: Yahweh’s word came to me. It was something that had happened, not an idea.</p><p>In chapter 31 he has already spoken much judgment. The opening of this chapter is very short and very calm, as if reminding the hearers: the words that follow are not emotion; they are a commission.</p><p>The original word for “came” is <em>hayah</em>, a very ordinary verb: to become, to happen. When God’s word falls on a person, it becomes a fact that person must answer.</p><p>This is the first date in the chapter, followed at once by the lament; verse 17 has a second. Two dates divide the chapter into two receivings: first the song to Pharaoh himself, then the scene of going down to Sheol. God’s word has dates because God’s word enters real time.</p><p>Landing: today you hold a passage of Scripture you have read many times. Have you let it “happen” to you, or only let it stay in your head?</p>

<p><strong>What does “great fish” refer to?</strong></p><ul><li>One view: it refers to the crocodile, a symbolic animal of Egypt.</li><li>Another view: it refers to a monster of the water (<em>tannin</em>), hinting at a symbol of chaos.</li></ul><p>Both are tied to the Nile. The CUV’s rendering “great fish” is acceptable.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the original wants a beast that churns up the water, yet will in the end be caught in a net.</strong> Whether crocodile or monster, both are in the same picture.</p></blockquote>
@@13986@@
<p><strong>He thought he was a lion; God said, you are only a beast in the water.</strong></p><p>“Lion” is the image Pharaoh wanted for himself: walking among the nations with majesty. God replaced this image: “yet you are as a monster in the seas.”</p><p>The exchange is exact. A lion is on land, and people fear him. A monster in the water only stirs and tramples, and makes the water muddy. What Pharaoh did was not leadership, but muddying.</p><p>Please notice that the instruction the prophet received was to “take up a lamentation.” He is not scolding; he is singing a dirge for one who is about to fall. The message of judgment God gave the prophet is spoken with sorrow in its voice.</p><p>The lament (<em>qinah</em>) in the prophets is a funeral genre, with a rhythm of long and short lines, and a feeling of sinking, “half a beat behind.” God wrote judgment as a lament to tell the prophet: his heart must not be cold.</p><p>Landing: a person is often in the greatest danger when he is most like a lion. If you find that where you pass, the water is always muddied, stop first and ask: am I leading, or trampling?</p>

<p><strong>What does “a company of many peoples” mean?</strong></p><ul><li>It refers to the united armies of the nations, serving as the net that God uses.</li><li>It refers to people of many nations, so that the judgment becomes a witness before the nations.</li></ul><p>Both views work: the “net” in verse 3 is God’s, and those who carry it out are the nations.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the net is in God’s hand, and the meshes are the nations.</strong> This reading fits the picture in verses 9–10 of the nations being amazed at him.</p></blockquote>
