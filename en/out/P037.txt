<p><strong>① Who profaned whom?</strong> The same verb חלל (Piel) changes its subject between verse 16 and verse 18:</p><ul><li><strong>Verse 18</strong>: חִלַּלְתָּ, “you have profaned your sanctuaries”</li><li><strong>Verse 16</strong>: וָאֲחַלֶּלְךָ, “I have cast you as profane”</li></ul><p>The way he treated holy things is the way God treated him.</p><p><strong>② “A fire from the middle of you”</strong> (מִתּוֹכְךָ):</p><ul><li><strong>Inner self-destruction</strong>: the source of the fire is inside him</li><li><strong>A fire sent by God</strong>: וָאוֹצִא, “I have brought out a fire”</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: both together.</strong> The firewood is what he piled up himself; the one who lit it is God.</p></blockquote>
@@12092@@
<p><strong>The most deadly fire is the one that comes out from the middle of him.</strong></p><p>Verse 16 begins with the cause: “by the abundance of your commerce,” he was filled with violence. The same root is used: מָלֵא (to be full). In verse 12 he was “full of wisdom”; now he is “full of violence” (חָמָס). The same vessel, with different contents.</p><p>Verse 17 points to the root of the disease: “Your heart was lifted up because of your beauty. You have corrupted your wisdom by reason of your splendor.” His wisdom was not stolen by someone else. It was “you” who corrupted it (שִׁחַתָּ). When a person values brilliance more than wisdom, wisdom is the first thing to spoil.</p><p>The picture in verse 18 is the most startling: “I have brought out a fire from the middle of you. It has devoured you.” The fire did not burn in from outside; it came out from within him. Through unjust trade and many iniquities, bit by bit, he piled up the firewood himself.</p><p>Finally, the same sentence we heard in 26:21 and 27:36 sounds again: “You have become a terror, and you will exist no more.”</p><p>Landing it in life: many of the fires in our lives were not set by others. They were slowly piled up in our own hearts: a little favoritism, a little injustice, a little “no one will know anyway.” Rather than wait for the fire to break out, let us hand that pile of wood to the Lord now.</p>

<p><strong>① נִכְבַּדְתִּי and נִקְדַּשְׁתִּי: to be glorified, or to show his own glory?</strong> Both verbs are Niphal perfect, first person.</p><ul><li><strong>Passive</strong>: “I will be honored as glorious, honored as holy” (others glorify him)</li><li><strong>Reflexive / self-revealing</strong>: “I will show my glory, show my holiness” (he displays himself)</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: he displays himself, so that people see and acknowledge.</strong> In Ezekiel this pair of verbs often describes God making his glory and holiness seen before the nations, in judgment and in rescue (compare 38:16).</p></blockquote><p><strong>② What did the CUV leave out?</strong> The Hebrew of verse 22 has הִנְנִי עָלַיִךְ (behold, I am against you), the same sentence as in 26:3; the CUV does not translate it.</p>
@@12095@@
<p><strong>This time the purpose of judgment is written very directly: “I will be glorified among you.”</strong></p><p>The prophet’s gaze turns from Tyre to Sidon. Sidon is Tyre’s sister city, and those rowers in chapter 27 came from Sidon. These four verses are very short. There is no list of charges and no lament. Here the Hebrew has הִנְנִי עָלַיִךְ, “Behold, I am against you,” the same sentence as in 26:3, which the CUV does not translate.</p><p>Then comes one verb: וְנִכְבַּדְתִּי, “I will be glorified.” We have seen this root before, in 27:25, where Tyre’s ships were “very glorious” (and also very heavy). The same root, with a different owner. Tyre took pride in its goods, and so did Sidon; God says: No, the glory is mine.</p><p>Some people hear “I will be glorified” and feel that God is defending his own face. But look at the purpose that follows: “Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” Glory means letting people see who he is. For a world where everything has been put in the wrong place, this is what is needed most: give the center back to the center.</p><p>Landing it in life: we seldom sweat for “God to be glorified.” We sweat for “me to be glorified.” Today, let him go first: let Jesus be King, and let Jesus receive all the glory.</p>

<p><strong>① “After I have executed judgments,” or “when I execute judgments”?</strong> The Hebrew בַּעֲשׂוֹתִי שְׁפָטִים in verse 26 literally means “when I execute judgments.” Verse 22 uses the same construction, and the CUV renders it “when I execute judgment …”; but in verse 26 it is rendered “after executing judgment.”</p><ul><li><strong>After</strong>: judgment first, then dwelling in peace (the CUV)</li><li><strong>At the same time</strong>: God judges the scornful neighbors all around while giving his people peace; peace is built within the act of judgment</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: “when I execute judgments.”</strong> Both readings are possible; but being consistent with verse 22 lets us see that God’s judgment and God’s keeping are one and the same hand.</p></blockquote><p><strong>② “A pricking brier”:</strong> This is an echo of Numbers 33:55: those who remain will be thorns in the eyes and in the sides. Ezekiel turns that old saying into a promise: that thorn will be pulled out.</p>
@@12098@@
<p><strong>After three judgments, this passage speaks of houses, vineyards, and dwelling securely.</strong></p><p>Everything before was storm: the ship sank, the king fell, and pestilence came on Sidon. By the last three verses the tone suddenly relaxes. The “pricking brier” is pulled out, and God gathers the scattered house of Israel, and in the sight of the nations, in them, he is “sanctified.”</p><p>This “them” is the very people of Jerusalem whom Tyre laughed at earlier (26:2). Tyre said, “Aha! The gates are broken.” Here God says: one day they will dwell in the land I gave to my servant Jacob. Verse 26 says “dwell securely” twice (לָבֶטַח), and between them are two of the plainest things: building houses and planting vineyards.</p><p>Notice that plainness. These are not palaces, not a triumph. They are roofs, grapevines, one ordinary day after another. God’s promise finally lands in the most everyday place.</p><p>The closing words, “Then they will know that I am Yahweh their God.” “Their God” is the heartbeat of the whole book of Ezekiel: he is not only the Lord of judgment, he is their God.</p><p>Landing it in life: if you are waiting for a promise, do not stare only at the “big things.” What he promises is often these small things: a home you can return to, a vineyard growing slowly, a day when you are no longer pricked.</p>

I. I Sit in the Seat of God, in the Middle of the Seas (28:1–10)
@@12524@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. At the Lunar New Year reunion dinner, there is one chair at our family table that is always empty. It is Grandpa’s seat. All the children know that no one may sit there. One year, the youngest grandson, while the grown-ups were not looking, climbed up and sat there very properly, his two feet hanging in the air, chopsticks in his hand, and shouted loudly: I am Grandpa! The whole family burst out laughing, and Grandpa laughed the loudest of all. But I thought afterward that it was funny only because he was four years old. If the same thing happened with a forty-year-old, no one could laugh. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 28, the story of a man who sat in a seat that was not his.
@@12525@@
In verse 1, Yahweh’s word came to me again. In verse 2, God said to the prophet: “Son of man, tell the prince of Tyre, ‘The Lord Yahweh says: “Because your heart is lifted up, and you have said, ‘I am a god, I sit in the seat of God, in the middle of the seas;’ yet you are man, and not God, though you set your heart as the heart of God.”’” Please notice the order in this sentence. First the heart is lifted up, then the mouth says “I am God,” and last he sits in that chair. Pride does not begin with the mouth. It begins in the heart, and the mouth is only the last place it leaks out.
@@12526@@
Next, verses 3 to 5 are very interesting. God did not say this king was a fool. He said: “Behold, you are wiser than Daniel; there is no secret that is hidden from you; by your wisdom and by your understanding you have gotten yourself riches, and have gotten gold and silver into your treasures.” God acknowledged his talent and his wealth. But the last line of verse 5 is the turning point of the whole passage: “and your heart is lifted up because of your riches.” You see, this is a line: wisdom, then wealth, then pride. Wisdom is not a sin, and wealth is not a sin. The sin is the one word that quietly slipped in between: “by yourself.”
@@12527@@
God acknowledged that he had wisdom and wealth, and yet rebuked him. Then is it dangerous for me when my hard work succeeds?
@@12528@@
Success from hard work is not dangerous. What is dangerous is when the heart quietly changes the name on it. In Ezekiel 28:3, God himself first acknowledges that this king has wisdom, so talent is not the problem. The problem is in verses 4 and 5: “by your wisdom” and “your heart is lifted up because of your riches.” You can check yourself with three questions. First, when I succeed, can I name other people? Second, when I lose part of what I have, is my peace still there? Third, is there one thing I do that only God knows about, that is not seen by people? If you can answer all three honestly, your hard work is the use of a gift, not a moving of the chair. Doing your work well and giving the glory to God do not conflict.
@@12529@@
Verses 6 to 10 are God’s response. Strangers draw their swords against the beautiful things he gained by wisdom and defile his brightness; he goes down to the pit and dies in the heart of the seas. We must be careful not to treat these verses as a show to watch. I do not intend to enlarge those pictures. The point of verse 9 is only one sentence: “Will you yet say before him who kills you, ‘I am God’? But you are man, and not God, in the hand of him who wounds you.” A man sat on God’s chair and stayed there until the last moment, and only then discovered that this chair, from beginning to end, had not been prepared for him.
@@12530@@
Here is a very important reminder: these words were spoken to one specific king. We do not use them to judge any modern country, group, or person; and when we see others in disaster, we do not say, “This is God’s judgment.” We read it to look in the mirror.
@@12531@@
II. You Were in Eden, the Garden of God (28:11–19)
@@12532@@
In verses 11 to 19, God again instructed the prophet: take up a lamentation over the king of Tyre. Do you remember? In the previous chapter God also told him to sing a lament for a ship. This time it is for a person. Verse 12 says: “You were the seal of full measure, full of wisdom, and perfect in beauty.” Verse 13 says: “You were in Eden, the garden of God. Every precious stone adorned you: ruby, topaz, emerald, chrysolite, onyx, jasper, sapphire, turquoise, and beryl.” This is a list more splendid than the one in chapter 27, but this time it is not cargo; it is light draped on a person.
@@12533@@
Verse 14 says: “You were the anointed cherub who covers. Then I set you up on the holy mountain of God. You have walked up and down in the middle of the stones of fire.” Verse 15 is the most heartbreaking sentence in the whole passage: “You were perfect in your ways from the day that you were created, until unrighteousness was found in you.” Notice “the day that you were created.” His beauty, his wisdom, and his position were all prepared in the day he was created; they were given. Later, unrighteousness was found in him.
@@12534@@
Verses 16 and 17 give the reason: “By the abundance of your commerce, your insides were filled with violence, and you have sinned. … Your heart was lifted up because of your beauty. You have corrupted your wisdom by reason of your splendor.” You see, chapter 28 tells the same line again: commerce, beauty, splendor, then pride. Verse 18 says: “By the multitude of your iniquities, in the unrighteousness of your commerce, you have profaned your sanctuaries. Therefore I have brought out a fire from the middle of you.” Notice “from the middle of you.” It was not a fire that came from outside; it was something that burned up within him.
@@12535@@
Reading this far, everyone will certainly ask: Who is this person, really? In the history of the church there are four readings. First, this is a poetic description of a historical king, using the language of Eden to say everything about his honor and fall to the extreme. Second, there is an echo of Adam: Eden, the day of creation, being driven out. Third, through the king, it points to the first proud one, that is, Satan; many ancient church traditions read it this way, and often read it together with Isaiah 14. Fourth, the layers lie on top of each other: a king, the story of humanity, and a deeper background showing through. These notes set out each view side by side, do not draw a conclusion, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. Whichever you take, one thing is clear: the root is “your heart is lifted up.”
@@12536@@
Is the king of Tyre in chapter 28 really about Satan? Which view should I believe?
@@12537@@
Christians sincerely hold different views on this question, so we need not argue, and we need not make any one reading the bottom line of faith. You can handle it this way. First, hold on to what the text itself says clearly: this person was created, his beauty and wisdom were given, and he left his position because his heart was lifted up. Second, do not out of curiosity invent details about heaven that the text does not give; that will take us away from the point. Third, if your church tradition holds one particular reading, you can respect it, and you can also listen to others’ reasons. What this chapter finally brings us is not the name of a person, but a mirror: Is there that “lifted up” in my heart?
@@12538@@
At the end of this lament, verse 19 says: “All those who know you among the peoples will be astonished at you. You have become a terror, and you will exist no more.” As in the previous chapter, we do not use it to make any timetable, and we do not point to any modern person. We only hear in God’s tone that he cannot bear to lose him: he sang a lament for a created being who was once perfect.

III. Light from the Original Language: Lifted Up, Glory, Dwell (28:2, 22, 26)
@@12540@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter. Please remember them; you can take them home in thirty seconds.
@@12541@@
The first is “lifted up,” the Hebrew גָּבַהּ (gavah). Verse 2 says: your heart is lifted up. The word means “to become high,” and is also used of a tall person or a towering hill. It appears three times in this chapter: in verse 2, because of position; in verse 5, because of riches; in verse 17, because of beauty. Each time, the heart is lifted up. You see, the reasons people lift themselves up are no more than three: my seat, my money, my looks. Which one most easily lifts your heart today?
@@12542@@
The second is “be glorified,” the Hebrew וְנִכְבַּדְתִּי (nikhbadti). In verse 22, God says: I will be glorified among you. The root of this word means “heavy.” In Hebrew, glory is weight. What people lift up is light; God’s glory has weight. The proud person keeps wanting to lift himself higher; but what truly has weight is not the one who is lifted up, but the one whom God has placed in the right position.
@@12543@@
The third is “dwell,” the Hebrew יָשַׁב (yashav). Verse 2 says: I sit in the seat of God, and “sit” is this word; verse 26 says: they will dwell securely in the land, and “dwell” is also this word. The same root has two endings in this chapter. The one who wanted to sit in God’s seat, in the end, does not even have a secure seat; the one who is willing to return to his own position, in God’s hand, builds houses, plants vineyards, and dwells securely. A seat that is grabbed cannot be held steadily; a seat that is received can be lived in peacefully.
@@12544@@
Put the three words together and you have the road of this chapter: the person whose heart is lifted up (gavah) goes to grab another’s seat (yashav); God is the one who has weight (kavod), he does not need to be lifted up, and he is willing to let his people dwell securely.
@@12545@@
How do we use the saying “glory is weight” in daily life?
@@12546@@
You can think of it this way: light things are blown high by the wind, but when they land, they make no sound; heavy things, set in place, stand firm. We often lift ourselves very high so that we will be seen; but what really makes people secure is a life that has weight: honest, trustworthy, not showy. You can practice it this way: this week, in one matter, say one less sentence meant to make people think you are impressive. For example, do not add your own credit, and do not polish your past. Then notice whether your heart is more at peace. Giving glory to God is not making yourself small; it is letting yourself land at the right weight.

IV. Sidon, the Thorns, and Dwelling Securely (28:20–26)
@@12548@@
In verses 20 to 23, God again instructed the prophet to prophesy against Sidon. Sidon was Tyre’s neighbor, a city that traded along the coast together with it. God said: I will be glorified among you. When I execute judgments in her and am sanctified in her, they will know that I am Yahweh. I do not intend to enlarge the picture of verse 23; I only ask you to notice the most distinctive thing in this passage: the purpose of judgment is not destruction, but “they will know that I am Yahweh.” This sentence appears four times, in verses 22, 23, 24, and 26. In the last four small sections of the chapter, every section has this same purpose.
@@12549@@
Why does God do this? Because the question of the whole of chapter 28 is “who is God.” The king of Tyre said: I am God. Sidon did not know God. God said: You will all know that I am Yahweh. He does not do it for his own face, but for the people. When people do not know who God is, they look for a substitute, and put themselves in his place, or put someone else there. Knowing who God is is how we know who we are.
@@12550@@
Verse 24 is a very gentle sentence: “There will be no more a pricking brier to the house of Israel, nor a hurting thorn of any that are around them that scorned them.” A brier is something that pricks; a thorn is something that stabs the foot. The house of Israel was a people who had been pricked very painfully. God says: in the future these will no longer be briers. To the brothers and sisters who are being pricked right now, I want to say a word here: God sees that thorn. He did not say, “You must endure.” What he said is: I will deal with it.
@@12551@@
Verses 25 and 26 are the ending of the chapter: “When I have gathered the house of Israel from the peoples among whom they are scattered, and am sanctified in them in the sight of the nations, then they will dwell in their own land which I gave to my servant Jacob. They will dwell in it securely. Yes, they will build houses, plant vineyards, and will dwell securely. … Then they will know that I am Yahweh their God.” You see, this promise is stated very plainly: build houses, plant vineyards. No one is lifted up to heaven, and no one has to sit above anyone else. It is just a person returning to his land and living a very ordinary day. This is the end of the chapter: the proud one wanted to sit in God’s seat and could not dwell in peace; the humble one returns to his position and, in God’s hand, dwells securely.
@@12552@@
Please notice two things, so that it is not misused. First, this passage is God’s own act, not a condition for people. It is not that if you believe enough, everything will go smoothly. If you are not secure now, it is not because your faith is not enough. Second, we do not use this promise to make a timetable, and we do not paste it on any modern border. We see it only as God’s heart: he wants his people to have a place where they can settle down with a calm heart.
@@12553@@
Verse 26 says “dwell securely,” but I am not secure at all right now. Is my faith not enough?
@@12554@@
No. Verse 26 is God’s promise to his people, not an exam on the size of your faith. The text says it clearly: it is “I” who gather, “I” who execute judgments, “I” who give the land. This is God’s work. If you are not secure now, it may be because of work, health, family, or a wound you cannot put into words. This is not your fault. You can say to God: Lord, I am not secure right now; please let me know that you are still here. Then find someone you trust and say one sentence: I haven’t been sleeping well lately. If this unease is already affecting your daily life, please see a pastor, and if necessary ask for professional help as well. God’s promise is that he will do it, not that you must do it first.
@@12555@@
The first deviation is to say outright that chapter 28 means “the king of Tyre is Satan,” to draw a conclusion, and even to invent details of heaven and timetables. As for the subject of the text itself, there are different readings in the church, and the text does not give those details. We must first set out the various views side by side, and let the congregation hold on to what is clear: the root is a heart that is lifted up.
@@12556@@
The second deviation is to treat “pride” only as someone else’s fault, and use it to blame some leader, co-worker, or relative. This chapter is a mirror, not a pointing finger. We want every listener to think first of himself, not of “that person.”
@@12557@@
The third deviation is to say that talent and wealth themselves are sin, or to preach “humility” as “low self-esteem.” God himself first acknowledged the king’s wisdom (Ezekiel 28:3). The sin is not in having, but in “by yourself.” We want people to be neither proud nor to put themselves down, but to accept that they are created and loved.
@@12558@@
The fourth deviation is to preach “a fire from the middle of you,” the sword and the pestilence, as saying that everyone who meets disaster, illness, or loss must be the result of pride, or to use it to judge others’ disasters. These are words to one specific king and one city, not an accusation against the suffering. We want the suffering to be comforted, not accused.
@@12559@@
The fifth deviation is to preach “dwell securely” in verse 26 as a guarantee that believing in the Lord means everything goes well, or to say “you must be humble” to the oppressed, so that they are made to endure harm. Dwelling securely is God’s own act; humility is returning to one’s position, not letting people trample you. We must first protect the wounded, and then talk about our own pride.
@@12560@@
Is humility not mentioning your own strengths? How should I answer when others praise me?
@@12561@@
No. Humility is not putting yourself down, and it is not pretending to have no talent. In Ezekiel 28:3, God himself says this king was wiser than Daniel. Humility is honesty: this was given by God, and many people helped. So when others praise you, you can simply say: Thank you, this is God’s grace, and many people helped too. Saying it this way both receives the praise and puts the glory back where it belongs. On the other hand, if you keep saying “I’m not good enough” and “Oh, not at all,” while in your heart you are waiting for them to praise you once more, that is not humility; it is another way of lifting yourself up. Honesty is the weight of humility.
@@12562@@
The first question: we are very used to using titles and seats to decide a person’s worth. Chairman, general manager, pastor, elder, teacher: a title is often more important than a person’s name. The order of seating, the title on a business card, who sits where at a banquet, all are carefully arranged. These are not sins in themselves, but as the days go by, we mix up the “seat” with “me”: when the seat is taken away, we feel we are nothing. You can begin this way: this month, find an occasion with no titles, do not give your title, introduce yourself only by name, and see how you feel.
@@12563@@
The second question: we are very used to using polite modesty in place of true humility. When others praise me, I say: oh, not at all, I’m not good; yet in my heart I am waiting for them to say it once more. The king of Tyre in chapter 28 said plainly: I am God; we are more refined, and say “not at all” with our mouths, but in our hearts we too keep a chair for ourselves. You can check yourself this way: when someone sincerely thanks you, can you simply say: “You’re welcome, this is from God”? Then receive it quietly, not pushing it away and not adding to it.
@@12564@@
People who serve in the church are often called “teacher,” “pastor,” “elder.” How can they keep their hearts from being lifted up?
@@12565@@
The title itself is respect, and you can accept it; you do not need to refuse it every time. What matters is the position in your heart. You can ask yourself three questions every day. First, today I was called “teacher” many times; did I also call a co-worker by name? Second, today who helped me, and did I say it out loud? Third, if tomorrow no one called me this title any more, who would I be? The third is the most honest one. You can write the answer down and put it in your Bible. A servant is God’s servant, and also God’s child; neither identity needs to be raised any higher, and neither needs to be lowered. If one day this title is taken away, you are still the child he loves.
@@12566@@
VII. Living Out 321: Return to Our Position
@@12567@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He was in the form of God, yet “didn’t consider equality with God a thing to be grasped,” but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant. He sat in the seat he had the right to sit in, yet bent down to wash the disciples’ feet. Those who follow him learn this pattern: do not grab, do not seize, and leave the seat to the one who deserves it.
@@12568@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 28 not to guess who that person is, but to let the text ask me: Is there a corner in my heart that says “I am God”? Have I lifted up my heart because of position, riches, or beauty? 1 Peter 5:5 says: “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” This is my standard.
@@12569@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. When we are pleased with ourselves, the Spirit gently says: Who gave you this? When we are down, the Spirit gently says: I am still here. Spend a quiet moment each day and ask him to show you: today, which chair did I move to where I should not sit?
@@12570@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is putting down the chair of “I am God,” and letting him sit in his own seat. This is not destroying the self, but returning to our position: let man be man, and let God be God. Those who let Jesus be King no longer grow tired from guarding a seat, because the King is not me; he is him.
@@12571@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. Verse 22 says: I will be glorified among you. God does it for the sake of his name, so that people know he is Yahweh. Our achievements, talents, and beauty were all prepared on the day we were created; the credit we give to him, and also to those who gave together with us.
@@12572@@
To build God’s system. A God-centered group is not a group of people each wanting to sit on high, but a group of people each returning to their position: some are human beings, some are servants, and only God is God. In such a group, the promise of verse 26 comes true little by little: everyone builds houses, plants vineyards, and dwells securely.
@@12573@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “return to one’s position”?
@@12574@@
The relationship is direct. The definition of pride is leaving man’s position, grasping God’s power, and wanting to take God’s place. The king of Tyre in chapter 28 sat in “the seat of God,” which is leaving his position. To let Jesus be King is to give that chair back to him. This is not humiliating yourself; it is gaining freedom, because for a man to be a man is a light thing. You can practice it this way: every morning, say three sentences to Jesus: Today what I most want to control is ___; today what I most want people to value in me is ___; today I give these two back to you. After a while you will find how secure it is not to sit up there.
@@12575@@
The proud type says: “I earned this seat by my own effort; I deserve it.” The text says to him: “Yet you are man, and not God, though you set your heart as the heart of God.” The proud person leaves his position and grasps power; returning to one’s position is acknowledging: I am a man, and he is God. You can begin with one sentence: “This is what you gave me.”
@@12576@@
The fearful type says: “If I don’t hold on tight to this seat, I will have nothing.” The text says to him: they will dwell securely in the land. The fearful type, to keep a seat, often grips it tightly. You can begin with one small thing and loosen one hand: hand one piece of work over to someone else.
@@12577@@
The shame type says: “What strengths do I have? I can’t compare to others.” The text does not tell you to put yourself down. God himself acknowledged that king’s wisdom, and said he was perfect from the day he was created. Shame says: you have nothing; the gospel says: you are created, and you are loved. The shame type does not need to prove anything first, but can begin by saying to God: “Thank you for creating me.”
@@12578@@
The wounded type says: “I have been looked down on and oppressed; telling me to be humble is something I cannot do.” God did not tell you to endure oppression. Verse 24 says God will remove those pricking briers. The wounded can slowly find someone trustworthy and tell your story; those who accompany should not hurry to say “you must be humble” or “you must forgive,” but just say: “I am here.”
@@12579@@
First, write down the one thing that most easily lifts you up: position, money, or looks, and write down the name of one person who helped you gain it.
@@12580@@
Second, say one specific thank-you to one of them, say what it was for, and write down which day you said it.
@@12581@@
Third, spend three minutes each day saying to God: “This is what you gave me, and this seat is yours too.” And write down the date.
@@12582@@
Fourth, this week, when someone praises you, simply say: “Thank you, this is God’s grace.” Do not push it away, and do not add to it.
@@12583@@
Fifth, choose one piece of work or one decision you have been clutching, and this week hand over a small part of it to someone else; say which one and which day.
@@12584@@
I declare: I am a man, not God; all I have was prepared on the day I was created.
@@12585@@
I declare: I will not lift myself up by my own wisdom and riches; I am willing to say other people’s names.
@@12586@@
I declare: I return to my position and let Jesus be King; the glory is his.
@@12587@@
I declare: God is glorified in me; I will dwell securely in his hand.
@@12588@@
The simplest thing is to take a piece of paper today and write one sentence: The seat I most want to hold on to lately is ______, and the person who helped me is ______. No need to polish it, and no need to show it to anyone. Then, right now, send a short message to the person who helped you: I thought of you today, and thank you for helping me the way you did. It does not need to be long, and it does not need a reason. If you cannot think of anyone, then first say to God: This is what you gave me; thank you. This action is the answer of chapter 28: return to our position, beginning with a single thank-you. At the next small group, just say one sentence: This week I said thank you to one person, and I do not need to say the details.
@@12589@@
Chapter 28 begins with a chair and ends with a piece of land where one can build houses and plant vineyards. At the beginning is a man sitting in the seat of God in the middle of the seas; in the middle is a lament sung for him, saying that he was once in Eden, the garden of God, perfect from the day he was created; at the end is God’s promise: he will gather his people, and they will dwell securely in their land. From the chair to the vineyard, from the height to the level ground, not for one moment was God acting for the sake of his face.
@@12590@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that pride begins in the heart, when the heart is lifted up; God also wants us to see that, so that people will know he is Yahweh, he both judges and gathers. That God, in Jesus, left the seat he had the right to sit in, came among us, and said: I am gentle and humble in heart.
@@12591@@
So please remember the three key points of this lesson: Pride, Garden, Fall. The focus of this lesson is not who that person was, not how much talent you have, and not how others see you, but Jesus, who was seated in the place of God and yet willingly bent down.
