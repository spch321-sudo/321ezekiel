I. The Person on the High Chair (33:1–9)
@@14851@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Imagine a swimming beach at two o’clock in the afternoon, with the sun at its fiercest. The sand is full of people. Children are shrieking in the shallow water, adults are holding umbrellas, and popsicles are dripping onto hands. In the middle of the beach stands a very tall chair, and on it sits a person wearing a hat. His eyes are not on the fun. They are on the thin stream of water, a different color, behind the waves.
@@14852@@
Perhaps nothing happens all afternoon. But he cannot doze off. He cannot look down at his phone. He cannot put the whistle in his pocket just because the weather is nice. When the whistle blows, everyone turns to look at him, and some even find him a killjoy. But you should know that a beach without this person is not the same beach. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 33, and this chapter is the story of a high chair and a whistle.
@@14853@@
In verse 2, God first states a general rule: “Son of man, speak to the children of your people, and tell them, ‘When I bring the sword on a land, and the people of the land take a man from among them, and set him for their watchman.’” Please notice two things. First, the sword is something God says “I bring.” It is not an accident. Second, while God speaks of the sword, he also speaks of preparing a person. He does not only announce judgment; at the same time he has prepared someone to raise a voice. This person is not the highest official. He is one taken from among the people, and his work is only two things: to see, and then to blow the trumpet.
@@14854@@
Verses 3 to 5 divide the responsibility into two halves. If the watchman blows the trumpet, and a person who hears does not take the warning, his blood will be on his own head. If he takes the warning, the text says, he will have delivered his soul. Look at that last line: the purpose of the alarm is to save life, not to condemn. The one who hears has a choice. He can get up, or he can roll over, and the result rests on himself. This is how God honors people: he gives the alarm, and he leaves the response to them.
@@14855@@
Verse 6 is the heaviest sentence in this section. If the watchman sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet, God says: “his blood I will require at the watchman’s hand.” Some people read this and their hearts tighten. Let me first say this slowly: this verse speaks of a duty that God clearly assigned. It does not say that whenever a relative is not saved, someone has failed in their duty. If you are blaming yourself for a relative who has passed away, or you lie awake at night over a family member who has not yet turned back, please first hear this: God swore by his own life that he has no pleasure in the death of anyone. This chapter first comes to comfort you, and only then sends you out.
@@14856@@
In verses 7 to 9, God appoints Ezekiel again: “So you, son of man: I have set you a watchman to the house of Israel. Therefore hear the word from my mouth, and give them warnings from me.” The order is: first hear, then speak. A watchman does not blow the trumpet out of his own temper or his own prejudice. What he blows is the sound he first heard from God’s mouth. By verse 9, God says it plainly: if you warn him and he does not turn from his way, you have delivered your soul. Your responsibility is to be faithful, to open your mouth, not to decide the outcome for someone else. A watchman does not have to carry another person’s choice for the rest of his life.
@@14857@@
Chapter 33, verse 6 says God will “require” the watchman’s hand for the person’s blood. Does that mean that if I did not share the gospel with a relative and he was not saved, it is my responsibility?
@@14858@@
No. First look at the text: this was said about Ezekiel’s duty as a watchman specially appointed by God, and the setting is that the sword comes, someone sees it, and does not blow the trumpet. Readers of Scripture see this differently. Some think it applies only to a prophet who has been clearly entrusted. Some think the principle can extend to the church’s responsibility to witness: once you know, you must not stay silent. Others stress God’s sovereignty and grace, and say that a person’s salvation does not hang on any one messenger. The position of these notes is this: it is a solemn charge, from which ordinary believers can learn that love makes us speak, but they should not be driven by guilt. What a watchman is responsible for is faithfulness, not the outcome. So please do not say to anyone: your relative was not saved because you did not tell him. If you are blaming yourself right now, you do not need to sort out who is right and who is wrong first. First say to God: Lord, I hand him over to you, and I ask that you let me still love him today.
@@14859@@
II. Turn, Turn Back (33:10–20)
@@14860@@
Verse 10 is the people’s words, and a very heavy sentence: “Our transgressions and our sins are on us, and we pine away in them. How then can we live?” Have you ever worn a coat whose holes multiplied the more you mended it, until finally you said: it is like this anyway, I will not mend it any more? A sentence like that sounds like repentance, but it may already be giving up. We cannot use one label to say who these people were. Among them, some were truly so full of remorse that they could not lift their heads, and some used “How then can we live?” as a reason not to move. The text does not rush to tell them apart. It first lets God speak.
@@14861@@
Verse 11 is the heart of this chapter: “As I live,” says the Lord Yahweh, “I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live.” Please notice that when people say “How then can we live?”, God swears by his own life. He puts his own life up as the guarantee and says: I have no pleasure in your death. Then he does not give a long speech. There is only one invitation: “Turn, turn from your evil ways! For why will you die, house of Israel?” He says it twice, like a father standing at the door calling his child home, calling once and then calling again.
@@14862@@
In verses 12 to 16, God speaks with a pair of scales: the righteousness of the righteous will not deliver him in the day of his disobedience; and as for the wickedness of the wicked, he will not fall by it in the day that he turns from his wickedness. Readers of Scripture see this passage differently. Some think the warning is real, and a righteous person who leaves God may lose his life. Some think the warning is there to show whether faith is true or false, that true believers will be kept, and that the warning itself is a tool God uses to keep people. Others remind us that this was spoken to the people of the Old Testament, concerning obedience within the covenant and life and death, and should not be applied directly as the whole of New Testament teaching on salvation. These notes do not make any one of them a condition of faith. The three readings share one thing: do not rely on your own righteousness. Verse 13 says that if a person trusts in his righteousness and commits iniquity, none of his righteous deeds will be remembered.
@@14863@@
Now look at verse 15: turning back must land in daily life. “If the wicked restore the pledge, give again that which he had taken by robbery,” and walk in the statutes of life. Turning back is not only tears. It has an address: returning what you owe someone, making an apology that needs to be made, stopping one thing that is hurting someone. Saying many words in prayer is quick. Going this week to knock on that one family’s door is slower. God delights in the latter, because it is alive.
@@14864@@
In verses 17 to 20, the people say twice: “The way of the Lord is not fair.” God’s answer is also short: it is their own way that is not fair. Please do not hear this as God forbidding people to ask questions. In the Bible many people complain to God and question him, and God does not refuse them. The point here is this: the people used “not fair” as a shield to block the step they needed to take in turning back, so God turned the scales back into their hands. If you really have been treated unfairly, this verse is not about you. You do not need to sort out who is right and who is wrong first before God accepts you.
@@14865@@
Chapter 33 says, “The soul who sins shall die.” Does that mean sin counts only against the individual, and does not affect the family and later generations?
@@14866@@
No. This chapter and chapter 18 are sister chapters. Both answer the people’s shifting of blame: they said it was the sin of the ancestors, the fault of the times, and there was nothing they could do. God’s answer is that each person must face his own way. This is an answer to blame-shifting, not an accusation against those who suffer, and it does not deny the real effect of sin on families, communities, and later generations. Both levels exist side by side: the consequences of sin extend outward, but judgment is that each person stands before God. So this verse cannot be used to blame the bereaved, and cannot be used to say to a victim: you share the responsibility too. However deep the wounds of the past, verse 11 says that God has no pleasure in anyone’s death, and that he delights in people turning from their way and living.
@@14867@@
If a righteous person sins he will die. Does that mean someone who is saved can lose his salvation? I am very afraid of this. What should I do?
@@14868@@
This is a place where readers of Scripture see things differently, and these notes do not make any one view a condition of faith. Some think the warning is real, and a person who leaves God may lose his life. Some think the warning is there to show whether faith is true or false, that true believers will be kept, and that the warning is a tool God uses to keep people. Others stress that this was spoken to the people of the Old Testament and cannot be applied directly as the whole of New Testament teaching on salvation. Whichever view you hold, the purpose of this passage is not to make people uneasy every night. The problem that verse 13 names is trusting in one’s own righteousness: people think they are secure, and so they relax. So if you are afraid because of this, please first listen to verse 11: God has no pleasure in your death, and he is calling you to turn back.
@@14869@@
III. Light from the Original Language: צֹפֶה、שׁוּבוּ、עֲגָבִים (33:7, 11, 31)
@@14870@@
This chapter has three Hebrew words that are worth looking at slowly. The first is “watchman,” the Hebrew צֹפֶה (tsofeh). Verse 7 says: I have set you a watchman. The meaning of this word is someone who stands in a high place and looks far. Please notice that it is not “ruling,” and it is not “judging”; it is “seeing.” A watchman has only one kind of authority: what he sees, and that he says it out loud. Thirty seconds to turn it into life: you do not need authority to remind someone; you only need to see first, and then speak honestly.
@@14871@@
The second is “turn back,” the Hebrew שׁוּבוּ (shuvu). Verse 11 says: turn, turn from your evil ways. The root שׁוּב means to turn around and come back. Interestingly, this same word means both “leave the evil way” and “return to God.” One turn, two directions: back to one side, face to the other. The end of verse 11 is וְחָיָה (vekhayah), “and live.” What God delights in is not people’s death, but that they turn around, and live. Thirty seconds to turn it into life: repentance is not remaking yourself by yourself; it is turning around to face the voice that is calling you.
@@14872@@
The third is “love,” the Hebrew עֲגָבִים (agavim), which appears in verses 31 and 32: with their mouth they show much love, and you are to them as a very lovely song. This word refers to love songs, full of longing and even passion. Think about it: these people were not cold. They were very warm listeners, and the words in their mouths were as sweet as love songs. But the same verse goes on to say that their heart goes after their gain. The sting of the original is right here: the mouth is hot, the heart is cold. Thirty seconds to turn it into life: the next time you say “that was a great message,” first ask yourself one question: this one sentence, how do I plan to act on it this week?
@@14873@@
Put the three words together, and you have the road of this chapter: the one in the high place sees (tsofeh) and blows the trumpet; the ones who hear turn around (shuvu) and live; and at the very end, a group of people turn the word into a song (agavim) and never set out. Keep watch, turn back, listen: these are the three verbs of this chapter.
@@14874@@
A watchman “sees” and “says it out loud.” I have no spiritual gift. Can I still be a watchman?
@@14875@@
Yes, but please distinguish first: Ezekiel was a watchman as a prophet specially appointed by God, and that is a specific office. However, from this office we can learn a principle that ordinary believers can also practice: love makes us speak. A watchman’s authority is not in his position, but in what he has seen and that he has said it. So you do not need any impressive gift. What you need is three things. First, listen to the word from God’s mouth first, and do not speak out of temper. Second, speak clearly, and also gently. Third, after you have spoken, hand the result over to God. For example, if you see a colleague working late for several months in a row, you can ask in the tea room: How have you been lately? That is already a very small trumpet.
@@14876@@
IV. The News Has Come, and the Song Is Over (33:21–33)
@@14877@@
In verse 21, someone who had escaped from Jerusalem came to the prophet and said, “The city has been defeated!” Before that day the prophet’s mouth had been shut. Verse 22 says: “my mouth was opened, and I was no longer mute.” This was very heavy news, and a turning point in the prophet’s office: from announcing judgment to the words of comfort and rebuilding that come afterward. As for the date, the text simply records it. It does not ask us to calculate anything from it, and we do not make any timetable. This verse also reminds us that both silence and speech are in God’s hands. If you cannot speak for a while because of trauma or grief, this verse is not urging you to speak right away. God knows when to open your mouth.
@@14878@@
In verses 23 to 29, God speaks to those who remained in the waste places. They had a very forceful saying: “Abraham was one, and he inherited the land; but we are many. The land is given us for inheritance.” The meaning is: even one man received it, how much more we, who are so many. This sounds very reasonable, but it has a hole. They took a promise as a right, and took numbers as a guarantee. What God asks is: you eat with the blood, lift up your eyes to your idols, shed blood, stand on your sword, and defile your neighbor’s wife. “So should you possess the land?” He asks twice, and the question reaches into everyone’s life.
@@14879@@
Have you ever seen an old family house passed down through the generations? Grandfather left it behind, a plaque hangs over the door, and there are photographs in the living room. The people who live in it now have broken the plumbing, left a hole in the roof unrepaired, and still say to visitors: this is our family’s house. A house is a trust, not a get-out-of-death-free card. This passage asks: am I inheriting the promise, or am I using it up? Let every listener ask this question of himself, and not use it to point at a certain people or a certain country and say they are unworthy.
@@14880@@
Verses 30 to 33 are the last scene of this chapter, and the most heartbreaking. The people talk about the prophet by the walls and in the doors of the houses, and say to one another: “Please come and hear what the word is that comes out from Yahweh.” They really came, and sat before the prophet “as my people.” But God says: “they hear your words, but don’t do them; for with their mouth they show much love, but their heart goes after their gain.” They looked at the prophet as at a person with a pleasant voice, who plays well on an instrument, singing a love song.
@@14881@@
There are several readings here. Some say they loved the prophet’s eloquence. Some say they treated God’s word as entertainment and something to talk about. Some say the point is that mouth and heart were split apart. The three readings stand side by side, and these notes do not throw any of them away, because they all point to one thing: hearing is not the same as doing. More important, let us begin by checking the speaker and the listeners, ourselves, and not use this passage to criticize one particular speaker or blame one particular listener.
@@14882@@
Have you ever had a Sunday like this? You walk out of the meeting, and say to the person next to you: that was a great message today. You get in the car, and you are thinking about what to have for lunch. By Wednesday you cannot remember even one sentence. God says: with their mouth they show much love, but their heart goes after their gain. This is not blaming us for loving to listen. It is an invitation: take the one sentence you heard, and act on it with one step. Verse 33 says that when this comes to pass, it comes, and then they will know that a prophet has been among them. God’s word does not fall to the ground; it is only that he would much rather we set out before it is fulfilled.
@@14883@@
I am often very moved when I listen, and then I forget it when I get home. Am I the kind of person that chapter 33 talks about?
@@14884@@
Do not rush to condemn yourself. The point of this chapter is not to blame people who love to hear the word, but to point out an easy trap: hearing can take the place of doing. The people the text speaks of showed much love with their mouths while their hearts went after gain, so they heard and did not act. If this often happens to you, you can do three very small things. First, after you listen, choose just one sentence and write it in your phone, not ten sentences. Second, turn it into an action with a time on it, for example: on Wednesday evening, send a message to someone. Third, tell a companion and ask him to check with you next week. In this way the sound of the song slowly turns into footsteps.
@@14885@@
The first deviation is to take God’s “requiring the watchman’s blood” in chapter 33, verses 6 and 8, and preach it as meaning that whenever a relative is not saved, the one who should have shared the gospel has failed in his duty. This is an office clearly assigned by God, and what the watchman is responsible for is faithfulness, not the outcome. We want the congregation to love people more and dare to speak more, not to feel more guilt and more fear.
@@14886@@
The second deviation is to take “the soul who sins shall die” in chapter 33, verses 10 to 20, and preach it as meaning that sin counts only against the individual and does not affect the group, or to use it to blame the bereaved and victims. This passage is an answer to blame-shifting, not an accusation against those who suffer, and it does not deny the real effect of sin on families and later generations. We want the wounded to be accompanied, not interrogated.
@@14887@@
The third deviation is to take the righteous person who sins and dies in chapter 33, verses 12 to 13 and 18, and preach it as meaning that he will certainly lose his salvation, or that he certainly will not, and to make one of these a condition of faith. There are different theological explanations of this passage, and whichever one you hold, none of them is meant to make people live in fear. We want the congregation to rely more on God, not more on their own righteousness, and not to argue with one another more.
@@14888@@
The fourth deviation is to take the people saying “the way of the Lord is not fair” in chapter 33, verses 17 and 20, and preach it as meaning that people may not ask God questions, or to use it to shame someone who is in pain. God accepts honest questions; he just does not accept “not fair” being used as a shield to block turning back. We want the congregation to dare to be honest, and also to be willing to be corrected, not to stop asking.
@@14889@@
The fifth deviation is to use chapter 33, verses 24 to 29 and 30 to 32, to point at a certain people or a certain country and say they are unworthy, or to criticize a certain speaker. These verses are a mirror for every listener, and we begin by checking ourselves. We want the congregation to be more humble and more willing to act, not to have one more reason to blame others.
@@14890@@
The first question: we are very used to “sharing good news and hiding bad news,” and also used to “not spoiling the mood,” so a watchman’s words are hard to say. The other extreme is to say “it’s for your own good” in a great pile of words. Watching is neither of these. The watchman in chapter 33, verse 3, blows the trumpet so that people will hear it, not so that people will be embarrassed. You can begin like this: this week, find someone you carry in your heart, go to a quiet place, and ask him in one sentence: How have you been lately? Then listen. Do not rush to argue, and do not rush to a conclusion. Sometimes a gentle greeting is the clearest trumpet.
@@14891@@
The second question: we are very used to the sense of security in “our ancestors were virtuous, our family has roots,” and also used to counting “I’ve heard it and nodded” as having done it. “Abraham was one, and he inherited the land” is not strange to our ears: our family has believed in the Lord for many years, I am a third-generation Christian. These are all grace, not a lucky charm. You can check like this: this past month, was there even one time after hearing the word that I did something other people could see, and can I name the date and the person? If not, please begin with the smallest thing.
@@14892@@
I am an elder, and my children often find my talk annoying. How can I keep watch without becoming a nag?
@@14893@@
Your intention is good. The trumpet just needs to sound to people like an alarm and not like noise. You can try three things. First, listen before you speak. Chapter 33, verse 7, says the watchman must first hear the word from God’s mouth, and you can also first listen to what your child has been going through lately. Second, say only one thing at a time, and do not bring out all the old accounts. Third, after you have spoken, hand the result over to God: verse 9 says, if you warn him and he does not turn, your duty is done. You do not have to carry your child’s choices for the rest of your life, and you do not have to keep quiet for fear that he will not listen. Finally, pray for him for one minute every day. If your child is in the middle of pain, please accompany him first, and do not rush to correct him.

VII. Living Out 321: First Hear the Word from God’s Mouth, Then Blow the Trumpet Clearly
@@14895@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep. When he saw the crowds, he had compassion on them; he did not threaten them. He is also the One who truly heard the Father’s words and spoke the Father’s words. Those who follow him learn: hear first, then speak; and when you speak, speak with tears and love, not in a tone of looking down from above.
@@14896@@
The Bible is my Standard. The watchman first hears the word from God’s mouth, as verse 7 says plainly. Every reminder we give must first be checked by this Book: is it God’s word, or is it my own temper? Is it to save someone, or to win over someone? The standard of this chapter is verse 11: God has no pleasure in the death of anyone, but delights that people turn from their way and live. Any warning that does not match this heart must go back to the Scripture to be recalibrated.
@@14897@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lets us see the person who needs a reminder, and it is the Holy Spirit who lets us speak gently and speak accurately. Verse 22 says that Yahweh’s hand was on the prophet and opened his mouth. We do not rely on our own eloquence or our own zeal. We wait quietly, and when he opens his mouth, then we open ours.
@@14898@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is to put down two voices: one says “I am finished, how can I live,” and the other says “I am secure, this land is ours.” To let Jesus be King is to put both “my ending” and “other people’s endings” back into his hands. A watchman is not the savior, and the one who passes on the message is not the one who decides the outcome.
@@14899@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If someone turns back because of our reminder, do not say “I talked him into it”; give the glory to the Lord who called him. If someone does not turn back, do not say “I failed” either, because the outcome is not in our hands. All the glory goes to the God who has no pleasure in the death of anyone.
@@14900@@
Build God’s system. God’s system is a group of people who act on what they hear, keeping watch for one another: the trumpet is not for intimidation, it is for saving life; keeping watch is not being above others, it is a quiet kind of love. First life, then relationships, then ministry: first be gripped by the love that has no pleasure in my death, and then I can become a good watchman.
@@14901@@
How can I tell whether my reminder comes from love, or from wanting to control?
@@14902@@
You can ask yourself four questions. First, before I said this sentence, did I pray for him first? Second, is what I am saying God’s word, or my own opinion? Verse 7 says, hear the word from my mouth. Third, if he hears and does not change, will I get angry, turn cold, or stop dealing with him? If so, perhaps what I care about is the result and not him. Fourth, am I willing to be reminded first? A watchman must listen too. After a reminder that comes from love, you are still willing to sit and have a cup of tea with him. After a reminder that comes from control, you will be in a hurry to see whether he does what you said. You do not need to get it right all at once; you only need to be willing to bring these four questions into prayer.
@@14903@@
The proud type says: “This land is ours, we are many, we are very secure.” The text says to him: should you possess the land? Pride is leaving one’s position as a person, grabbing God’s authority, and even wanting to decide other people’s endings on God’s behalf. To return to one’s position is to admit: I am a watchman, not the savior. Say one sentence to God first: “Lord, the outcome is in your hands.”
@@14904@@
The fearful type says: “If I do not blow the trumpet, it is my sin; if I blow it and he does not listen, that is my fault too.” This heart has read “require the blood” as an iron chain. God’s word is: you warned him, he did not turn, and your duty is done. The fearful type needs to learn to replace “afraid of making a mistake” with “I hear first, then speak, and then hand it over to God.”
@@14905@@
The ashamed type says: “I have so many transgressions, how then can I live?” These are the words of verse 10. God’s answer is to swear by his own life: I have no pleasure in your death. Shame says “you are finished,” and God says “turn back.” The ashamed type does not need to tidy himself up first; he only needs to turn toward him once today.
@@14906@@
The wounded type says: “I spoke, and they still did not listen; I loved, and they still left. I never want to open my mouth again.” God does not blame you for being wounded; he has seen every night you stood on the wall. If you cannot speak, that is all right too. God knows when to open your mouth. A wounded person can slowly find someone trustworthy and say: “I am tired.” The one who accompanies needs only say: “I am here.”
@@14907@@
First, every day, spend two minutes saying to God: “You have no pleasure in the death of anyone, and no pleasure in my death; today I turn back to you.” And put a mark on the calendar.
@@14908@@
Second, write down one thing that “I have heard but not yet done,” choose one smallest action, and write down which day you will do it.
@@14909@@
Third, pray for one minute every day for someone you carry in your heart, and say his name; this week, use a greeting, a cup of tea, or some company with no conditions, to let him know you care.
@@14910@@
Fourth, find one thing you owe someone, or one apology you need to make (the “restore the pledge” of verse 15), take one small step this week, and write down the person and the date.
@@14911@@
Fifth, ask a companion to ask you next week: “That sentence you heard, have you done it yet?” Say his name, and the time you have agreed on.

<p><strong>Who are the “shepherds”?</strong></p><ul><li>They are the leaders of the nation, including kings and officials (the ancient Near East often used the shepherd as a figure for the king).</li><li>They also include religious leaders: priests and prophets.</li></ul><p>What this chapter goes on to discuss is the office, not the person. What the text rebukes is a pattern of behavior: “feeding oneself.”</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: all who have been entrusted with leading God’s flock.</strong> These notes do not use this chapter to attack anyone, but invite everyone who holds an office of leading to first ask themselves this question: whom am I shepherding?</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>“Woe” is the sound heard at funerals; when God opens his mouth, first there is a sigh.</strong></p><p>Verse 2 has a very simple question: aren’t shepherds supposed to feed the sheep? The answer needs no thinking. A shepherd exists for the sheep.</p><p>The Hebrew word for “shepherd” (<em>ra‘ah</em>) is both a verb and the root of the noun “shepherd.” A shepherd is simply the one who does the work of shepherding. <p><strong>“The shepherds won’t feed themselves any more”: is this punishment or grace?</strong></p><ul><li>Punishment: the shepherds are stripped of their office.</li><li>Grace: the shepherds are released from the burden of “feeding themselves.”</li></ul><p>This sentence can be read both ways: God is removing a distorted office.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: first judgment, and also release.</strong> Feeding oneself was a heavy burden from the start.</p></blockquote>
@@14997@@
<p><strong>The sheep are his, so he will require them himself.</strong></p><p>These verses repeat “my sheep.” The sheep do not belong to the shepherds; the shepherds are only people who were entrusted. When the shepherds forget this, God steps forward himself.</p><p>“I am against the shepherds”: the weight of this sentence is not in anger, but in protecting the sheep. Those God protects are the ones who were overlooked. He stands across from the shepherds on their behalf.</p><p>The last sentence leaves a way out: the shepherds won’t feed themselves any more. Only when a person no longer relies on the sheep to feed himself does he have a chance to become a real shepherd.</p><p>God’s words repeat again and again here: “my sheep,” because God wants people to remember that every relationship of shepherding stands under the four words “his sheep.”</p><p>God’s anger is kindled for the sake of the sheep. He does not allow anyone to treat what he loves as something to be used freely.</p><p>Landing it: do not use this passage to judge others. First ask yourself: of those I lead, do I often say “mine”? Or do I remember that they are “his”? Humility is remembering whose the sheep are.</p>

<p>The <em>hinneni ’ani</em> of verse 11 (“Behold, I myself, even I”) is the turning point of the chapter. Before it there were three verses about “shepherds”; now “I” becomes the subject.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Worth noticing:</strong> the word “bring out” (<em>hotsi’ti</em>) is the verb of the Exodus. God will once again bring his sheep out.</p></blockquote>
@@15000@@
<p><strong>“I myself, even I”: this is the warmest sentence in the whole chapter.</strong></p><p>Before it comes a long list of neglected duties, and at verse 11 the subject of the sentence suddenly changes: Behold, I myself, even I, will search for my sheep.</p><p>God did not say, “I will find better shepherds.” He said: I myself. This is, in the prophets, the picture that comes nearest to people: the God who created heaven and earth stoops down to look for one lost sheep.</p><p>The “bring out” of verse 13 is the verb of the Exodus. This shepherd is also the One who led people out of Egypt. His way has always been like this: not shouting from far away, but going in himself.</p><p>“Bring out,” “gather,” and “bring in” are three verbs in a row. This is the whole journey of returning, and at every step the subject is “I.”</p><p>This “I” is, in Hebrew, an emphatic pronoun, as if pointing a finger at himself and saying: it is me, not someone else.</p><p>Landing it: if you are the one that was scattered, he does not send someone to find you; he comes himself. You do not need to crawl back first; you only need to let him find you.</p>

<p><strong>What is “I will destroy the fat and the strong” in verse 16?</strong></p><ul><li>The MT reads <em>’ashmid</em> (destroy): God will remove the fat sheep who oppress.</li><li>Ancient versions (such as the Septuagint and the Syriac) read “watch over” (<em>’eshmor</em>): God will watch over and protect the strong.</li></ul><p>The verses that follow, 17–22, speak of judgment on the fat sheep, which is consistent with “destroy.”</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: read it as “destroy” following the MT.</strong> This runs together with the later “the fat sheep oppress the lean sheep”; what is destroyed is the act of oppression, and the aim is to protect the weak.</p></blockquote>
