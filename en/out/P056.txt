The holy rooms had three stories, with gallery against gallery on the third floor, and the upper stories were narrower than the lower and middle ones because the galleries took up some of the space. Verse 6 also says that the holy rooms were in three stories, but had no pillars, unlike the buildings of the outer court. This is a description of architecture; scholars read the plan in different ways, some drawing it as stepped terraces and some as connected corridors, and the original meaning is hard to settle. These notes do not decide for any one of them, and do not ask you to memorize it. You only need to hold on to two things: this is a house with order and with levels; and it was not built in the middle of the court, but on the open space beside the temple.
@@19197@@
Verses 7 to 9 say that outside the holy rooms, on the east there was a wall next to the outer court, fifty cubits long; the holy rooms on the north of the temple were a hundred cubits long; and below the holy rooms, at the east end, there was an entrance, the one from the outer court. Please notice this last sentence: entering from the outer court. These buildings were entered from the court outside, not walked out into from inside the temple. They stand on the border between the holy and the common, a place in between.
@@19198@@
There was a head nurse who, every day before shift change, would first put on her uniform in the changing room. She said: That little room is only three square meters, but every day in that room I go from “me going home” to “me at the bedside.” She said: Without that room, I might carry the exhaustion from outside into the ward just as it is. The holy rooms are such a little room too, a place where a person goes from this side to that side.
@@19199@@
There is one more thing worth pausing over. In this chapter the prophet says not a single word. He is only brought out, brought in; he looks, listens, and remembers. In a place where everything is measured so clearly, he has no need to offer opinions. Sometimes what a person needs most is not more words, but to be led, slowly, through God’s house, and to keep it in the heart.
@@19200@@
The architecture of chapter 42 reads as very confusing, and I can’t draw the plan. Can I still understand it?
@@19201@@
Yes. Even scholars read the architecture of this chapter in different ways, so you don’t need to draw a complete plan. Please remember three main points. First, there is a set of holy rooms on the north and on the south of the temple, both facing the open space. Second, they have doors and a passage; this is a place you can walk into. Third, they lie between the holy place and the outer court. You can read it this way: first read it once, asking only, “Where does he bring me?” Then read it again, asking only, “What is this place for?” The answer is in verses 13 and 14. Reading the Bible is not about drawing a perfect plan; it is about listening to God speak. If you find the plan too difficult, set it aside and skip to verse 13, then come back; you will not lose the main point of the chapter.
@@19202@@
II. The Southern Holy Rooms and the Priests’ Table (42:10–13)
@@19203@@
Verse 10 says there are also holy rooms toward the south, within the thickness of the wall of the inner court, one row opposite the pavement and one row along the open space. Verse 11 says the passage before the rooms was the same length and width as the passage of the northern holy rooms, and the exits were like the pattern of the doors of the northern rooms. Verse 12 says there was a door at the head of the passage, at the east end, as one enters, like the door of the southern rooms. South and north have the same pattern, and the doors are the same. This is a very orderly picture: both sides match, and neither is more special than the other.
@@19204@@
Then verse 13 explains what these buildings are for. “He said to me, ‘The north rooms and the south rooms, which are before the separate place, are the holy rooms, where the priests who are near to Yahweh shall eat the most holy things. There they shall lay the most holy things, with the meal offering, the sin offering, and the trespass offering; for the place is holy.’” Look at the weight of this sentence: these buildings were prepared for the priests who are near to Yahweh. The priests eat here, and they also lay things down here.
@@19205@@
Here is something that is easy to overlook: the priests’ food comes from the offerings that were brought. The meal offering, the sin offering, and the trespass offering were all brought by the people, and in the holy rooms the priests ate those most holy things. It is a very quiet picture: a person confesses, a person offers, and the priest sits in the holy rooms and has a meal. What was offered for sin was not wasted and not laid down carelessly; it was received well, there in the holy rooms.
@@19206@@
A friend of mine has served in the church kitchen for more than twenty years. She says what she fears most is handling what others have offered in a careless way. Once someone brought a basket of vegetables they had grown themselves, saying it was an offering to the church. In the kitchen she washed them carefully, cooked them carefully, and carried them out. She said: That day I understood that receiving what others offer is a holy thing. She was not a priest, but that day she stood in the holy rooms.
@@19207@@
Some ask: Are these offerings literal, or symbolic? Some say this is a real future temple and real sacrifices; some say this is Old Testament language describing God and people dwelling together again; some say this has already been fulfilled in Christ and should be read spiritually. These notes set out all three readings side by side, assume no timetable, and do not judge those who see it differently. What we agree on is this: God is holy, his house has order, and those who draw near to him must be prepared.
@@19208@@
The text says the priests “eat the most holy things” in the holy rooms. What does this have to do with me today?
@@19209@@
First we must admit that this is the language of the Old Testament priesthood, and the schools read its meaning for today differently. Still, there is an unchanging principle: what those who draw near to God receive comes from God’s grace, and is to be received with a reverent heart. The New Testament says believers are a royal priesthood. If you read it as spiritual application, you can think of it this way: what I eat, use, and receive today, do I take it with thanksgiving, and not as something owed to me? You can practice one small thing: before each meal, pause for three seconds and say to God: This is from you. This is the home edition of the kind of focus the priests had in the holy rooms.
@@19210@@
III. Changing Clothes and Measuring All Around (42:14–20)
@@19211@@
Verse 14 is the most everyday sentence in this chapter: “When the priests enter in, then they shall not go out of the holy place into the outer court, but they shall lay their garments in which they minister there; for they are holy. Then they shall put on other garments, and shall approach that which is for the people.” It means: when the priests have finished serving, they must first take off the holy garments and lay them in the holy rooms, and put on other garments before going out to meet the people.
@@19212@@
Why? Because the holy garments are set apart for a specific use. They are worn in the holy place, not in the marketplace. This rule protects two things: one is to protect what is holy from being stained by everyday dust; the other is to protect the people from a weight they should not have to bear. The holy is God’s matter. People are people’s matter. Both are to be respected.
@@19213@@
But please notice that this rule does not mean that when a priest returns among the people, he is no longer a priest. He is still a priest; he has only changed his garment and walked into the crowd. This is a kind of humility: not showing off in holy garments before people. An old pastor said this was his favorite verse, because it told him that once he steps down from the pulpit, he is an ordinary brother who can stand in line, wash dishes, and be corrected. The garment of the pulpit is to be laid down in the holy rooms.
@@19214@@
Then verses 15 to 20. When he had finished measuring the inner temple, he brought me out by the gate that faces east and measured the court all around. The east side five hundred cubits, the north side five hundred cubits, the south side five hundred cubits, the west side five hundred cubits. Verse 20 sums it up: “He measured it on the four sides. It had a wall around it, the length five hundred, and the width five hundred, to make a separation between that which was holy and that which was common.” It is a square boundary, equal on all four sides. Outside is the common ground, inside is the holy ground, and between them is a wall.
@@19215@@
Please notice the purpose in the last sentence: to make a separation. The wall is not for exclusion; it is for separation. Separation lets the holy be holy and the common be common. This does not mean the common ground is bad; fields, markets, and homes are all good places that God made. But they are not the Most Holy Place, and they must not be confused with it. The wall’s work is to let each place do what it is meant to do.
@@19216@@
A mother made a rule at home: no phones at the dinner table. At first the children complained, but one day a child said: Mom, I’ve found that when we eat, I’m really eating. The rule did not say phones are bad; it only said: this place belongs to us together. A wall is often a form of love.
@@19217@@
So the act of changing clothes, small as it looks, is actually a kind of honesty. It admits: what I do in the holy place is not mine; and what I do when I return to the people is not to show off anything. A brother who is a manager at his company and an elder at church says that his shortest daily prayer is in the parking lot, after he turns off the engine, sitting for thirty seconds. He says: These thirty seconds are my holy room.
@@19218@@
Why do the priests still need to change clothes after serving? Is changing clothes only a formality?
@@19219@@
It is not only a formality. In the Bible, clothing often stands for identity and trust. When the priests wear the holy garments, they stand before God on behalf of the people; when they take them off, they are returning to the people to be with them. This act of changing clothes has two meanings: one is not to treat holy things as your own halo; the other is not to treat everyday things as unclean. You can apply this to yourself: when I step down from the pulpit, from my post, from the service, can I still be like an ordinary person, reminded and helped by others? Each day when you get back to your front door, you can take ten seconds and say to God: I lay down today’s work, and return to the people who love me.
@@19220@@
IV. Light from the Hebrew: Separation, Garments, Room (42:20, 14, 1)
@@19221@@
Three words in this chapter are worth looking at closely. The first is “separation,” the Hebrew לְהַבְדִּיל (lehavdil); verse 20 says “to make a separation between that which was holy and that which was common.” The root of this word, בָּדַל (badal), is the very word used in Genesis 1 where God separated the light from the darkness and the waters from the waters. Separating is an act of creation, not a rule people invented. God separates first, and then says: this is good. So separation is not coldness; it is order, and where there is order, people are safe.
@@19222@@
The second is “garments,” the Hebrew בִּגְדֵי (bigdei); verse 14 says “their garments in which they minister.” This word is the construct form of בֶּגֶד (beged), meaning garment or robe. Interestingly, its consonants are exactly the same as those of “betray” (בָּגַד, bagad). A garment can be a trust, and it can be betrayed; the one who wears the holy garment is the one who has been entrusted. The identities you wear today, father, mother, coworker, supervisor, are each a garment of trust.
@@19223@@
The third is “room,” the Hebrew לִשְׁכָּה (lishkah); verse 1 says “the holy rooms.” This word is simply an ordinary room or chamber, and in Ezra and Nehemiah it also refers to the rooms beside the temple. In God’s house there is not only the Most Holy Place and the altar, but also very ordinary rooms: for storing things, for eating, for changing clothes. His house is a house with a kitchen and a wardrobe. This reassures us: God is not only in the holiest place, but also in that little room where the priests eat and change their clothes.
@@19224@@
Put the three words together: separation (lehavdil) lets the holy be holy; garments (bigdei) let everyone know what they have been entrusted with; the room (lishkah) gives people a place to breathe between the holy and the everyday. Separation is God’s work; garments are a person’s trust; the room is God’s grace to people.
@@19225@@
“Garment” and “betray” have the same consonants. Is God hinting at something? Can I preach it this way?
@@19226@@
Be careful. This is a coincidence of Hebrew letter forms, and Old Testament scholars do not usually treat it as a theological argument, because many words have such coincidences and the text itself does not say so. A steadier way to put it is: in the Bible, clothing often stands for identity and trust, and failing a trust is betrayal. You can take it as a meditation and not as doctrine: What identity am I wearing today? Have I been faithful to it? When you preach, if you cite this letter pattern, please say that it is a reminder and not an interpretation of the text. What the text does not say, we should not turn into “the Bible says.” Facing the original honestly is also part of fearing God.
@@19227@@
The first deviation is to treat the measurements and buildings of chapter 42 as a blueprint from which the time can be calculated, saying that the day of building the temple is near. The text gives no date and does not tell anyone to draw up a timetable; the schools read chapters 40 to 48 literally, symbolically, and spiritually, and we set them side by side without judging. We want the congregation to love the Lord more and to be more patient, not more anxious and fonder of guessing.
@@19228@@
The second deviation is to preach the wall between holy and common as meaning that people cannot draw near to God, or that daily life is unclean. The wall of verse 20 is for separation, not for exclusion; the everyday that God made is good. We want people to respect the holy more and also to treasure daily life, not to fear God more and think less of life.
@@19229@@
The third deviation is to turn the rule of the priests changing clothes into a claim that today’s pastors and coworkers need a special dress and status, higher than the members. The point of verse 14 is laying down the holy garments and returning among the people; it is humility, not privilege. We want those who serve to be more humble, not more distant.
@@19230@@
The fourth deviation is to preach the meal offering, sin offering, and trespass offering of verse 13 as something people today must still offer in the same way, or to deny the redemption Christ has already completed. The schools read the new temple and its offerings differently; these notes set them side by side, assume no timetable, and do not judge. What we agree on is this: God is holy, sin must be faced, and grace comes from him. We want the congregation to be more thankful and to take sin more seriously, not to quarrel over offerings.
@@19231@@
The fifth deviation is to preach the square of five hundred cubits on each side as meaning that we must do something exactly according to this measurement, or to calculate something with it. In the text, this square is there to make a separation between the holy ground and the common ground; it is not a number for people to compute. We want the congregation to see God’s order more, not to cling to numbers.
@@19232@@
If holy and common must be separated, does that mean I can’t be a person who belongs to God at work or at home?
@@19233@@
No. Separation lets each place do what it is meant to do; it does not say God is only in the holy place. The New Testament says that the believer’s body is the temple of the Holy Spirit, and whether we eat or drink or whatever we do, we are to do it for the glory of God. So at work and at home you are still a person who belongs to God; you only need to know which garment you are wearing now: at work, you are a coworker; at home, a husband, a wife, a child. Holiness does not turn the everyday into church; it places the everyday in God’s hands. Before you go out each day, you can say one sentence to God: Lord, this place today is where you sent me.
@@19234@@
The first question: we are used to “bringing work home,” and also “bringing home emotions to church.” In Chinese society, diligence is a virtue, but often we have no place to take off the uniform. As a result, we are still talking business at home, and still thinking about bills at church, and there is no place that belongs completely to God. The holy rooms of verse 14 remind us: changing clothes is a form of respect. You can practice it this way: each day before you go through your front door, stand at the door for ten seconds and say to God: Today’s matters, I give to you. Name one matter from today, and lay it down.
@@19235@@
The second question: we are used to “role over person”; pastors, elders, and coworkers are often bound by their roles and dare not speak of weakness. Verse 14 says the priests must lay down the holy garments and put on other garments before going among the people. You can check yourself this way: at church, do I have one friend who does not look at my role, only at me as a person? If so, say his name, and reach out to him this month. If not, start this month with a smaller circle.
@@19236@@
I’ve served in the church for many years, and I feel I’ve been “wearing the holy garment” all along, and can’t lay it down even at home. What should I do?
@@19237@@
This is real exhaustion. You can begin with three small steps. First, find a fixed time for “changing clothes”: on the way home from work, before you go in the door, or ten minutes before bed; lay down the day’s role and say to God: You are the Lord of the temple, and I am an ordinary person. Second, find one trusted person and, once a month, talk not about ministry but about life. Third, allow yourself an afternoon with no role at all. This is not laziness; it is the obedience of verse 14: the holy garments are laid in the holy rooms so that a person can walk into the crowd. If you find that exhaustion is already affecting your sleep, your emotions, or your family, please talk with a pastor or a professional; that is courage, not weakness.

VII. Living Out 321: The Holy Rooms and the Four Walls
@@19239@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the Most Holy Lord, yet he was willing to come among people, to eat, to wash feet, to sit at table with tax collectors. He prayed on the mountain, and he walked in the marketplace; holiness and daily life in him are not in conflict, but one life. What we learn is his pattern: focused before God, humble among people.
@@19240@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 42 not to draw an architectural plan, but to let God’s word measure us, cubit by cubit: In my daily life, is there a time and a place set aside for God? Have I taken holy things as my own halo? What the text does not say, we do not add; what the text says, we do not avoid.
@@19241@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who reminds us when to enter the holy rooms quietly and when to change our clothes and return among people. Each day, a small transition: from work to home, from pulpit to dinner table, asking one sentence: Lord, which garment should I wear now?
@@19242@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is laying down the holy garment of “I am a special person,” and also the torn garment of “I am nothing”; one who lets Jesus be King wears the garment he gives, and stands within the boundary he has measured.
@@19243@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. When the priests serve, the glory does not go to the priests; however orderly the holy rooms, however square the wall, the glory still goes to the Lord who dwells in the temple. If we can be set apart as holy, it is not because we are better than others; it is because he chose us, and the glory goes to Jesus.
@@19244@@
To build God’s system is to build a home with separation, order, and room: the temple is God’s presence, the holy rooms are the place of preparation, the outer court is the people’s daily life, and the four walls are the boundary of love. Life first, then relationships, then ministry. A community like this is not a high wall, but a wall with doors; not a ranking of garments, but an order of serving one another.
@@19245@@
What does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with “separating the holy from the common”?
@@19246@@
It has to do with “who sets the boundary.” Separating the holy from the common is not up to us; it is God who measured it, God who set it: with a rod he measured four sides, each five hundred cubits. To let Jesus be King is to give back to him the authority to define “what is holy and what is common.” We often set boundaries ourselves: this I can compromise on, that I cannot touch. But the true order comes from him. You can practice this: write down one thing in your daily life that is hard to tell apart, such as work, money, relationships, or entertainment; then ask before God: Lord, where is your boundary in this? Listen quietly. Sometimes the answer is not a sentence but a peace.
@@19247@@
The proud person says: “I am a priest, I am a coworker, I am more spiritual than others.” The text says to him: lay down the garments of ministry in the holy rooms, and put on other garments before going to the outer court of the people. Pride is leaving one’s position and grasping God’s authority; returning to one’s position is taking off the halo and walking into the crowd. You can start in one small place by letting someone correct you.
@@19248@@
The fearful person says: “If I don’t keep wearing this garment, I have no worth, and others will not respect me.” The text shows him a room set aside for changing clothes; God has prepared a place for this. The fearful person needs to learn to change “afraid of losing my identity” to “he has already given me my identity.” Starting today, find ten minutes in which you play no role at all.
@@19249@@
The ashamed person says: “I don’t deserve to be in the holy rooms; I don’t even qualify to change clothes.” The holy rooms were prepared for the priests, but grace comes from Jesus. The ashamed person does not need to fix himself first; today he only needs to put himself in the place that he has prepared. He prepared the place, and he prepared the garment.
@@19250@@
The wounded person says: “I served God for so many years, and was treated as a tool; I don’t want to wear that garment again.” God does not blame you for being hurt; he sees how much weight you carried in that garment. The wounded person can slowly find someone who truly loves you, and tell your story; the one who accompanies should not rush to say “you must lay it down,” but only say: I’m here, I’ll stay with you.
@@19251@@
First, each day find a fixed time for “changing clothes,” on the way home from work or before you go in the door; pause ten seconds and say to God: Today’s matters, I give to you. Write down the date, and remember the one thing you laid down.
@@19252@@
Second, at home or at work, find a small corner that belongs to God, with no phone and no talk of work; this week, sit quietly there three times, five minutes each time.

Third, find a friend and have a meal together this month, not talking about ministry, only about life. Say his name and the date, and ask him to remind you.

Fifth, for a brother or sister who is very tired in service and wearing too many roles, send one sentence: I see you; you don’t have to keep wearing that garment. Say the name and the date.

What is most special about chapter 42 is that it begins with a door facing north and ends with a wall on four sides. The focus of this lesson is not how high your role is, nor how strict your wall is, nor whether you have done everything right, but Jesus, who measured all four sides and who was also willing to walk into the crowd.

The exiles have an empty place in their hearts: “He has gone. Will he ever come back?” The glory left, station by station, and even the temple was destroyed. Chapter 43 does not first give the details of an answer; it first gives a direction: the glory of the God of Israel comes from the east, by the very road he left on, and he has returned. He does not come because people have tidied the house; he himself decided to dwell. He cleanses the altar first, and then says, “I will accept you.” If you have ever felt that God had left, or felt that you were not clean enough and did not dare to open your mouth, this chapter first says to you: he knows the road back, and he knows you.
