@@54@@
<p><strong>Even her security was hired.</strong></p><p>The ships were borrowed, the men were hired, and so was the army. The men of Persia, Lud, and Put, who came from other nations, served as warriors in the camp of Tyre; the men of Arvad stood on the walls; and the shields and helmets hung on the walls so that they “showed your beauty.”</p><p>Notice the verb “hung” (תִּלּוּ). The shields were hung up, not only for fighting but also for looking at. They were like a decoration: Look, see how many guards my walls have.</p><p>Twice the Hebrew says that they “perfected your beauty” (כָּלְלוּ יָפְיֵךְ). In verse 4 it was the shipbuilders who made her beautiful; in verse 11 it is the defenders of the city. The beauty of the whole city was outsourced layer by layer: outsourced timber, outsourced hands, outsourced safety.</p><p>Verse 10 says they “hung the shield and helmet in you,” and verse 11 says they were “on your walls all around” and “valiant men were in your towers.” Walls on every side, towers, shields, warriors, layer upon layer, and it looked watertight. But in this whole picture not one word mentions God. Her security was so complete that she had no need of him.</p><p>Application: we are also very good at “outsourcing” our security: the number in the bank account, the list of contacts, insurance, degrees. None of these is bad, but they are all shields hung on a wall. What can truly protect us is not how much hangs on the wall, but who is with us.</p>

@@56@@
<p><strong>God’s judgment is a judgment with a reason.</strong></p><p>“Because” and “therefore”: these two little words matter a great deal. God did not dismiss Egypt with a single “I have said so.” He made the reason clear: you said, “The river is mine.”</p><p>Sword, desolation, and ruins sound heavy, and we will not enlarge the picture. What this passage wants us to hear is not the instruments but the purpose: “Then they will know that I am Yahweh.”</p><p>The sentence repeated most often in the whole book sounds again here. Judgment is not meant to make people afraid, but to make them know. When people are most at ease, they often fail to recognize God; when they are torn most deeply, they suddenly see him.</p><p>Notice that in this passage there is not one word defending God. He simply lays out the facts: words that are spoken have consequences, and when the consequences come, it is so that people may look back and see what that sentence really said.</p><p>The last words of verse 9 are the reason for the whole passage: “Because he has said, ‘The river is mine, and I have made it.’” The river was what supplied Egypt. By saying he had made it, he turned the supply into his own achievement. God’s judgment begins from that sentence.</p><p><strong>Application:</strong> Is there something you are saying to God right now: “I will handle this myself”? Let him in, and do not wait until the day things break.</p>
@@57@@
<p><strong>Desolation is not the end of the story.</strong></p><p>From Syene to the border of Cush, this is all of Egypt. “No foot of man will pass through it, nor will any animal foot pass through it” is a very quiet picture: no travelers, no animals, no human voices. There is no need to enlarge the details. What the text wants to say is a land that has been made silent.</p><p>Then comes “forty years.” In the Bible, forty is often a period that is enough: forty years in the wilderness, forty days and nights. The forty years here are not meant to make us calculate, but to say: God has measured this time.</p><p>God himself set the limit. This limit lets people see that desolation has its season and is not forever.</p><p>It is worth noticing that “forty years” appears three times, and each time it follows “desolation.” This is not for emphasis, but to measure a stretch of road: if desolation has a length, it has an end, and so there is someone waiting at the other end.</p><p>Verse 12 says “a desolation in the middle of the countries that are desolate” and “among the cities that are laid waste,” meaning that among many desolations, Egypt is also counted. At the end of the same verse God scatters the Egyptians “among the nations” and disperses them “through the countries”; they leave their own river and live in other people’s lands. Desolation has two sides: the land is empty, and the people have left.</p><p><strong>Application:</strong> Are you going through a stretch of desolation that feels like “forty years”? Do not hurry to ask “when will it end?” First admit this: this time has a measure in God’s hand.</p>

@@59@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He was in the form of God, and did not consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men (Philippians 2:6--8). The cedar wanted to push its top into the clouds, but Jesus came down first by himself. For those who follow him, the example is not in the height of others, but in his low place.
@@60@@
<p><strong>They once terrified the living, and now they lie in a ring of graves.</strong></p><p>Verses 22 to 25 name Assyria and Elam. They were great powers of the ancient Near East, with armies, with fame, with a strength that made people tremble.</p><p>The text puts these powers in the same picture: each nation has its own graves, and each nation’s multitude is around it. Once it was she who struck terror; now she lies still.</p><p>The Hebrew repeats the words “terror” (<em>chittit</em>) and “shame” (<em>klimmah</em>). The first word says what they did; the second says what they now receive.</p><p>These four verses are structured like a roll call: Assyria, Elam, and each nation has the same words: “graves,” “her company around her,” “terror,” “sword.” The prophet deliberately uses dull repetition to create a vast, silent graveyard.</p><p>Verse 24 says they “have borne their shame with those who go down to the pit,” and verse 25 says, “They have set her a bed among the slain with all her multitude.” Someone has made up a bed for them, but it is among the slain. The power that once terrified people is now only a quiet bed.</p><p>Application: In the group or workplace where you are, if one day people mention you, will they remember that you once made people afraid, or that you once gave people rest? The question is very simple, and very hard.</p>
@@61@@
<p><strong>They went down with their weapons, and they went down with their iniquity.</strong></p><p>Meshech and Tubal are peoples of the north. The picture in verse 27 is of warriors going down to Sheol with their weapons, with their swords laid under their heads, so that the weapons of a whole life have become their pillow.</p><p>Then the text adds a very heavy line: “their iniquities are on their bones.” A person cannot take money with him, cannot take fame, but there is one thing he can take: what he has done.</p><p>Verse 28 suddenly turns to Pharaoh and says, “But you.” All the nations before were mirrors; now the mirror is turned around and shines on him.</p><p>Meshech and Tubal were distant northern peoples, thought of in those days as fierce and wild. Putting them on the list as well says that, civilized or rough, the end is the same. A sword under the head is the last posture of a warrior.</p><p>Verse 26 says they were “uncircumcised, slain by the sword,” and verse 27 says, “They will not lie with the mighty who are fallen of the uncircumcised.” Even in Sheol there are differences of rank, and the place arranged for Pharaoh is among these uncircumcised. The strength he relied on all his life has left him no special seat here.</p><p>Application: Our lives can leave many things behind, and some things will go with us. May what you carry down not be your own weapons, but a conscience that has been washed clean.</p>
@@62@@
<p><strong>“Woe” is the sound of a funeral; when God begins to speak, he first sighs.</strong></p><p>Verse 2 has a very simple question: “Shouldn’t the shepherds feed the sheep?” The answer needs no thought. A shepherd exists for the sheep.</p><p>The Hebrew word for “feed” (<em>ra‘ah</em>) is both a verb and the root of the noun “shepherd.” A shepherd is someone who does the work of shepherding. When the one being shepherded is changed to oneself, the word turns sour.</p><p>The tone of this chapter is severe. But let us not read it as an attack on certain people. The text’s own conclusion is that God himself will be the shepherd, and that is both an invitation and a comfort.</p><p>This chapter has a deep echo in the New Testament: when Jesus saw the multitudes, he was moved with compassion for them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36), and later he said, “I am the good shepherd” (John 10:11).</p><p>Verse 1 has only one sentence: “Yahweh’s word came to me,” and then verse 2 directly commands the prophet to “prophesy against” them. There is no build-up and no small talk. When those who were entrusted with the flock think only of themselves, this is how directly God’s word comes.</p><p>Application: Anyone who leads others can ask himself one simple question every day: What I did today, was it so that the sheep were fed, or so that I gained something?</p>
@@63@@
<p><strong>The fat sheep thrust with side and shoulder, and push with their horns.</strong></p><p>The action in verse 21 is very concrete: thrusting with the side, pushing with the shoulder, butting with the horns. This is not violence of words but pressure of bodies. This is how the weak sheep are pushed out of the flock.</p><p>Please notice that God does not say the fat sheep are “not good enough.” What he says is that they used their own strength to scatter the others. Strength itself is not the problem; the problem is how strength is used.</p><p>The end of verse 22 is the key to the whole passage: “I will save my flock.” The end point of judgment is not punishment, but rescue.</p><p>The “save” of verse 22 is the purpose of this judgment. God’s judgment is so that the weak may be saved, not so that the strong may be knocked down.</p><p>The tone of judgment is very heavy, yet its purpose is very tender: rescue. This is a manner of writing that recurs in the prophets: heavy first, then light.</p><p>Verse 20 says, “I, even I, will judge between the fat sheep and the lean sheep,” and verse 22 says again, “I will judge between sheep and sheep.” The judge is not an outsider but the owner of the sheep. He stands among the flock himself and looks at them one by one, and not one lean sheep will be missed.</p><p>Application: If you are one of the stronger sheep, this passage is not asking you to shrink back, but to learn to turn your shoulder around: use it to hold others up, not to push them aside.</p>
@@64@@
<p><strong>Speaking to the mountains, because even the mountains heard the shame.</strong></p><p>In chapter 6 the prophet was also told to prophesy to “the mountains of Israel,” and that time it was judgment. This time it is the same mountains and the same command, but the content is turned around into grace. The whole book makes a turn here.</p><p>“Aha!” (<em>he’ach</em>) is a cry of gloating. The enemy said, “The ancient high places are ours in possession!” They thought the mountains had no master and that no one would speak for a mountain.</p><p>But God told the prophet to speak to the mountains: “Hear Yahweh’s word.” What was taken away is called by name first; what was looked down on is spoken to first.</p><p>The “hear” of verse 1 has one more layer: mountains have no ears, yet they were told to listen. God’s word leaves a place even for what cannot respond. What is abandoned and laid waste is, with him, first treated as someone who can be spoken to.</p><p>The enemy’s words are recorded word for word in verse 2: “Aha!” and “The ancient high places are ours in possession!” God quotes the words that shamed his people inside his own words, which shows that he heard them all. Every word that was said about you, he writes down before he answers.</p><p>Hearing what people say about you and hearing what God says about you are two different things. When people have decided “it’s finished,” do not hurry to argue back; first listen to what he says.</p>
@@65@@
<p><strong>When the mountains were shamed, God said, “In the fire of my jealousy I have spoken.”</strong></p><p>God’s “jealousy” is not a bad temper; it is the other side of love. When someone tramples what he loves, he cannot be indifferent. This zeal burns for the ravaged land and the despised people.</p><p>Please notice the list in verse 4: the mountains and the hills, the watercourses and the valleys, the desolate wastes and the cities that are forsaken. They are named one by one, like a roll call. He calls out the name of every place that was trampled.</p><p>Verse 3 says the mountains were “taken up in the lips of talkers,” and became the evil report of the people. For someone surrounded by gossip, the most tiring thing is to have been given a name.</p><p>“Jealousy” (<em>qin’ah</em>) comes from the same root as “a jealous God.” He is a God whose heart hurts for those he loves until it burns. Only a cold God would never be angry for anyone; coldness is not love. When the mountains are trampled, it hurts him.</p><p>Verse 5 says those nations took his land “for a possession with the joy of all their heart, with despite of soul, to cast it out for a prey.” They regarded the land as ownerless, but God calls it “my land.” People say “forsaken,” he says “mine,” and that one word decides who the mountains belong to.</p><p>God names in a different way: he calls your name, not the nickname. Gossip can spread through the whole city, but only one person has the right to write a name.</p>
@@66@@
<p><strong>The ruins are built again, but what God says is not “restore to the way it was.”</strong></p><p>The verbs in this passage are all “multiply”: men multiply, animals multiply, and the cities are inhabited again. Then verse 11 joins two verbs together: “inhabited as you were before,” and “you will do better than at your beginnings.” First they come back, and then they go beyond.</p><p>The Hebrew word behind “do better” has the root “good.” God does not merely fill the numbers back in; he wants this land to be better than at the beginning.</p><p>This is where we most easily misread: what we hope for is often “getting back what we lost.” What God says is often not rewinding but rebuilding. The old days need not be the standard, and what was lost need not be lost forever.</p><p>There is one more detail: verse 11 says man and animal increase together. God’s restoration is not only the number of people; even the animals, the fields, and the cities are in his eyes. The range of his concern is wider than the list we write out.</p><p>The promise of verse 12 is more specific: “I will cause men to walk on you, even my people Israel.” The mountains were empty and desolate; now people walk on them, live on them, and receive them as an inheritance. The ruins are not only mended, but have footsteps again.</p><p>If you are standing beside ruins, please do not first ask “how do I go back to the way it was?” His promise may be bigger than what you are asking for.</p>
@@67@@
<p><strong>The nickname others gave the mountains, God tears off with his own hand.</strong></p><p>“You are a devourer of men, and have been a bereaver of your nation”: this is the name the nations laid on this land. Long ago, the spies who scouted Canaan said something similar (Num 13:32). Once a place has been given a label like this, it is very hard to wash off.</p><p>God answers one by one: “You shall devour men no more, and not bereave your nation any more.” He wants to replace the label with a new name.</p><p>Some people feel their heart tighten when they read the words “bereave of children.” Please know that this passage is not blaming anyone who has lost a child. What the text speaks of is a land falsely accused of being ill-omened, and God is clearing the name of this land.</p><p>Verse 15 says “any more” three times: no more hearing shame, no more bearing reproach, no more stumbling. Like three locks, opened one by one to let the bound person go free.</p><p>The “no more” of verse 14 is spoken to the mountains, and verse 15 says, “I won’t let you hear the shame of the nations any more.” The mountains cannot defend themselves or close their ears; they can only wait for God to act. If you are falsely accused and have no strength to speak for yourself, this promise was written for you.</p><p>We too are often labeled by ourselves or by others: “bad luck to others,” “useless.” God does not tell you to argue back; he wants to rewrite that sentence.</p>
@@68@@
<p><strong>God’s verbs come one after another: take, gather, bring, sprinkle, cleanse.</strong></p><p>Verse 24 first says “bring you into your own land,” and only then does verse 25 speak of sprinkling water. The order here is worth noticing: God first brings the people home, and only then does he wash them. He did not say, “First wash yourselves clean, and then I will bring you back.”</p><p>Sprinkling is the cleansing rite of Numbers 19, used on people defiled by death. Israel in exile was covered, among the nations, with foreign defilement and with their own idols. This is water they cannot use to wash themselves clean; someone has to sprinkle it on them.</p><p>Please notice that in the Hebrew the subject of “I will sprinkle” and “I will cleanse” is “I.” The people’s part is only “you will be clean,” which is to receive.</p><p>“Cleansing” here is double: from “filthiness” (what was done) and from “idols” (what was worshiped). The dirt on a person is of two kinds: some is what we do, and some is what we worship.</p><p>Verse 24 uses three actions in a row: “take,” “gather,” “bring.” Those scattered among the nations were found group by group; they were not left to find their own way home one by one. Before they were clean, God had already moved to bring them to his side.</p><p>If you feel that however much you wash you can never be clean, the invitation of this verse is not to try harder, but to stand where you are and let him sprinkle the water.</p>
@@69@@
<p><strong>The prophet was told to speak to the bones.</strong></p><p>A heap of bones with no ears was to hear “Yahweh’s word.” This is the absurdity repeated all through this chapter: speaking to those who cannot possibly hear. The prophet had to speak as he was told.</p><p>Please notice two layers of verbs: in verse 6, it is he who “lays,” “brings up,” and “covers,” and the prophet only opens his mouth. Sinews, flesh, skin, and breath all have “I” as the subject.</p><p>This picture recalls Genesis 2: God formed with his own hands and then breathed in, though there a different word is used (<em>neshamah</em>). The word used here, <em>ruach</em>, can mean breath, wind, or spirit.</p><p>The order in which this “breath” comes in is also the first reminder of this chapter: it is not that the bones are first put in order and then the breath is invited. What he says is, “I will cause breath to enter into you,” and the promise comes first, the process after.</p><p>Verses 5 and 6 both end with “you will live,” and verse 6 adds, “Then you will know that I am Yahweh.” The bones come alive not only to live, but so that people may know him. The purpose of resurrection is to bring people to know the God who gives life.</p><p>You and I also have many words to say to people who “cannot take them in”: a weary heart, a cold relationship, someone who will not turn back. Your part is to open your mouth. The breath is not your business.</p>
@@70@@
<p><strong>Prophesying to the wind: this is the second time of speaking to what cannot possibly hear.</strong></p><p>The first time was to the bones. This time it is to the wind. The wind does not belong to the prophet and does not obey the prophet, but when his word comes, the wind listens. “Come from the four winds, breath.” The winds of the four directions are all at his command.</p><p>Please notice: the object is “the slain.” These bones were people who died by the sword. God’s word does not leave behind even those who died by violence.</p><p>The result: they lived, and they stood up. Not lying there gasping, but standing up, and that is dignity. Those who were taken captive, slain, and despised now stand on their own feet, “an exceedingly great army.”</p><p>By now the prophet no longer asks “is it possible?” In verse 3 he said, “You know,” and in verse 9 he speaks as commanded. Faith is sometimes not having it all figured out, but being willing to open your mouth one more time.</p><p>Verse 10 has only one short sentence: “So I prophesied as he commanded me, and the breath came into them, and they lived.” The prophet spoke and the breath came, with no delay or explanation in between. All he did was say the words as instructed, and God did the rest.</p><p>John 3 puts wind and Spirit in the same word (it is one word in Greek, too). Prayer is sometimes speaking to the wind: he blows, and we live. The breath is always his.</p>
@@71@@
<p><strong>The people asked, “Won’t you show us what you mean by these?” A good symbol always draws good questions.</strong></p><p>The prophet did not explain first; he acted first. An action is silent, and when the people did not understand, they asked. Once they asked, the answer had a place to land.</p><p>His answer was: “I will take the stick of Joseph … and I will put them with it, with the stick of Judah.” Notice that verse 17 says “in your hand,” and here it becomes “in my hand.” The prophet’s hand became God’s hand. What he held was what God would do with his own hand.</p><p>There is one more detail: “before their eyes.” This was to be seen; it was not a secret matter.</p><p>The word translated “show” is, in Hebrew, “tell” (<em>higgid</em>). The people were not opposing; they were curious. Those who are willing to ask are often already ready to listen. Do not be quick to treat the people around you who ask “what does this mean?” as an interruption.</p><p>Verse 20 says, “The sticks on which you write will be in your hand before their eyes.” The sticks had writing on them, and what the people saw was two concrete pieces of wood and the action of holding them in the hand. A great promise is first made visible with a small thing.</p><p>Explaining faith to people often does not begin with doctrine, but with a life that makes people want to ask. When the one who asks comes, the words have a place to fall.</p>
@@72@@
<p><strong>One nation, one king, no more divided in two: the condition of unity is cleansing.</strong></p><p>Notice that verses 22 and 23 are joined. Right after saying “no longer two nations,” he immediately says “won’t defile themselves any more with their idols.” The root of the division is in the idols. When the northern kingdom split in the first place, it began with Jeroboam setting up the golden calves (1 Kings 12:28).</p><p>So unity and cleansing are one thing here. Unity that does not deal with idols is only living in the same place.</p><p>The verbs in the Hebrew are also worth noticing: take, gather, bring in, make one nation, save, cleanse. Every subject is “I.” From beginning to end, they did not “bring about” their own unity; they were only gathered, cleansed, and brought in.</p><p>Verse 21 says, “from among the nations, where they have gone,” and verse 23 says, “I will save them out of all their dwelling places, in which they have sinned.” What was rescued was not only leaving a foreign land, but also leaving the places where they themselves had sinned. The last sentence is “They will be my people, and I will be their God,” and this is the true center of unity.</p><p>When we pray for “unity” in the church and in the family, perhaps we should first ask: What idol is in my heart: face, being right, control? What lets idols divide people is often that in everyone’s heart there is an “I” sitting in the middle. Let man be man, and let God be God, and then there is one center.</p>
@@73@@
<p><strong><strong>God speaks first, and face to face.</strong></strong></p><p>This chapter does not begin on the battlefield. It begins with a command: “Son of man, set your face toward Gog, of the land of Magog.” The prophet is to turn his face toward someone who has not yet come on stage.</p><p>Then God says the heaviest sentence in the whole chapter: “I am against you.” The Hebrew is <em>hinneni</em>: Behold, I am here. It is not a judgment from far away; he stands across from him.</p><p>Notice the order. Gog’s troops had not yet moved, but God’s word had already arrived. All this king’s plans happen in the presence of a God who has known him all along.</p><p>One more point: Gog is a name, not a timeline. What the text gives us is a God and a man who is coming to attack, not a list that can be used to count days.</p><p>Look again at the opening of verse 1: “Yahweh’s word came to me.” The word came to the prophet; the prophet did not go looking for it. He only stood where he was and was called by name by this sentence. A captive, sitting by a river in Chaldea, was entrusted with a task of speaking to a king far away. God’s word can cross distance, arrive before the swords, and land first on someone who is willing to listen.</p><p>Application: Is there a name bigger than you in front of you, one that keeps you awake at night? Before you turn your head to look at it, first hear this sentence: the one who rules has already turned his face toward it.</p>
@@74@@
<p><strong><strong>War begins with a thought.</strong></strong></p><p>The previous verses said that God pulled him with hooks. Here the text turns the words around again: “things will come into your mind, and you will devise an evil plan.” Both are true. The hooks are in God’s hand, and the greed is in Gog’s heart.</p><p>His calculation is very clear: there are no walls, no gates, no bars; the people are at rest, dwelling securely. He did not see God; he saw only an unprotected target.</p><p>Interestingly, what he sees as “without defense” is exactly the peace that God set in place. He read peace as a weakness.</p><p>In verse 13, the merchants of Sheba, Dedan, and Tarshish ask him from a distance: “Have you come to take the plunder?” The merchants looking on see most clearly: all of this is for goods.</p><p>Verse 11 reads out his thoughts one line at a time: “I will go up to the land of unwalled villages. I will go to those who are at rest.” Two “I wills,” like a man pacing back and forth in his tent. The more certain he sounds, the more one thing shows: this campaign was not forced on him; he thought it out himself, step by step.</p><p>Application: Have you ever treated someone else’s quietness as something you could take? There is also a reverse version: Have you ever treated your own lack of defense as a shame? The text says it is not a breach, but a security God has given.</p>
@@75@@
<p><strong><strong>Judgment is not slaughter; it is God himself “entering into judgment.”</strong></strong></p><p>The end of verse 21 has a frightening sentence: “Every man’s sword will be against his brother.” This coalition is not defeated by an outside force; it falls into confusion among its own people.</p><p>The verb in verse 22 (<em>shafat</em>) is in the Niphal, which literally means “I will be judged with him.” It means that God will sort out the case with him face to face. It is not a single blow and done; he himself hears the case.</p><p>“Pestilence and blood, an overflowing shower, great hailstones, fire, and sulfur”: these words make us think of Sodom and of Egypt. The text uses the most familiar images to say that God’s judgment is not some new invention; it is his consistent way of acting.</p><p>This passage is very heavy, and we should read it slowly and not treat it as a spectacle. The text is not teaching people to look forward to certain people falling, but saying that God rules in righteousness.</p><p>Think again of the picture in verse 21: an army gathered from the four directions, with different banners and different accents, never of one heart. “Every man’s sword will be against his brother” means that at night one cry goes up, and the comrade beside you becomes the enemy. The swords they brought to attack others turn and fall on one another.</p><p>Application: If you are waiting for someone to get what is coming to them, please first hand this over to him. The sword is not in your hand. What you can hold in your hand is the next name to forgive.</p>
@@76@@
<p><strong><strong>The heart of this chapter is this phrase: my holy name.</strong></strong></p><p>The whole chapter, front and back, speaks of a war, but at verse 7 God turns the subject back to himself: “I will make my holy name known among my people Israel.”</p><p>War is the means; the holy name is the goal. What God cares about most is not winning, but how his own name is regarded.</p><p>The sentence “I won’t allow my holy name to be profaned any more” is the heaviest in this verse. To profane (<em>chalal</em>) is to treat what is holy as common. When Israel was taken into exile, the nations thought the name of Yahweh was common, the name of a defeated god. Here he says: I will not let this happen again.</p><p>Look also at the title: “the Holy One in Israel.” His holiness is not a distant separation; it is “in” Israel, in their midst. He is with his people, and his name is on his people.</p><p>There is another layer around this sentence. Verse 7 says “I won’t allow my holy name to be profaned any more,” and verse 8 follows right away: “This is the day about which I have spoken.” The name and the word stand side by side: his name will be honored because his word will not fall to the ground. If the holy name is despised, it is as if the day had never been spoken; if the day is fulfilled, the holy name is seen. These two sentences are joined together.</p><p>Application: You also carry God’s name. The way you speak, the way you handle money, the way you treat people: are they bearing witness for him? You do not need to be perfect; you only need to be willing to let his name be seen on you and not looked down on.</p>
@@77@@
<p><strong><strong>What the war leaves behind becomes firewood for daily life.</strong></strong></p><p>The picture in this verse is unusual: the people of Israel go out of their cities, not to fight but to “gather.” Gather what? The shields, bows and arrows, war clubs, and spears the enemy left behind.</p><p>These things once made people afraid. Now they are thrown into the fire and become wood for cooking and keeping warm. They do not even need to take wood from the field or the forest.</p><p>Notice the last sentence: “They will plunder those who plundered them, and rob those who robbed them.” It is turned around. Those who were robbed become the ones who take back. This is not the thrill of revenge, but God returning to his people everything that was stolen.</p><p>The number “seven years” is read in different ways by different interpreters. However it is read, what the text says is that this judgment is not wrapped up hastily; it is thorough.</p><p>Look also at the detail in verse 10: “so that they will take no wood out of the field, and not cut down any out of the forests.” The Israelites would have had to go out and cut wood with great effort for warmth and cooking; now the wood is right there, piled outside the cities. The iron and timber that were once a threat became the fire under every household’s stove, and even the housework by the hearth was changed by this victory.</p><p>Application: One day the things that once hurt you may no longer hurt you. You do not need to hurry to throw them away, and you do not need to pretend they were never there. God has a way to turn them into another use.</p>
@@78@@
<p><strong><strong>God’s response begins with a “now.”</strong></strong></p><p>The first word of verse 25 is “Now.” Before it was a long passage of judgment, and here God’s word turns: “Now I will reverse the captivity of Jacob.”</p><p>Three actions stand together: reverse (bring back), have mercy, and be jealous. Bringing back is an action; mercy is the inner heart (<em>racham</em>, whose root is connected with “womb”); being jealous (<em>qin’ah</em>) is fierce love. The fierce wrath that brought Gog down before now turns into passionate love for his people.</p><p>Then there is “I brought them back, calling them from the land of their enemies.” The subject of every verb is he. Israel did not crawl back; they were led back.</p><p>Next, they “will bear their shame.” Restoration is not pretending nothing happened. God does not tell them to hide their shame, but to face it in peace.</p><p>Look back at verse 26: “when they dwell securely in their land, and no one will make them afraid.” This echoes the picture Gog once fixed his eyes on: he saw villages “dwelling securely” and wanted to treat them as prey, while what God wants is to make this security truly the daily life of his people, with no one to frighten them again. The same words were a weak point in Gog’s eyes and a gift of peace in God’s mouth.</p><p>Application: If you want to come back to God but feel too dirty and too ashamed, please hear this passage: the road back is not one you build; he leads. What you do is turn around, confess, and be caught.</p>
@@79@@
<p><strong><strong>The farther in you go, the more steps there are.</strong></strong></p><p>From the outer court to the inner court, the prophet is again brought through the south gate. The measurements are the same as the outer gate: fifty long, twenty-five wide. But there is one small difference: the outer gate has seven steps, and this one has eight.</p><p>No one explains the extra step. The text does not ask us to explain it either. But the reader feels it: the farther in you go, the higher you must climb. Drawing near to God is not flat; it goes up.</p><p>The second detail is the porch: “Its arches were toward the outer court.” The gate of the inner court has its porch facing outward, as if stretching out a hand to those in the outer court. Those inside beckon to those outside.</p><p>Put the two details together: going in means one more step up, and those inside are also looking out at you.</p><p>There is one more picture not to miss: verse 28 says he was brought “to the inner court by the south gate.” He did not walk in by himself; he was brought in. Verse 30 then measures the arches, twenty-five cubits long and five cubits wide. The prophet had no measuring rod in his hand; he only followed, watching the one who measured, step by step, until the gate was clearly measured.</p><p>Application: If you feel today that drawing near to God is getting harder and harder, it is not necessarily that you have done something wrong. Sometimes there is simply one more step. You climb this step not to prove something, but because the deeper it goes, the more worthwhile it is.</p>
@@80@@
<p><strong><strong>Saying the same sentence three times says: there are no exceptions.</strong></strong></p><p>This passage reads most like repetition. The east gate and the north gate are both “according to these measurements”: fifty long, twenty-five wide, eight steps, palm trees.</p><p>An ordinary reader’s eyes skip over it automatically. But this kind of repetition is itself a message. It says that in this temple there is no crooked gate and no “second-class” entrance. Every entrance has been measured by the same standard.</p><p>God’s standard is not a different one for each gate; it is the same for every gate. This is fairness, and it is faithfulness. Once he said “according to these measurements,” he keeps doing so.</p><p>This also reminds us that faithfulness is often not spectacular; it is just repetition. Time after time, the same measurements, the same quality.</p><p>Look also at how this passage moves: verse 32 goes to the east, verse 35 to the north gate, and each time it is “he brought me.” The prophet did not choose the gate himself and did not ask why; he simply followed the one who led him, going around and looking around. Joined together, the route of the three gates is like walking around the outside of the inner court, looking at the entrances, each one open toward the outer court, waiting for people to come up.</p><p>Application: Is there something you keep doing, without change, without anyone praising it? It may be a fixed prayer, or a fixed kind of care. You are not wasting your time; you are “according to these measurements.”</p>
