I. A Proverb on Everyone’s Lips (18:1–4)
@@7918@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. At my old home there was a door that had never closed properly, ever since I was a child. Every time we shut it, we had to push it with a shoulder before it would lock with a “click.” Dad said: this door is just like this. My brother said: this door is just like this. I said the same to my friends. Then one year a carpenter friend came to the house, squatted down, took one look, and said: it is not the door, it is a loose screw in the hinge. He gave it three turns, and the door closed with a gentle push. I stood there stunned: for decades we had pushed with our shoulders, and the fix was only three turns away. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 18, and it also has a saying that is always on people’s lips, and everyone says “it is just like this.” Let us see where that one screw is.
@@7919@@
At the beginning of chapter 18, God asks a question: “What do you mean, that you use this proverb concerning the land of Israel, saying, ‘The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children’s teeth are set on edge’?” The picture is clear: the father eats unripe grapes, but it is the son’s teeth that turn sour. The exiles said this because they had real pain: the mistakes of the last generation had truly left marks on this one. God did not say, “You have no such thing.” He only asked: what do you mean by saying this? Because hidden in this saying is a conclusion no one says aloud: so there is nothing I can change.
@@7920@@
God’s answer first says: “As I live … you shall not use this proverb any more in Israel.” Then he says the most basic sentence of the whole chapter: “Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine. The soul who sins, he shall die.” Please notice the order: first belonging, and only then responsibility. He does not first ask, “What sin have you committed?” He first says, “Whose are you?” Your family line is not your master. The father is his, the son is his too, and every person stands directly before God.
@@7921@@
This sentence is two different voices to two kinds of people. To those used to passing the blame, it is a sobering word: you cannot keep pointing at the previous generation and saying, “It is all their fault.” To those who have been wounded at home, it is a word of comfort: you are not a stand-in for the previous generation; you have your own name, and God knows you. These two voices come from the same verse, and we must be careful not to reverse them: do not say to the wounded, “You also have your share of responsibility,” and do not say to those who pass the blame, “Your family has not affected you.”
@@7922@@
Exodus says that the consequences of iniquity reach the third and fourth generation, while chapter 18 says a son does not bear his father’s iniquity. Does the Bible contradict itself?
@@7923@@
No, they are speaking of two different aspects. Exodus 20:5 speaks of the consequences of sin: one person’s wrong extends to the family and the descendants, and this is real; the wounds of a family are passed on. Ezekiel 18 speaks of where judgment belongs: before God, each person faces him personally and cannot use the previous generation’s account as an excuse. So chapter 18 does not overturn the former; it corrects those who use the former to pass the blame. You can remember it this way: the consequences are real, the excuse is false. If there are wounds in your family, please do not feel, “I suffer because my family has sin, so I am condemned.” The text does not say that. The wounds in your family are real, and God sees them; at the same time, you can still choose a different road before him.
@@7924@@
II. Three Generations, Three Outcomes (18:5–18)
@@7925@@
Next God tells a story of three generations. The first generation is a righteous man. Verses 5 to 9 describe him: in worship, he does not bow to idols; in his relationship with his neighbor, he keeps the boundary; with money, he does not wrong anyone, he returns the pledge to the debtor, and he does not lend to his brother with interest; toward the poor, he gives his bread to the hungry and covers the naked with a garment. And the last line: “he is just, he shall surely live.” Please notice that this is a portrait, not an exam. It is not telling you to take a checklist, score yourself, and earn salvation. It lets you see that a righteous person is one whose whole life turns toward God, from the meeting hall, to the market, to the dinner table.
@@7926@@
The second generation is his son. In verses 10 to 13, this son becomes a robber and sheds blood; he walks his father’s road in reverse. The text says: “shall he then live? He shall not live.” The father’s righteousness is not a family heirloom. You cannot live on your father’s goodness, just as you cannot die for your father’s wrongdoing.
@@7927@@
The third generation is the grandson. Verse 14: “if he fathers a son, who sees all his father’s sins, which he has done, and fears, and does not such like.” This is the most powerful verse in the whole story. He saw. The things his father did, the consequences, the harm, the tears, were all before his eyes. Yet he did not do the same. Verse 17 says, “he shall not die for the iniquity of his father. He shall surely live.” And his father? Verse 18 says, “he will die in his iniquity.” God did not let the child’s turning excuse the father’s sin. Each person stands before God for himself.
@@7928@@
I know a middle-aged brother whose father had a violent temper. He said that as a child, what he feared most was the sound of the key in the door. Later he had children of his own. One day he found that his hand had been raised. He stopped. He crouched down and said to his child: I’m sorry, Daddy should not have done that just now. The child looked at him, stunned. He said that in that moment he understood that some things can stop with him. He did not blame his father, and he did not pretend nothing had happened in his childhood; he simply saw it, and then did not do the same.
@@7929@@
This story gives us great hope: after you see, you can turn. When you see a certain way of speaking in your family, a certain kind of silence, a certain habit, you do not have to do the same. This is not being unfilial; it is each person choosing a road before God. And this grandson did not become good by gritting his teeth. The text says he “fears”: he saw the weight of sin, and his heart was awakened. Turning begins with seeing.
@@7930@@
I cannot do a single item on the list of the righteous man. Does that mean I have no salvation?
@@7931@@
No. Verses 5 to 9 are a portrait, not an exam paper for earning salvation. What this passage says is that the righteous person in God’s eyes is not only someone who can recite Scripture and keep rituals, but someone whose whole life, including money, neighbors, and the poor, turns toward God. If someone takes this list to measure himself and ends up with a heart full of shame, it has been misused. You can read it this way: not “how many did I achieve,” but “which one can I first bring back to him today.” For example: what I owe someone, I can repay; when I see a hungry person, I can give a meal. Begin with one small thing. If you are weak, yet still want to draw near to God, this list is not rejecting you; it is pointing out to you that he wants the whole person.
@@7932@@
III. “The Soul Who Sins, He Shall Die”; Yet You Say (18:19–29)
@@7933@@
The people were still not convinced. In verse 19 they ask again: “Why doesn’t the son bear the iniquity of the father?” They wanted a guarantee: shouldn’t my family’s account be counted against me? God’s answer is in verse 20, the heaviest verse of the whole chapter: “The soul who sins, he shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son. The righteousness of the righteous shall be on him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be on him.” Please notice: this sentence is spoken to “passing the blame.” It is tearing down an excuse: “I am like this now because of them.” It is not spoken to the bereaved, and certainly not to the victims. If you have lost someone, please do not look in this verse for “whose sin it was”; if you have been wounded, this verse does not tell you to carry someone else’s sin.
@@7934@@
Then God opens the door. Verses 21 and 22: “if the wicked turns from all his sins that he has committed … he shall surely live. He shall not die. None of his transgressions that he has committed will be remembered against him.” “Not remembered” is a very great grace. Then comes verse 23, the gentlest sentence of the whole chapter: “Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked? … and not rather that he should return from his way, and live?” God is asking: do you think I enjoy watching people die?
@@7935@@
Verse 24 is a warning: “when the righteous turns away from his righteousness, and commits iniquity … should he live?” Some read this and are very afraid: can salvation be lost? There are several readings in the church: some see it as a warning to hold fast, not a declaration that salvation can be lost; some traditions hold that the text supports the possibility of leaving salvation; others stress Israel’s situation within the covenant rather than a doctrine about an individual’s eternal destiny. These notes set the views side by side and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. This verse is first of all spoken to those who treat past righteousness as a bank deposit and think they can spend it however they like. If you are weak, yet still want to return to the Lord, this verse is not speaking against you; please do not let this issue steal your peace.
@@7936@@
Then the people say twice: “The way of the Lord is not fair!” God’s answer is remarkable. He does not burn with anger, and he does not disappear. He says: “House of Israel, aren’t my ways fair? Aren’t your ways unfair?” He invites them to look at the scale once more. In a traditional market I once saw a woman selling vegetables. A customer said her scale was not accurate. She did not argue. She put the vegetables on another scale beside it and said: see for yourself. She dared to, because her scale was true. God is like this too: he is not afraid of being questioned. He says, come, let us look again together.
@@7937@@
There is a very deep comfort here: God is willing to be questioned. The text recorded this accusation, and he answered it; he did not remove it. If you are in bereavement, serious illness, or deep pain, you can come with your most honest question, and you do not need to dress it up to sound spiritual first. Only please notice: a question can be brought before God, but please stop at the place where you are willing to hear his answer.
@@7938@@
I am very angry at God right now and feel that he is unfair. Is this unbelief?
@@7939@@
Not necessarily. In chapter 18 the people twice say “the way of the Lord is not fair,” and God did not drive them away; he answered. So God is willing to be questioned. The difference is whether you come to him with your question, or leave him with your question. The former is an honest relationship; only the latter truly closes the door. If you have just lost a loved one, or are in serious illness or deep pain, you can say to God: I do not understand, I am in pain, I feel it is unfair. You do not need to change your words into a beautiful prayer first. Please also find someone you trust to sit with you as you say it out loud. If this anger and helplessness lasts a long time and affects your sleep and daily life, please find a pastor or a professional to walk with you for a while; this is not weakness, it is taking care of yourself.
@@7940@@
IV. Light from the Original Language: Soul, Delight, Turn Back (18:4, 23, 32)
@@7941@@
Verses 30 to 32 are the summary of the whole chapter: “Return, and turn yourselves from all your transgressions; so iniquity will not be your ruin. Cast away from you all your transgressions, in which you have transgressed; and make yourself a new heart and a new spirit: for why will you die, house of Israel? For I have no pleasure in the death of him who dies,” says the Lord Yahweh. “Therefore turn yourselves, and live!” There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth a close look.
@@7942@@
The first is “person,” the Hebrew נֶפֶשׁ (nefesh). Verse 4 says, “all souls are mine” (the CUV has “all people belong to me”); the “all” there is, in the Hebrew, “every נֶפֶשׁ,” every living person. In the second half of the same verse, “The soul who sins, he shall die,” the “one who sins” uses the very same word: הַנֶּפֶשׁ הַחֹטֵאת. Belonging to God and being responsible for oneself are the same word. You are his, so he invites you to turn; you are responsible, so you are able to turn. Belonging comes first, responsibility after.
@@7943@@
The second is “delight,” חָפֵץ (khafets), in verses 23 and 32. This word means “to be willing, to want in the heart.” The Hebrew of verse 23 says it twice: הֶחָפֹץ אֶחְפֹּץ, which is an emphatic way of speaking: would I truly delight? Delight in the death of the wicked? What God wants most in his heart is not your death, but your life. You can keep this word in your heart: he first delights in my living, and only then invites me to turn.
@@7944@@
The third is “turn back,” שׁוּב (shuv), which means “to turn around, to go back.” It appears several times in this chapter. “Turning back” is not an emotion; it does not depend on whether you have cried enough. It is a direction: from facing away, to facing him. The last two verbs of verse 32, הָשִׁיבוּ וִחְיוּ, “turn yourselves, and live,” sit tightly side by side. Turning back and living are two sides of one thing.
@@7945@@
Put the three words together: you are his (nefesh), he delights in your living (khafets), so you can turn around (shuv). As for the sentence in verse 31, “make yourself a new heart and a new spirit,” some ask: isn’t the new heart given by God? Ezekiel 36:26 indeed says that God gives the new heart. The position of these notes: the command is not a harsh demand but an invitation; turning is the human part, renewing is God’s work, and neither can be missing.
@@7946@@
Ezekiel 18:31 says “make yourself a new heart,” but I have tried many times and cannot change. Is it that I am not trying hard enough?
@@7947@@
It is not that you are not trying hard enough. This verse must be read together with 36:26, where it says that God gives people a new heart and a new spirit. The command in 18:31 points out the direction: you are to turn around; the promise in chapter 36 explains where the strength comes from: he renews. So this verse is not telling you to fix yourself by willpower. You can pray like this: Lord, I am willing to turn around; the step I can take is very small; please give me a new heart. Begin with the smallest step: lose your temper one time less today, say one more true word today, say sorry to one person today. If you are in depression or exhaustion and cannot even manage this step, please first let others walk with you; this verse is an invitation, not a pressure.
@@7948@@
The first deviation is to use “the soul who sins, he shall die” to reproach the bereaved, hinting that a loved one’s death is someone’s sin. This sentence is an answer to passing the blame, not an accusation against the suffering. We want the bereaved to be comforted, not judged.
@@7949@@
The second deviation is to use “each one’s own sin” to tell a victim, “You also have responsibility,” or to hurry the wounded to forgive at once and be strong. Chapter 18 does not ask the wounded to carry another person’s sin. We want victims to be protected and accompanied, not condemned again.
@@7950@@
The third deviation is to read chapter 18 as saying that the sins of the previous generation have no effect at all on the next. The text does not deny the real effect of sin on communities and descendants; it only denies using it to pass the blame. Set beside Exodus 20:5, the picture is complete. We want the congregation both to admit that the wounds of the family are real, and to believe that they can still turn around.
@@7951@@
The fourth deviation is to treat the list of the righteous man in verses 5 to 9 as an exam for earning salvation. It is a portrait, not an exam; and do not preach verse 24 as if salvation can be taken back at any moment, leaving people living in fear. We want the congregation to love the Lord more, not to fear being thrown away more.
@@7952@@
The fifth deviation is to use 18:31, “make yourself a new heart,” to tell people to change by willpower, without mentioning that God gives the new heart. Human turning and God’s renewing must be spoken of together. We want the weary to have the source of strength, not merely one more command.
@@7953@@
Chapter 18 says each person is responsible for himself. Do we still need to support one another?
@@7954@@
Yes. Chapter 18 speaks of responsibility before God: no one can push his own account onto another, and no one can pay off another’s account. But this does not mean “everyone sweeps only the snow in front of his own door.” Elsewhere the Bible clearly says to bear one another’s burdens and to weep with those who weep. What chapter 18 tears down is “passing the blame,” not “caring for one another.” You can tell the difference this way: am I using this chapter to say, “Your business is your own responsibility, and it has nothing to do with me,” or to say, “I will face God with you”? The former is a misuse; the latter fits the heart of this chapter. The beauty of the church is exactly this: each one faces God for himself, and we also walk together.
@@7955@@
The first question: we are used to “not airing the family’s dirty laundry” and to “our family is just like this.” Some wounds everyone at home knows about, yet no one speaks of them; some habits are handed down from generation to generation, and we call them the “family style” and dare not touch them. We also often read “filial piety” as “we may not change anything in the family.” But Ezekiel 18:4 tells us: all souls are God’s, including your father, and including you. Filial piety does not mean copying the previous generation’s bad habits. You can begin like this: pick one habit handed down in your family that you want to change, make a different choice just once this week, and say to God: Lord, I see it. This is not betrayal; it is each person choosing a road before God. If this matter involves violence or harm, please first seek a pastor or professional help, and protect yourself and your children.
@@7956@@
The second question: we are used to explaining everything by “fate.” “This is my fate,” “my ancestors did not store up merit,” “my birth chart is just bad.” When these words are spoken, they often come with weariness and resignation. We also often look at other people’s suffering through the lens of “karma,” trying to find “who did something wrong.” Chapter 18 asks us to change our eyes: all souls belong to God, and suffering is not a signed verdict. This is not belittling our ancestors, nor denying the influence of the family; it is saying that what God sees first is whose you are. You can examine yourself like this: this month, when others were suffering, did I think in my heart, “He must have done something wrong”? If so, please say one sentence to God today: Lord, I am willing to accompany him first, and not hurry to look for the cause.
@@7957@@
I grew up in a very hurtful family. Is chapter 18 telling me to forgive and not bring it up again?
@@7958@@
No. Chapter 18 does not rush you to forgive, and it does not tell you never to bring it up again. What this chapter says is: you are not a stand-in for the sins of the previous generation; you have your own name, and God knows you. The wounds you have suffered are real, and you do not need to settle “who is right and who is wrong” before God accepts you. You can take it slowly: first find someone you trust and tell your story, and you do not have to tell it all at once. Forgiveness is a very long road, not an assignment you must hand in today. If your situation is still unsafe, please protect yourself first and ask a pastor or professional for help. The most important sentence of chapter 18 for you is verse 23: he delights in your living. Let this sentence fall first into your heart.

VII. Living Out 321: People Who Are Called Back
@@7960@@
Jesus is my Role Model. When he met the man born blind, the disciples asked: who sinned, this man or his parents? Jesus’ answer did not point to anyone’s sin; he first saw the man, and then let God’s works be displayed in him. He looks at a person by first seeing belonging, and only afterward the past. Those who follow him learn his way of seeing: facing a person who suffers, we do not first ask, “Whose fault is this?” but first, “How can I walk with him?”
@@7961@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 18 not to judge other people’s families, and not to find a way out for ourselves, but to let God’s word point out the loose screw in “I am just like this”: verse 4, I am his; verse 23, he delights in my living; verse 32, so I can turn back. In reading the Scripture we must hold on to these three verses at the same time, not one fewer.
@@7962@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lets us see a certain habit in our family, and it is the Holy Spirit who gives us the strength to turn around. The command of verse 31 must be read together with the promise in chapter 36 that God gives a new heart: a person’s turning depends on the Holy Spirit’s renewing. Each day you can be quiet for a moment and ask one question: Lord, today is there some place where you want me to pause and turn around?
@@7963@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of the resignation of “our family is just like this,” and also letting go of the pride of “I will fix myself by my own effort.” A person who lets Jesus be King no longer clings to any reason of “it is all their fault,” and no longer sits on the judge’s seat judging “the way of the Lord is not fair.” He simply returns to his position: I am his, he is King, and I turn around.
@@7964@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we are able to stop the suffering that has been passed down in our family from generation to generation, let us not say, “I finally did it”; everything is because he delights in my living, and he gives the new heart and new spirit. Glory to the Lord who takes no pleasure in anyone’s death.
@@7965@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is made of people who were called back, one by one. God judges each person, and he also invites each person. One person who turns around can stop the suffering handed down in a family from generation to generation. In such a community, no one is labeled and no one is pushed out; the wounded are accompanied and those who turn are encouraged. This is renewal that begins with life: first life, then relationships, then ministry.
@@7966@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “turn back and live”?
@@7967@@
The relationship is direct. “Turning back” is giving the throne back to the King. When we say “our family is just like this,” we have handed the throne to the family tree; when we say “I will fix myself by my own effort,” we have handed the throne to willpower; when we say “the way of the Lord is not fair,” we have put the throne on our own judge’s seat. To turn back is to come down from these places and say: Lord, you are King. And what he says next is not “I will punish you,” but “I delight in your living.” You can practice it this way: every morning, say three sentences to Jesus: today, what is the reason I most want to hold on to; today, whom do I most want to judge; today, I give the throne back to you. Over time, you will find that turning back is no longer a great resolution, but a quiet turning, again and again.
@@7968@@
The proud type says: “I will fix my own road. I do not need anyone to tell me, and I do not need God to renew me.” The text says to him: make yourself a new heart, and let God give you a new heart. Pride is leaving man’s position, grasping God’s power, and wanting to take God’s place. Returning to one’s position is to admit: I am his, and he is the Lord who gives a new heart. You can begin with one sentence: “Lord, I am willing to turn back; please renew me.”
@@7969@@
The fearful type says: “If I turn around, will my family blame me? Or will God think I am too slow?” Verse 23 asks: “Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked?” He is not waiting for you to make a mistake; he is waiting for you to turn around. The fearful type needs to learn to exchange “fear of being blamed” for “being delighted in.” You can begin with the smallest thing: make one small different choice today.
@@7970@@
The ashamed type says: “My family is like this, and I am like this; I am cursed.” Ezekiel 18:4 says: “all souls are mine.” Shame says, “You are that person from your family.” The gospel says, “You are his.” The ashamed type does not need to make himself good first or deal with the family tree first; today, just listen to verse 23: he delights in your living.
@@7971@@
The wounded type says: “I have been hurt so deeply, and now you tell me to turn back and take responsibility?” God does not blame you for being wounded, and he does not ask you to carry anyone else’s sin. Verse 20 says clearly that you are not a stand-in for the previous generation. The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story; and those who accompany them should not hurry to say, “You must forgive,” but only say: “I am here, I will walk with you.”
@@7972@@
First, every evening spend three minutes saying to God: “You delight in my living, and I am yours.” And mark a sign on the calendar, for seven days in a row.
@@7973@@
Second, write down one “I am just like this” that you often say, and next to it write: “I am his, and I can turn around one small step.” Say whom you are willing to tell.

@@7975@@
Fourth, greet one person in your family who is going through hardship with one sentence: “I thought of you. How have you been lately?” Name the person and the date, and give no advice.
@@7976@@
Fifth, find one person you have viewed with “he must have done something wrong,” and pray for him three times this week, saying his name.

I declare: I am God’s, he delights in my living, and I am not a stand-in for the previous generation.
@@7978@@
I declare: I will no longer say “I am just like this”; I have seen it, and I am willing to turn around one small step.
@@7979@@
I declare: I will not use “it is all their fault” as my reason, and I will not use “I will do it by myself” as my pride; I return before him.
@@7980@@
I declare: I am willing to accompany the suffering first, and not hurry to ask whose fault it is; God delights in people turning back and living, and so do I.
@@7981@@
The simplest thing is this: tonight before bed, take a piece of paper and write one sentence: The “just like this” I say most often is ______. No need to polish it, and no need to show it to anyone. Then say to God: Lord, I am yours, and I see it. Next choose one small action for tomorrow: the next time that habit flares up, do just one thing: stop for three seconds and say, “Lord, I see it.” That is all; there is no need to solve it. Or send a message to someone in your family who is going through hardship, and write only: I thought of you. At the next small group meeting, you only need to say: this week I paused once. The answer of chapter 18 begins with one small pause.
@@7982@@
What is most distinctive about chapter 18 is that it begins with a proverb and ends with an invitation. At the beginning, the exiles say: the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children’s teeth are set on edge; in the middle, God tells the story of three generations, showing that every person stands before him himself; at the end, he says: “I have no pleasure in the death of him who dies. Therefore turn yourselves, and live!” From passing the blame, to turning around; from “just like this,” to “why will you die?” This is a road from resignation to life.
@@7983@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that we are his: he spoke of belonging first, and only then of responsibility. God also wants us to see that he delights in our living: he is not looking for people to condemn, he is waiting for people to turn around. That God, in Jesus, came among us; he is the Lord who first sees belonging when he looks at a person, and the Lord who gives people a new heart and a new spirit.
@@7984@@
So please remember the three key points of this lesson: all souls are his; the soul who sins, he shall die; he delights in your turning back and living. The focus of this lesson is not how heavy your family tree is, not what others say about you, and not how many times you have changed, but Jesus, who delights in your living.

Lord, thank you that we all belong to you. Forgive us for saying “just like this,” and forgive us for using other people’s wrongs as excuses. Heal the wounds in our families, give us a new heart and a new spirit, make us willing to turn around, and make us willing to accompany everyone who is suffering. @@8014@@
Some losses cannot be reasoned away. The leader changes, the system changes, a whole set of people changes, and the same thing happens again; you stand to the side and watch the people you raised, lifted up, and hoped for being led away one by one. What you want to ask is not “whose fault is it?” but “what can I do?” Chapter 19 does not hurry to answer. God first tells the prophet to sing a lament: a lioness raises two young lions, both learn to devour men, and both are taken away by nets and hooks; then the mother becomes a vine by the waters, lifted high and flourishing, yet plucked up, planted in the wilderness, and burned up by fire. This song gives no conclusion, only a place to stand: first admit that this is a lamentation, and allow people to weep over what is not yet well.
@@8015@@
Before judgment comes, God first sings a lament for his people: he sees the loss, and he allows us to weep; a scepter lifted high that is not in his hand will break in the end.
@@8016@@
God told the prophet to sing a lament for the princes of Israel. A lioness raises a young lion, and he learns to devour men; the nations pull him with hooks to Egypt. She raises another, and he too is caught in a net and taken to Babylon, and his voice is heard no more on the mountains. Then the mother becomes a vine by the waters, its strong branches fit to be scepters for rulers, but in fury it is plucked up and planted in the wilderness, and fire goes out from its own branches and consumes the scepter. The whole song stops at “This is a lamentation,” and gives no conclusion.
@@8017@@
A lament (קִינָה) is a funeral song sung for someone who has not yet died: God sees the loss one step before us, and weeps over it first, before he speaks of judgment and renewal.

What you rely on now: is it a voice that can be taken away, or a calling that cannot be taken away?</p>

<p><strong>① בְּדָמְךָ: how is this word read?</strong></p><p>After “like a vine” in verse 10, the text has a hard word, בְּדָמְךָ, literally “in your blood.” The CUV does not translate it.</p><ul><li><strong>“In your blood”</strong> (literal): the literal reading, with a meaning that is hard to determine</li><li><strong>“In your vineyard” or “in your likeness”</strong>: emended readings</li><li><strong>Leave it untranslated</strong>: treat it as an uncertain word</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: it does not affect the picture.</strong> A vine by the waters, its branches full: this is the main tone of verses 10 and 11.</p></blockquote>
@@8060@@
<p><strong>The picture in the song changes: the lioness becomes a vine.</strong></p><p>The prophet suddenly turns the image. The mother is no longer a beast, but a tree, planted by the waters, branches full, heavy with fruit. This is a picture of something well raised.</p><p>Look at its proudest part: “strong branches for the scepters of those who ruled.” The tree’s strength is not in the fruit, but in the branches. It could produce a rod straight and hard enough for a ruler to hold. It existed to bring forth kingship.</p><p>This is an important hint: this tree was a success; it really did what it was meant to do. Its ruin was not because it was useless.</p><p>Compared with the low vine of chapter 17, this one was lifted high above the thick boughs, and people saw it from far away. It was the kind that is seen, looked up to, and counted as glory.</p><p>The prophet does not preach here. He only describes, setting the picture before you and letting you see for yourself: what is lifted high will one day also lie on the ground.</p><p>Landing: sometimes we worry that we are “useless,” but what we should worry about more is that we are too useful, so useful that we forget who planted us. Where does the water come from?</p>

<p><strong>① Whose “fury”? Who is plucking?</strong></p><p>The verbs of verse 12 are all passive (plucked up, cast down), and no subject is named.</p><ul><li><strong>God’s fury</strong>: the uprooting of judgment</li><li><strong>The enemy’s anger</strong>: the nations’ attack on the royal house</li><li><strong>Deliberately unsaid</strong>: the passive lets the wound appear first</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: the prophet deliberately leaves the verbs without a subject.</strong> He wants you to see the tree lying on the ground first, and only then ask why.</p></blockquote>
@@8063@@
<p><strong>A tree by the waters, planted where there is no water.</strong></p><p>Before this it was all long sentences: planted, water, fruit, branches. Here, the verbs all become passive: it was “plucked up,” “cast down,” “consumed.” No one says who did it.</p><p>This is the way of the song: first let you see the wound, and then ask the cause. There is fury, there is the east wind, there is fire, but the prophet does not name them one by one. He only lets you see a tree lying on the ground.</p><p>One sentence in verse 13 hurts especially: “Now it is planted in the wilderness.” The same verb, “planted”: in verse 10 planted by the waters, in verse 13 planted in the wilderness. The act of planting has not changed; what has changed is the place: from the fullness of water to a place without water.</p><p>Please notice that it is still “planted,” not thrown away. Planted in the wilderness is still planted.</p><p>Nor is the wilderness the end. In Israel’s history the wilderness has always been the place God himself led people through; it is only that in this song, people are first allowed to see the dry ground, and only later the water.</p><p>Landing: if you are standing in the wilderness now, you do not need to hurry to explain why. You can admit first: it is dry here. That is true.</p>
