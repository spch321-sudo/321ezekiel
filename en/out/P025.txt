<p><strong>The last word of this song is its first word.</strong></p><p>The song begins with “lamentation” and ends with “lamentation.” In between are a mother, young lions and a vine, and only the last line says it: all of this is a lamentation.</p><p>Look at where the fire comes from: “Fire has gone out of its branches.” Not from outside, but out of its own branches, and it devoured the fruit, and burned the very scepter that could have been held in the hand of one who rules. I. Sing for Those Who Are Not Yet Dead (19:1–4)
@@8355@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. There was a mother whose front door, at night, was never bolted with the last lock. A neighbor asked her, and she said: In case he comes back in the middle of the night and doesn’t want to ring the bell and wake everyone. Her son had left home many years before. She never said where he had gone, and she never said what he had done. She simply left that one lock unbolted every night. The first time I heard this, my heart tightened. She was not waiting for an answer. She was keeping a door open for someone.
@@8356@@
Chapter 19 is a song like that. God said to the prophet: “Take up a lamentation for the princes of Israel.” Please notice that at that time the kingdom was still there, the palace was still there, the king was still sitting on his throne, and no one had died. Yet God had already told people to sing a funeral song. Sometimes God weeps over a thing one step before we do. He saw the ending, and he did not wait for the ending to grieve.
@@8357@@
I would like you first to notice one thing: this song is not one the prophet wanted to sing. He was told to. God said: “Take up a lamentation.” And the prophet opened his mouth. Sometimes what God asks of us is not to give a talk of doctrine, but to sing a song, for others, and also for him.
@@8358@@
The song begins with a question: “What was your mother?” The question is not about the king, but about the mother. Then comes a picture: a lioness, crouching among the lions, nourishing her cubs in the middle of the young lions. She is not weak. In the pride she has a place and she has strength. Who is this mother? There are several readings. Some say she is the nation of Judah, or Jerusalem, or the royal house of David. Others say the prophet does not name her at all, and what he wants us to hear is the voice of “a mother.” These notes lean toward the last reading. She need not be given a name, because everyone who has raised a child recognizes her.
@@8359@@
Then she brought up one cub. Verse 3 says: “He became a young lion. He learned to catch the prey. He devoured men.” Please stop at the word “learned.” He was not born devouring men. He learned it in that place. When a place teaches only how to bite, the child learns how to bite. But he bit, and he himself must answer for it. The text does not mix these two things together, and neither should we. That the environment taught him wrong is true. That he chose to bite within that environment is also true.
@@8360@@
Verse 4 takes only one verse to carry him away: “The nations also heard of him. He was taken in their pit; and they brought him with hooks to the land of Egypt.” “Heard” is an ominous word. It means his name had spread and he had been marked. From being heard of to being led away was so fast that no one had time to react. Please imagine that mother standing by the roadside, seeing the child she raised with a hook in his neck. She cannot make a sound.
@@8361@@
What is most special about this song is its eyes. When we read the Bible, we often, without noticing, sit in the judge’s seat: this king was wrong, that king was wicked. But this song invites us down from the judge’s seat and says: First stand beside the mother. What she sees is not a charge, but a child. Every time, she hoped, and every time, she lost. The prophet’s voice is level, and not one word scolds the lion. This is not condoning. It is knowing that there are times when scolding is not the first thing to do.
@@8362@@
Why did God tell the prophet to sing a funeral song while the people had not yet died? Isn’t that too pessimistic?
@@8363@@
This is not pessimism. It is presence. The “lamentation” of 19:1 is a song sung when people are at a funeral, yet God told the prophet to sing it before the ending had happened. This shows that he is not pronouncing sentence from far away, but weeping close by. Elsewhere in the Bible there is a picture like this: Jesus wept over Jerusalem before it was destroyed (Luke 19:41). Please notice that a lamentation is not giving up. A lamentation says: I see it, and I will not pretend that nothing is wrong. If there is someone beside you who is going astray and you cannot yet change him, you can still weep for him once, and pray for him once. This is not useless. It is placing him before a God who knows him and cares for him.

II. Raise Up Another, Yet the Same Road (19:5–9)
@@8365@@
The mother’s hope was dashed. Verse 5 says: “Now when she saw that she had waited, and her hope was lost, then she took another of her cubs, and made him a young lion.” Look at her action: she did not stop. When the first child was taken away, she did not say, “I will not raise any more.” She lifted up another. This is not foolishness. It is love. A mother’s love will not let the place stay empty.
@@8366@@
But please listen to the result. Verse 6 says: “He went up and down among the lions. He became a young lion. He learned to catch the prey. He devoured men.” This sentence is almost word for word the same as verse 3. In poetry, repetition here is like a punishment: the second walked the road of the first. The mother thought the wrong one had been chosen, and that changing him would make it right. But when she changed, the road was still the same road.
@@8367@@
This picture is very familiar to us today. A new boss, and the atmosphere in the company is the same; a new manager, and the people in the department are worn down just the same; a new group of people, and the same thing happens again. What changing people cannot solve is the place that teaches people how to live. Chapter 19 does not say “changing people is useless.” It says: if that environment still teaches only how to bite, the next one who grows up there will still learn to bite.
@@8368@@
Verse 7 says he roared and the land was desolate; verses 8 and 9 say that the nations all around spread their net over him, put him in a cage, and brought him to the king of Babylon, so that his voice should no more be heard on the mountains of Israel. The words “no more be heard” are the heaviest part of this section. That roaring voice was now gone. We need not read it in a tone of victory. No one here is clapping. The mother can no longer hear her child’s voice. This is the weight of the song.
@@8369@@
Please think again about the mother. She raised the first and lost him; she raised the second and lost him again. What she was thinking was probably not “What did I do wrong?” but “Is there still a chance?” This is the truest picture of a mother: after failing, she has no time to sit and grieve, and goes first to do the next thing. But this song holds her back and says: Stop for a moment, and weep first.
@@8370@@
Who are the two young lions? Some think the first is the king taken to Egypt and the second the king taken to Babylon; others think the second is a different one; still others say the prophet deliberately gives no names, wanting us only to see “twice in a row.” The various readings are set side by side, each with its grounds, and these notes will not force you to choose. Just remember: the same mother, the same kind of learning, the same ending. What the prophet wants us to hear is this repetition.
@@8371@@
If leaders are changed and systems are changed and the same thing still happens, does that mean there is no hope?
@@8372@@
No. What 19:5–6 describes is the mother answering disappointment with “change one,” and the result is still the same. It does not say that God has no way. Changing people and changing systems are often the first road we think of, and sometimes it is truly needed. But if we only change, and do not renew lives, we only raise the next one who is the same. So the meaning of this chapter is to remind us not to put all our hope on the next person. True hope must be placed on the God who can renew the human heart, and the book of Ezekiel will say this, chapter by chapter, later. This week you can ask yourself: What is the thing I have lately been hoping to “change out”? Then ask again: Besides changing it out, what can I do for this thing first?

III. Light from the Original Language: Lamentation, Planted, Scepter (19:1, 10, 13)
@@8374@@
This chapter has three original words that can be turned into daily life in thirty seconds. The first is “lamentation,” the Hebrew קִינָה (qinah). Verse 1 says: take up a lamentation for the princes of Israel. This word refers specifically to a dirge sung for the dead. The last verse says again: “This is a lamentation, and shall be for a lamentation.” In the original it is the same word, once at the beginning and once at the end, wrapping the whole poem. You can use it this way: when you meet a loss that has not yet been made well, do not rush to find an answer. First ask whether this thing needs a song rather than an explanation.
@@8375@@
The second is “planted,” the Hebrew שְׁתוּלָה (shetulah), which appears in verses 10 and 13. Verse 10 says that the mother was formerly like a vine, planted by the waters; verse 13 says that now it is planted in the wilderness, in a dry and thirsty land. Please notice that both times the same word is used. By the waters, it was planted; in the wilderness, it was also planted. The verb of this word means to plant, to transplant. The meaning is: one who has been put on dry ground is still “planted,” not thrown away. This is the smallest and the most powerful word of this chapter.
@@8376@@
The third is “scepter,” the Hebrew מַטֵּה (matteh). Verse 11 says these branches could be scepters for those who rule, and verses 12 and 14 say that the strong branch was broken off, and that there is no strong branch to be a scepter to rule. This word is at once “branch,” and also “rod,” and also “tribe.” A branch grew into authority that can be held in the hand. But please look: it first grew from beside the water, and once it leaves the water, the scepter is a piece of wood that dries, breaks and burns. What is the “scepter” in your hand? A position, a talent, the esteem of others, all can be. Its source is the water, not you.
@@8377@@
Put the three words together and you have the journey of this chapter: first sing a lamentation (qinah), and admit that this is a true loss; then see that we are planted (shetulah), and that even the wilderness is in his hand; finally give the scepter (matteh) back to the source of the water. Lamentation, planted, scepter: three very short words, yet a road from weeping to entrusting.
@@8378@@
“Planted in the wilderness” still sounds very bleak. Can the word “planted” really bring people comfort?
@@8379@@
We must be honest: verse 13 does not say that the wilderness got better, and it does not say that this is God’s blessing. It only says that this tree was planted in a dry and thirsty land. But the same word also appears in verse 10, when it was planted by the waters. The original does not change the word, which means that in this story the waters and the wilderness were planted by the same hand, or at least that the same God saw both. This is not speaking well of pain. It is giving pain a place: you have not been thrown away where no one knows. If you are in the wilderness today, you can say to God: I know I am on dry ground. Do you see it? A prayer like this is honest, and it is allowed.
@@8380@@
IV. The Vine and the Scepter (19:10–14)
@@8381@@
In the second part of the song, the picture changes. The mother is no longer a lioness, but a vine. Verse 10 says: “Your mother was like a vine in your blood, planted by the waters. It was fruitful and full of branches by reason of many waters.” (The CUV has “your mother was like a vine, exceedingly flourishing, planted by the waters.”) This is a beautiful picture, and every farmer would be pleased. Please notice that the tree’s flourishing has a source: water. It is not that the tree is so capable, but that the place where it was planted was good.
@@8382@@
Verse 11 says: “It had strong branches for the scepters of those who ruled. Their stature was exalted among the thick boughs.” This verse reads with a sense of achievement. The tree put forth a branch that could be a scepter of authority, higher than all the other branches. This is the moment a person is most pleased: capable, in position, seen. But please notice that this verse does not say one word that it did wrong. It only grew tall, and grew very well.
@@8383@@
The turn in verse 12 comes very fast: “But it was plucked up in fury. It was cast down to the ground, and the east wind dried up its fruit. Its strong branches were broken off and withered. The fire consumed them.” The text does not say why, only “in fury.” This verse has many verbs: plucked up, cast down, dried up, broken off, consumed, like a series of shots. We need not enlarge this picture. We need only remember: a scepter that is lifted high, if it is not in God’s hand, will in the end break.
@@8384@@
Here is a very quiet reminder about “success.” We often take flourishing as a sign of being blessed, and the wilderness as a sign of being cast off. But this song sets the two together: the same tree, first by the waters, then in the wilderness. It is not saying that flourishing is wrong, or that the wilderness is a punishment, but this: we must not plant our roots in “flourishing,” but in the water.
@@8385@@
Verse 14 says again: “Fire has gone out of its branches. It has devoured its fruit.” The fire came from its own branches. This sentence is heavy. What destroyed it was not only the east wind from outside, but also the fire from within. The place where a person is strongest, if it is not in God’s hand, may be the first place to catch fire. This is not meant to frighten. It is a very honest observation: the scepter we lean on most tightly is also the one we most need to hand over.
@@8386@@
The vine was plucked up, and the text says “in fury.” Is this saying that everyone who suffers is being punished by God?
@@8387@@
No. Verse 12 is about the last kings and the nation of Judah, saying why they came to this point. It is not a judgment on every person who suffers. The Bible has clear counterexamples: Job’s suffering was not because he sinned; and Jesus also said that those killed when the tower fell were not worse offenders than all the other people (Luke 13:4). So when we read this song, we should not use it to ask a suffering person: What did you do wrong? If you yourself are in the wilderness, you also need not immediately ask “Am I being punished?” You can ask first: What have I been leaning on lately? To ask this way is to seek, not to condemn.
@@8388@@
The first deviation is to preach this lamentation as mockery of a certain king or a certain person, as if saying he deserved it. The prophet is walking in a funeral, not watching a spectacle; in the whole poem not one word scolds the lion. We want the congregation to be better able to weep for the lost, not better at blaming others.
@@8389@@
The second deviation is to preach “learned to catch the prey and devoured men” as entirely the environment’s fault, with the person bearing no responsibility; or the opposite, as entirely the person’s own sin, with the environment having no influence. Chapter 19, verses 3 and 6, say “learned,” and also say “devoured men,” setting the two side by side. Chapter 18 has just said that each person bears the responsibility for his own sin, and chapter 19 goes on to say that the environment truly does teach people. We want the congregation neither to make excuses nor to treat lightly the way the environment shapes people.
@@8390@@
The third deviation is to say to a person who is in the middle of loss: God has a good plan, so don’t cry. The God of this song is the God who first tells people to sing a funeral song, and he did not tell the mother not to weep. Hurrying to comfort is often a way of keeping ourselves from feeling uncomfortable. We want those who are hurting to have a place to weep, and not to feel even more that their weeping is wrong.
@@8391@@
The fourth deviation is to take the vine’s flourishing as the standard of blessing, and being planted in the wilderness as proof that God has forsaken it. Verses 10 and 13 use the same word “planted,” and both flourishing and dryness are before his eyes. We want the congregation not to take success as proof of God’s presence, and not to take the wilderness as proof of his absence.
@@8392@@
The fifth deviation is to force the two young lions and the vine to match some person, or to arrange them in a timetable, or even to point to someone today. The text itself gives no names, and commentators read it in different ways. We want the congregation to hear that the point of this song is loss and the scepter, and not to fit it onto people one by one.
@@8393@@
Chapter 18 says each person is responsible for his own sin, and chapter 19 says the lion “learned.” So does a person have to be responsible or not?
@@8394@@
Both. It is not a choice of one or the other. Chapter 18 is the answer to shifting blame: do not say “the fathers ate sour grapes” and push your own responsibility out of the door. Chapter 19 is the answer to indifference: do not think that those who have gone astray are simply bad, and fail to see the place that taught them. Verse 3 says he “learned,” which admits the influence; it says he “devoured men,” which admits that he did it. So if you are the one who was hurt, this lesson is not asking you to carry the responsibility again; if you are the one in authority, this lesson asks you to see what your position is teaching people. For yourself, ask one question: What have I learned that now it is my turn to choose not to do any more? This question is not a condemnation, but gives you a road.
@@8395@@
The first problem: we do not know how to mourn well. We are used to saying “take heart and accept the change,” used to saying “let the past be past,” used to hurrying to hand over a “be strong” before another person’s tears even come out. Men especially are taught that tears are weakness. But the God of chapter 19 is a God who tells people to sing and to weep. You can start this way: this week, find a quiet place and tell God about a loss that you have been calling “nothing,” in one sentence, with no explanation. When you meet someone who is weeping, sit down first and say nothing. That is also a kind of comfort.
@@8396@@
The second problem: we easily take our children’s future as our own hope, and our children’s failures as our own face. When a child goes astray, our first reaction is not heartache but “how embarrassing”; or, the other way round, we hurry to lift up another, hoping this one will be different. The mother in 19:5 moves her hope from one child to another. You can examine yourself this way: With my child, or with the person I lead, am I loving him, or am I asking him to be my face? This week, say one sentence to him: No matter what, I am here.
@@8397@@
My child or family member has gone astray and I am in great pain. Is all I can do to weep?
@@8398@@
You may weep, and weeping is not powerlessness. But chapter 19 also reminds us of two things. First, the mother lifting up another child shows that “changing one” is not the answer, so please be careful not to shift all your hope from this child to the next. Second, the setting of the lamentation is the prophet standing before God. Your tears can also be brought to him first. In practice, what you can do includes: pray for him regularly, keep a contact that does not judge, and let him know the door has not been locked. If the matter involves danger, addiction or self-harm, please be sure to seek the help of a pastor and professionals, and do not carry it alone.
@@8399@@
VII. Living Out 321: This Is a Lamentation, and It Is Also a Planting
@@8400@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He wept at the tomb of Lazarus, though he knew he would raise him, still weeping first with those who mourn; he also wept over Jerusalem. Those who follow him learn his way: not hurrying to reason, but standing first beside those who have lost. The prophet of chapter 19 did the same: he sang first, and spoke later.
@@8401@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 19 not to analyze who the two lions are, but to let God’s word invite us down from the judge’s seat to stand beside the mother. Verse 14 says: “This is a lamentation, and shall be for a lamentation.” A standard is not for condemning others, but for teaching us to see loss with God’s eyes.
@@8402@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. When people are in the greatest pain, they often cannot speak, and cannot even pray in order. The Holy Spirit is right there, groaning with us. You do not need beautiful words as soon as you open your mouth. Just say to him: Lord, this really hurts. Then stop for one minute, and listen for his presence.
@@8403@@
Let Jesus be King. The scepter of verse 11 is the scepter of those who rule. Selflessness is putting the scepter I lean on most and am proudest of back into his hand. That scepter can be a position, a talent, the esteem of others, or a place I have always leaned on. When he is King, I no longer need to use force, bite or lift myself high to prove that I am alive.
@@8404@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we come out of the wilderness, let us not say “I finally made it through.” We were planted, the water was given by him, and the branches were nourished by him. All the glory goes to the God who plants.
@@8405@@
To build God’s system. Chapter 19, verses 5 and 6, say that when people are changed, the same environment still teaches the same devouring. God’s system does not depend on changing one person or changing one set of rules, but on lives being renewed: life first, then relationships, then ministry. A group that belongs to God must first learn to weep and learn humility, so that it will not raise the next young lion. In such a group, those who have lost have a place to weep, and those who have gone astray have a door to come back through.
@@8406@@
“Selflessness” and “allowing yourself to weep” seem to contradict each other. How should we understand this?
@@8407@@
They do not contradict. Selflessness is not having no feelings, and not flattening yourself; selflessness is putting down the scepter of “I am in charge.” Weeping is admitting that I am limited and cannot control this thing. So allowing yourself to weep is exactly a step of selflessness: I no longer pretend that I can hold up. Jesus wept at the tomb of Lazarus (John 11:35). He was fully selfless, yet he did not suppress his tears. You can practice this way: the next time you feel “I should be strong,” say instead: “I am very sad right now; please stay with me.” This is not weakness. It is the beginning of putting the scepter back into his hand.
@@8408@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Lamentation
@@8409@@
The proud type says: “I have a position and ability. I don’t need anyone to lift me up, and I don’t need to weep.” The text says to him: that strong scepter grew from beside the water. Pride is leaving one’s position and grasping God’s authority, thinking the scepter is one’s own. You need not put down everything at once. First say one sentence to God: “Lord, this scepter is from you.”
@@8410@@
The fearful type says: “I am afraid of losing again. I am afraid that if I lift up another, he will be taken away too.” The mother’s fear is real, and her loss is also real. The fearful type should learn not to answer fear by “holding tighter,” but to say to God the name of the person they fear to lose. You can start by praying one sentence for him.
@@8411@@
The ashamed type says: “I am that lion who learned to bite. I am no longer worthy.” The text does not tell you to pretend you never bit anyone, but neither does it tell you to live forever in the cage. The word “planted” tells you: the one in the wilderness is still one who is planted. The ashamed type need not first make yourself right; just today, do not hide from him.
@@8412@@
The wounded type says: “I raised him, and he was taken away. I do not want to hope again.” God did not tell you to stop grieving; he gave you a song first. A wounded person can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story; the one who accompanies should not hurry to say “you must forgive” or “you must be strong,” but only say: “I am here. You may weep.”
@@8413@@
First, find a quiet time and spend three minutes saying one sentence to God: “Lord, this really hurts.” Say which thing it is, and write down the date.

The simplest way is to take a piece of paper today and write one sentence: One loss that has not yet been made well lately is ______. No need to polish it, and no need to show it to anyone. Then say to God: Lord, this really hurts. Stop for one more minute, saying nothing, simply sitting. Next, choose one small action you can do today: send a message to someone you miss and say: I thought of you. I am here. This action is the answer of chapter 19: first admit that this is a lamentation, and then let love have an outlet. At the next small group meeting, you need only say: This week I let myself weep once, or I accompanied one person. No need to say the details.
@@8423@@
What is most special about chapter 19 is that it has no conclusion. It begins with one sentence, “Take up a lamentation,” and ends with one sentence, “This is a lamentation, and shall be for a lamentation.” In between are two young lions, one vine, the net, the hooks, the cage, the east wind and the fire. The mother raised the children, the tree put forth a scepter, and in the end everything stopped in the wilderness. This song does not comfort people, yet it lets us know: God wept for us first.
@@8424@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is the God who sings the lamentation first; he wants us to come down from the judge’s seat and stand beside the mother; and he also wants us to know that one planted on dry ground is still one who is planted. This God, in Jesus, came among us, wept at the tomb, and wept over the city.
@@8425@@
So please remember the three key points of today’s lesson: lamentation, planted, scepter. The focus of this lesson is not whether you can get well at once, not who should be responsible for this matter, and not how dry the wilderness is, but Jesus, the one who plants, who weeps, and who holds the source of the water.

① Preaching “I will not be inquired of by you” as: your prayer has no answer because you have sinned. The text was said to people who held on to idols and still came to inquire, not to everyone who prays in suffering and gets no answer. Test: does the sermon make a suffering person feel “God ignores me, and it is my fault”? If so, change it.
@@8483@@
② Preaching “for my name’s sake” as God being self-centered, caring only about his face. It is that he staked his name on this people, which is the weight of a promise, and also faithfulness to people. Test: does the sermon speak of his name and his love for people together?
@@8484@@
③ Preaching “for my name’s sake” as an excuse for license: God will forgive me anyway for the sake of his name. Chapter 20 also has consequences: that generation never entered the land (20:15–16). Test: does the sermon honestly say that grace and consequence exist together?
@@8485@@
④ Preaching “statutes that were not good” (20:25–26) as God setting bad laws, or as God being the author of evil. The text follows “they rejected my statutes,” and is the shape of judgment: letting people go on the road they chose. Test: does the sermon first set the various views side by side, then give a position, and avoid making a horrifying picture?
@@8486@@
⑤ Using “all that opens the womb” passing through the fire (20:26) to dramatize a picture of child sacrifice, or to point to any modern group. Test: does the sermon enlarge the picture? If so, delete it.
@@8487@@
⑥ Preaching 20:18 “Don’t walk in the statutes of your fathers” as telling people to break with their parents and hate their family. God is speaking to the children; he is not telling people to hate their parents. Test: does the sermon let people think they must cut off their families?
@@8488@@
⑦ Preaching “which if a man does, he will live in them” (20:11) as salvation by works. The order is: first out of Egypt, then the statutes were given; the living is what he has already given. Test: does the sermon present obedience as a condition of salvation? If so, change it.
@@8489@@
⑧ Preaching the Sabbath only as a legalistic list of prohibitions. It is a sign: God makes people holy (20:12). Test: does the sermon say that “it is he who makes us holy”?
@@8490@@
⑨ Matching the return and promises of 20:33–44 directly to some modern nation or event and arranging a timetable. The text sets the various readings side by side, and does not require naming today. Test: does the sermon name a nation or set a timetable? If so, delete it.
@@8491@@
⑩ Using “loathe yourselves” in 20:43 to make people shame or blame themselves. This is said after being accepted (20:40–42). Test: does the sermon first speak of being accepted, and only then of remembering?
@@8492@@
⑪ Preaching the fire of 20:45–49 as a threat to the congregation, creating panic. “Isn’t he a speaker of parables?” also reminds us: the prophet was despised. Test: is the effect of the sermon that people love the Lord more, or fear more?
@@8493@@
⑫ Preaching “all the house of Israel, all of them” and “you will know that I am Yahweh” as a final verdict on any modern group. Test: does the sermon put a label on anyone? If so, delete it.

<p><strong>הֲתִשְׁפֹּט הֲתִשְׁפּוֹט——Is this a question, or a command?</strong></p><ul><li>A real question: “Will you judge?”, left to the prophet to decide</li><li>A rhetorical question with the tone of urging: “Won’t you judge them?”</li><li>An interrogative with the imperfect, in practice used as a command: “Judge them! Judge!”</li></ul><p>The CUV renders it “judge, judge.” The same repeated pattern appears again in 22:2, and in both contexts it comes right before the prophet is to list the charges.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: this is a summons.</strong> The form of a question, the force of a command. The prophet is sent to be the accuser, and the elders who came to inquire become, without knowing it, the accused.</p></blockquote>
