I. I Am Against You (38:1–9)
@@17262@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever had this experience? Before dawn, your phone lights up beside your pillow with a news headline of only a dozen or so words. You have not even opened it, and your heart is already tight. You cannot sleep again. You read one after another, and the more you read, the more awake you are. By morning you are more tired than before you went to bed. In our age big events come fast, bad news comes even faster, and every time it feels as if someone is saying in your ear: this time, it is really different.
@@17263@@
Chapter 38 is just such a “headline”: one name, one great army, coming from the north. The name is long, the force is huge, and even the reader wants to build a wall. So I want to say clearly the stance of this lesson: this chapter was not written to make you afraid. It was written so that you may know the God who is in charge. If, after this lesson, you love him more and have more peace, this lesson has not been taught in vain. If you fear the news more, and fear the world more, then we have gone off course.
@@17264@@
When God’s word came, the first sentence was a command: “Son of man, set your face toward Gog, of the land of Magog, the prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal, and prophesy against him.” The prophet is to turn his face toward a man who has not yet come on the scene. Then comes the heaviest sentence of the whole chapter: “I am against you, Gog, prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal.”
@@17265@@
Please notice the order. Gog’s troops have not yet moved, and God’s word has already arrived. All this king’s calculations take place before a God who already knew him. “I am against you”: this sentence is not a judgment passed from far away. He is standing across from him.
@@17266@@
Verse 4 goes on: “I will turn you around, and put hooks into your jaws.” A hook is the way you lead livestock. When we read the long list of distant peoples, with horses, horsemen, shields, armor, and swords, a question naturally comes up in the reader’s mind: with a force this big, who can stand against it? God’s answer is not a list of opponents. It is a hook. The direction of the army, the order of its appearance, are all held in another hand. Gog thinks he is marching forward. The text says he is being turned around.
@@17267@@
This chapter has been read in different ways down the years. Some say it is about an attack by the surrounding nations after the return, already fulfilled in history. Some say it is a war that will take place in the last days, to be read together with Revelation. Others say that Gog and Magog are a symbol of every force that opposes God’s people. Interpreters differ. We do not judge anyone, we do not name any modern country, we do not count days, and we do not draw up a timetable. We hold on only to two things that everyone sees: God himself protects his people, and he receives glory for the sake of his own name.
@@17268@@
Verse 8 says that in the latter years Gog will come into “the land that is brought back from the sword” and “gathered out of many peoples,” and “they will dwell securely, all of them.” You see, the text turns its eyes to the people who are attacked: they are the ones who were gathered. They did not climb back by themselves. They were called back. Their security is not because the world is quiet. It is because Someone called them back.
@@17269@@
Words like “After many days” and “In the latter years” are often taken by people to draw charts. The text itself gives no date at all. It says only: at that time, this is how things will stand. The storm will come, but the people God has gathered live securely. If people spend their strength counting days, they easily miss the very ones the text is comforting. This week you can practice this: count a little less, and dwell a little more.
@@17270@@
When I see words like “Gog” and “the latter days,” my heart gets very tight. What should I do?
@@17271@@
First, admit that your tension is real. You do not need to blame yourself for not having enough faith. Then put the point of this chapter back in place: the first word is “I am against you,” so God has turned his face first. Verse 8 says the people who are attacked are the ones he gathered. You can do this: when you cannot keep reading, stop, breathe slowly three times, and say to God: Lord, you are in charge. Then decide whether to continue. If you have lived through war or violence, and your body reacts as soon as you hear such scenes, please do not force yourself to keep listening, and seek help from a pastor or a professional counselor. Your body is protecting you. This is not weakness.
@@17272@@
II. The Unwalled Villages (38:10–16)
@@17273@@
At the start of verse 10, the text turns the word around again: “It will happen in that day that things will come into your mind, and you will devise an evil plan.” In the last section, God pulled him with a hook. In this section, the intention rises in his own heart. Both are true: the hook is in God’s hand, and the greed is in Gog’s heart. We cannot pull these two sentences apart and explain them neatly. We can only receive them together. God’s sovereignty does not excuse the wicked, and it does not ask the victim to stay silent.
@@17274@@
Listen to his scheming: “I will go up to the land of unwalled villages. I will go to those who are at rest, who dwell securely, all of them dwelling without walls, and having neither bars nor gates.” No walls, no gates, no bars, a people at rest. He did not see God. He saw only a target with no defense. Interestingly, the “defenselessness” in his eyes is exactly the peace God has put in place. He read peace as a weak spot.
@@17275@@
What does he want? “To take the plunder and to take prey”: silver and gold, livestock, goods. He looks at things, but God looks at people. He calculates how much he can seize, but God says: my people. Even bystanders can see it: Sheba, Dedan, and the merchants of Tarshish, standing at a distance, asked him one thing: “Have you come to take the plunder?” People in business often see most clearly: all of this, plainly put, is for what is in someone else’s hands.
@@17276@@
This section asks us two questions. The first: have you ever treated someone else’s quietness as something you could take? Someone else’s trust, someone else’s kindness, someone else’s weakness: have you, without realizing it, treated it as a gap in the wall? The second is the reverse: have you treated your own “lack of defense” as something shameful? Do you often think: how could I be so foolish, why didn’t I keep something in reserve? The text says that is not a gap in the wall. That is the security God has given.
@@17277@@
Perhaps you will ask: does that mean we need no protection at all? We can leave the door unlocked and ignore our money? No. The “security” here is not foolishness. It is a steadiness, with something in the heart to lean on. You may lock your door, buy insurance, and be careful. Only do not let your heart treat these walls as God. Please also remember: if someone is harmed when they were not on guard, the one at fault is the one who struck, not the one who had built no wall.
@@17278@@
In verse 14, God’s word begins with a question: “In that day when my people Israel dwells securely, will you not know it?” It means: Gog, on that day, how could you not know that I am there? In verse 16, the purpose of the whole story is spoken: “It will happen in the latter days, that I will bring you against my land, that the nations may know me, when I am sanctified in you, Gog, before their eyes.” All the armies before it, the hooks, the north, the horses, are placed in front of this purpose: “that the nations may know me.” All of this is for the sake of “knowing,” not for the sake of war.
@@17279@@
God leads Gog with a hook. Was the person who was harmed also “arranged” by God?
@@17280@@
No. The text sets two things side by side: evil intent rose in Gog’s heart, and he must answer for it; and God truly reigns, so that even the greatest coalition is under his hook. God’s sovereignty is for comforting those who suffer. It is not for demanding that the victim stay silent, and it is certainly not for saying, “This is God’s arrangement, and you must accept it.” If you have been hurt, you can honestly say to God: this should not have happened. He hears you, and he will not say that you lack faith. Take your time, do not rush to a conclusion, and if you need to, ask a pastor or a professional to walk with you for a while. You are not alone, and you do not have to figure it out alone.
@@17281@@
III. I Will Magnify Myself and Sanctify Myself (38:17–23)
@@17282@@
In verse 17, God’s word suddenly stops and asks a question: “Are you he of whom I spoke in old time by my servants the prophets of Israel, who prophesied in those days for years that I would bring you against them?” This verse is not easy, and interpreters read it differently. We do not need to add anything to it. But one thing is very clear: God spoke to his servants not once, but for years. He is not hurried, and he is not confused. We often feel that things “happen suddenly,” but in fact God had already prepared us in his word.
@@17283@@
Then come the hottest verses in this chapter. Verse 18 says that his wrath will come up into his nostrils. That is how the ancients described an angry face: the heat of the face wells up from within. The word “wrath” in verse 19 also carries the sense of “jealousy.” He is not out of control. He is saying: these are my people, this is my land, you may not touch them. This is a heat that burns for those he loves, not the temper of a tyrant.
@@17284@@
Then the created world appears one by one: the fish of the sea, the birds of the sky, the animals of the field, the creeping things of the earth, and people too, all shaking at his presence. People are not at the front. People are only one of the created things. Before God’s face, mountains, birds, and people stand in the same row. Not one is so great that it need not be afraid, and not one is so small that he forgets it. The last sentence of verse 20: “Every wall will fall to the ground.” In Gog’s eyes it was the place with no walls. On that day, even all the walls that people lean on will no longer be the final thing to lean on.
@@17285@@
In verses 21 and 22, the text describes judgment in very heavy language: sword, pestilence, blood, an overflowing shower, great hailstones, and fire. We do not magnify these pictures, and we should not treat them as “waiting for someone to be repaid.” This is God’s doing. Judgment belongs to God. It is not a weapon in human hands. If we have enemies, we hand the case to him. If we are wounded, we tell him about our wounds.
@@17286@@
The conclusion of the whole chapter is in verse 23: “I will magnify myself, and sanctify myself, and I will make myself known in the eyes of many nations. Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” Look at the verbs in this chapter. They are all his: I will magnify myself, I will sanctify myself, I will make myself known. Our role, from beginning to end, is not to read the situation clearly or to block the storm. Our role is to know him.
@@17287@@
In Ezekiel 38 there is wrath and judgment. Can I look forward to my enemies being repaid?
@@17288@@
Judgment belongs to God. The purpose of this chapter is “Then they will know that I am Yahweh” (verse 23), not human revenge. If we look forward to someone being repaid, we are taking God’s work and using it as our own weapon, and that makes our hearts harder. You can do this: write that person’s name on a piece of paper and say to God: this case, I hand over to you. Then hand over your wound to him as well. If you are being hurt right now, these verses are not asking you to hate more, and they are not asking you to forgive at once. You can take your time. Go as slowly as your own heart can keep up with. God is not hurrying you.
@@17289@@
IV. Light from the Original: Hook, Security, Shown Holy (38:4, 11, 23)
@@17290@@
There are three words in the original that are worth looking at slowly. The first is “hook,” חַחִים (khakhim), verse 4. This is a ring or hook for the nose or jaws of an animal. In ancient times it was used to lead livestock, and also to lead captives. This word reminds us: Gog looks as if he is driving a great army, but before God he is the one being led. However great the show of force, it is under one hand.
@@17291@@
The second is “security,” בֶּטַח (betakh), which appears in verses 8, 11, and 14. The text writes it as לָבֶטַח (lavetakh), meaning “securely, with something to lean on.” The word does not mean there is no danger. It means there is a place to lean. You may lock your door and be careful, but your true steadiness is in leaning. It appears three times, and each time it is the same word: those whom God has gathered live leaning on him. Gog reads this word as “no defense.” God’s word reads it as “Someone is there.”
@@17292@@
The third is “shown holy,” הִתְקַדִּשְׁתִּי (hitqaddashti), verse 23. This word is in the same family as “holy,” and its verb form carries the sense of “I will make myself seen to be holy.” Verse 23 also says: I will be shown great (הִתְגַּדִּלְתִּי), I will be shown holy. God is not holy in a corner of the temple. He is holy where he is seen, before the eyes of many nations. This is the purpose of the whole chapter: “Then they will know that I am Yahweh.”
@@17293@@
Link the three words together, and this is the road of the chapter: the hook (khakhim) is in his hand, the security (betakh) is what he gives, and being shown holy (hitqaddashti) is what he will do. We do not need to block the storm for him, and we do not need to bring about his glory for him. Our role is to know him, and to live where he has placed us.
@@17294@@
Does “dwelling securely” mean we need no protection, and not even the news?
@@17295@@
No. “Security” means having something in the heart to lean on. It is not foolishness. You may lock your door, buy insurance, and check whether news is true. Only do not let these walls become your god. Nor do you need to deliberately avoid looking or caring just to appear to “have faith.” A more practical way is to draw a boundary for yourself: for example, no news for one hour before bed, and no forwarding of messages that cause panic. After reading a piece of news that unsettles you, stop first and say: Lord, you are in charge. The body needs care, and God is glad for you to do this. Your body is also something he has entrusted to you. Taking good care of it does not mean you lack spirituality.
@@17296@@
The first deviation is to match Gog and Magog with some modern country or people and say, “That is them.” This is labeling, and it also creates fear and hostility. The text gives us no list of modern countries. We want to help the congregation love the Lord more, not fear a certain group more.
@@17297@@
The second deviation is to draw up a timetable from “After many days” and “In the latter years,” saying Gog “will surely happen soon,” or drawing a direct equal sign between Revelation 20:8 and this chapter in order to speculate about world events. The text gives no dates, and the different views stand side by side. If we make people panic, hoard, or be afraid to live, we have gone off course.
@@17298@@
The third deviation is to preach the “hook” as God excusing Gog’s evil deeds, or to say to victims, “This is God’s arrangement, and you must accept it.” The text sets the two side by side: evil intent rose in his heart, and God also reigns. Sovereignty is for comforting people. It is not for shutting the mouths of victims.
@@17299@@
The fourth deviation is to preach “dwelling securely” as “no need for defenses, no need for caution,” or to blame the victim: “Why were you not on guard?” Security is having something in the heart to lean on. It is not teaching people to be foolish. A person who has been harmed does not need to feel ashamed again of having been undefended.
@@17300@@
The fifth deviation is to preach God’s wrath and judgment as the tantrum of a God out of control, or as encouragement to look forward to enemies killing one another and being repaid, or to describe the battlefield vividly to people who have lived through war and violence. Judgment belongs to God. We comfort first, we do not magnify the pictures, and we do not urge people, “Do not be afraid” or “Be strong.”
@@17301@@
Someone uses this chapter to say, “That country is Gog.” How should I respond?
@@17302@@
You can respond gently: what the text gives is God and Gog, not a list of modern countries. This chapter has been read in different ways down the years, and even serious readers of Scripture have no single view, so we do not need to present any one of them as the only answer. You can also ask the other person: does this way of speaking make you love the Lord more and have more peace? Or does it make you fear a certain group more? If the one who finishes reading fears more and hates more, we have gone off course. There is no need to argue. Bring the conversation back to what God himself said: I will magnify myself, and sanctify myself. Your peace is more important than winning a debate, and it bears better witness to him.
@@17303@@
The first question: we are used to “better safe than sorry.” In the elders’ group chat, a sensational message gets forwarded “for your good.” At the first big event, people start stockpiling, making phone calls, getting anxious. We think this is love, but often it is passing on our own unease to others. The stance of Ezekiel 38 is: stop first, say “Lord, you are in charge,” and then decide whether to forward it. You can start like this: this week, before forwarding any message that causes panic, ask one question: will this give people more peace, or more panic?
@@17304@@

These are not bad things. Only do not let them become your god. When he takes them away, you still have him.</p>

<p>This verse has no real dispute, but there is a detail worth seeing: “dwelling securely” (<em>yoshevei lavetach</em>) in Chapter 38 was Israel’s situation, and here it is used of the people of Magog and the islands. One phrase used on both sides means: that false “security” of thinking oneself safe will also be broken by God’s fire.</p>
@@17404@@
<p><strong><strong>Even the far-off people who “dwell securely” are seen.</strong></strong></p><p>This verse is very short, but it stretches the view very far: not only the army, but even those who live in Magog and the islands are in God’s sight.</p><p>The word is a familiar one: securely. In Chapter 38, Israel “dwelling securely” was peace God gave. Here, the people of Magog and the islands also “dwell securely,” but that is a security that never expected to be disturbed, a security with nothing behind it to lean on.</p><p>Two kinds of security, the same word. The difference is: who is your security.</p><p>And there is that sentence: “Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” The purpose of judgment again lands on this “knowing.” The fire is used to make people know.</p><p>Landing: have you noticed what your own “steadiness” rests on? If you think about which one thing, if taken away, would make you panic, that is where your security lies.</p>

<p><strong>② What is the tone of “It comes, and it will be done”?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>The prophetic perfect view</strong>: the perfect tense shows “certainty,” written as if it has already happened.</li><li><strong>The accomplished fact view</strong>: the hearers of that time already saw some of it taking place.</li><li><strong>The intention view</strong>: the point is not the tense, but God’s “having said it.”</li></ul><p>What the text wants people to see is not a date, but the certainty carried by the phrase “this is the day of which I have spoken.” These notes do not count days.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: this is the prophetic perfect: it is not announcing a time, but saying that because he said it, it is as if it is already done</strong></p></blockquote>
@@17407@@
<p><strong><strong>The heart of this chapter is this sentence: my holy name.</strong></strong></p><p>The whole chapter before and after speaks of a war, but by verse 7, God turns the topic back to himself: “I will make my holy name known among my people Israel.”</p><p>War is the means, and the holy name is the purpose. What God cares about most is not winning, but how his own name is regarded.</p><p>That sentence, “I won’t allow my holy name to be profaned any more,” is the heaviest word of this verse. To profane (<em>chalal</em>) is to treat what is holy as common. When Israel was in captivity, the nations thought the name of Yahweh was common, the name of a defeated god. Here he says: I will not let this happen again.</p><p>Look also at the title: “the Holy One in Israel.” His holiness is not a distant separateness. It is “in the midst of” Israel. He is with his people, and his name is on his people.</p><p>Landing: God’s name is on you too. The way you speak, handle money, and treat people: is it bearing witness for him? You do not need to be perfect. You only need to be willing to let his name be seen on you, and not despised.</p>

<p><strong>③ Are the “seven years” and “seven months” literal or symbolic?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>The literal view</strong>: they will really burn for seven years and bury for seven months.</li><li><strong>The symbolic view</strong>: seven is the number of completeness, showing that God’s judgment and cleansing are thorough.</li><li><strong>The rhetorical view</strong>: an exaggerated quantity is used to show how complete this defeat is, so that Israel will never lack fuel again.</li></ul><p>These notes do not draw up a timetable and do not count days. What “seven” says in this chapter is thoroughness and completeness.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: set the views side by side, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith; what the text wants people to see is: weapons become fuel</strong></p></blockquote>
@@17410@@
<p><strong><strong>What the war leaves behind becomes fuel for daily life.</strong></strong></p><p>The picture of this verse is unusual: the Israelites walk out of the city, not to fight, but to “gather.” What do they gather? The shields, bows and arrows, clubs, and spears that the enemy left behind.</p><p>These things once made people afraid. Now they are thrown into the fire and become fuel for cooking and warmth. They do not even need to take wood from the fields and forests.</p><p>Notice the last sentence: “They will plunder those who plundered them, and rob those who robbed them.” It is reversed. Those who were once plundered became those who take back. This is not the thrill of revenge. It is God returning to his people everything that was stolen.</p><p>The number “seven years” is read differently by different interpreters. However it is read, what the text says is: this judgment is not a hasty finish. It is thorough.</p><p>Landing: one day the things that once hurt you may no longer hurt you. You do not need to rush to throw them away, and you do not need to pretend they do not exist. God has a way of turning them into something else to use.</p>
