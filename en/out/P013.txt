I. The Coals—Taking Fire from Between the Cherubim (10:1–8)
@@4530@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever pushed a shopping cart with something wrong with it? Four wheels, and only one is especially stiff. It will not go the way you want. You push left, and it insists on going right, and the whole cart rattles all the way along, so you have to grip it hard with both hands to keep from crashing into the shelves. After one round, your arms are sore. I thought at the time: if only the four wheels were of one mind, how easy it would be for the one pushing. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 10. This chapter also has wheels, wheels within wheels, but they do not wander and they do not veer, because there is one Spirit in them.
@@4531@@
Let us first connect the context. In chapter 9, the man clothed in linen put a mark on the foreheads of those who sighed and cried, and then reported: I have done as you have commanded me. Chapter 10 opens with the prophet saying: “Then I looked, and see, in the expanse that was over the head of the cherubim there appeared above them as it were a sapphire stone, as the appearance of the likeness of a throne.” This is a scene he has already seen in chapter 1, and now it appears again. The same throne, the same sapphire sky. The difference is this: in chapter 1, he was by the River Chebar, far from the temple; in chapter 10, this throne is in the temple in Jerusalem.
@@4532@@
Then God speaks to the man clothed in linen: “Go in between the whirling wheels, even under the cherub, and fill both your hands with coals of fire from between the cherubim, and scatter them over the city.” It is this same man again. In chapter 9 he carried an inkhorn; in chapter 10 he is to carry burning coals. Please notice the order: the mark first, the coals after. Before God acts, he has already taken note of his people.
@@4533@@
Where do the coals come from? Not from the altar, and not from a kitchen, but from between the cherubim, from beside the throne. Verse 7 says that a cherub stretched out his hand from between the cherubim to the fire, took some of it, and put it into the hands of the man. You see, the man clothed in linen did not go and grab it himself. The cherub handed the fire to him. Even judgment is not something anyone went looking for; it is passed down from the throne.
@@4534@@
These coals scattered over the city: is it judgment, or cleansing? Some read it as judgment: this city refused to repent, so fire will come on her. Some read it as cleansing: holy fire has always had the purpose of removing uncleanness, as in Isaiah chapter 6, where the prophet’s lips were touched with a coal. These notes, following the context, mainly read it as judgment, joined to chapters 9 and 11; but we do not rule out that the fire itself is holy fire. We also remind ourselves that this chapter does not name any place or any person today, and we do not use it to match it up with anyone.
@@4535@@
Next, in the middle of this, the text inserts a very quiet description: as the man went in, the cloud filled the inner court. Yahweh’s glory mounted up from the cherub and stood over the threshold; the house was filled with the cloud, and the court was full of the brightness of Yahweh’s glory. The sound of the wings of the cherubim was heard even in the outer court, like the voice of God Almighty. The glory is still there, and he is standing on the threshold.
@@4536@@
Verse 8 says one more very unusual thing: the form of a man’s hand appeared under the wings of the cherubim. These are not human creatures, and yet under their wings there is a man’s hand. God’s judgment does not fall mechanically; it is handed over through a hand, one action at a time. Even the fire is placed in a man’s two hands.
@@4537@@
When I read “coals scattered over the city,” I felt very afraid. Is this saying that God will destroy people with fire?
@@4538@@
This chapter is indeed very heavy, and it is natural for you to be afraid. But please notice a few things. First, before the coals, the mark had already been put on. God remembers those who sigh over sin. Second, the coals were taken from between the cherubim, from beside the throne; they were not thrown by anyone at random. It is God’s own act. Third, this chapter is spoken to a temple and to a people who had long refused to turn back. It is not a sentence on believers who are grieving, and it is certainly not to be pointed at any person or place today. If all you feel after reading it is fear, you can say to God: Lord, I am afraid, please walk with me as I read. You do not need to understand the whole chapter before you pray. If this chapter touches a past wound, you can stop, and find a pastor or a companion you trust, and read it together.

II. The Wheels and the Cherubim: A Wheel Within a Wheel (10:9–17)
@@4540@@
From verse 9, the prophet’s eyes turn from the coals to the wheels: I looked, and there were four wheels beside the cherubim. One wheel beside each cherub, and the appearance of the wheels was like a beryl stone. Verse 10 says that the four wheels had one likeness, like a wheel within a wheel. This is the part of the whole chapter that people understand least, and we should not pretend to understand it. We will not draw a chariot diagram, and we will not work out a ranking of angels. We only ask: what did the prophet see, and after he saw it, what did he become?
@@4541@@
But there are a few things the text says very clearly. First, when the wheels go, they go in all four directions and do not turn as they go. Whichever way the head faces, they follow. This is a movement that needs no turning: there is no need to stop first and then change direction. Second, their whole bodies, their backs, their hands, their wings, and the wheels all around, are full of eyes. This says that nothing escapes his sight. Third, the prophet heard these wheels called “the whirling wheels.” He gave the wheels a name, and the wheels listen to his voice.
@@4542@@
Look again at verse 14: every one had four faces. The first face was the face of the cherub, the second the face of a man, the third the face of a lion, and the fourth the face of an eagle. Interpreters hold different views about these four faces. Some say they are the noblest of created things, joined together to worship him. Some say they are a revealing of God’s attributes. Others say they cannot be matched one for one. These notes do not settle it, and do not make any one view a condition of faith. If you hold a different view, we are still companions.
@@4543@@
Verse 15 is a very important sentence: “The cherubim mounted up. This is the living creature that I saw by the river Chebar.” By verse 20 he says again: I knew that they were cherubim. When the prophet saw these living creatures in chapter 1, he did not say they were cherubim. He said living creatures, and said so for a long time, until now he finally says the name. It took him a long time to recognize what he had seen all along. If your knowledge of God is also slow, do not be anxious. The prophet also recognized it step by step.
@@4544@@
The most important is the last sentence of verse 17. “When they stood, these stood. When they mounted up, these mounted up with them; for the spirit of the living creature was in them.” Think back to that shopping cart: four wheels, one wanting to go somewhere else, and the whole cart goes crooked. But the wheels here are different: when one stands, all stand; when one rises, all rise. Why? Not because they talked it over, but because there is only one Spirit in the wheels. With a Spirit in the wheels, life is not a pile of parts put together; it is moved by one and the same Spirit.
@@4545@@
I cannot understand the wheel within a wheel or the four faces at all. Does that mean I am not spiritual enough?
@@4546@@
No. Even the prophet himself did not explain it. He only said: I saw. People through the ages have read this vision in different ways, and no one reading is a condition for salvation. You can read it like this: do not first ask “what does each detail stand for?” First ask three questions. First, where is his throne? Second, does he see everything? Third, after I have seen, have I bowed down? After the prophet saw these things, he lay face down on the ground. For the places you do not understand, you can say honestly: Lord, I do not understand this passage. Then read the sentence you do understand, for example verse 17: the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels. Take that sentence home and live with it for a week.
@@4547@@
Since “the spirit of the living creature is in the wheels,” does that mean everything is arranged by God, so I do not need to grieve?
@@4548@@
No. A Spirit in the wheels does not mean people cannot weep. The prophet himself, in chapter 9, fell on his face and cried: Ah, Lord Yahweh! God’s sovereignty and human tears can be held together. If someone says to you in your sorrow, “This is all God’s arrangement, so do not be sad,” you can tell him: I know he reigns, but right now I need to cry first. Those who have lost a loved one, who are seriously ill, who have lost a job, need to be allowed to weep first, and then slowly be comforted. You can say to God: I believe you are in the wheels, but today I am in great pain. Saying this is not unbelief; it is honesty. If the sorrow goes so deep that it affects sleep, eating, and daily life, please find a pastor or professional help.

III. Light from the Original Language: Weight, Going Out, Spirit (10:4, 17–18)
@@4550@@
There are three words in this chapter worth looking at slowly. The first is “glory,” in Hebrew כָּבוֹד (kavod). The original meaning of this word is “weight.” Glory has weight. Have you ever noticed that when a person of real substance walks into a room, he does not need to speak loudly, and the whole room goes quiet? That is weight. The heaviest thing in the temple was not the gold or the stone, but his presence. Now this heaviest thing is about to leave. You can ask yourself: in my life, what has the most weight? Is it he, or something else?
@@4551@@
The second is “went out,” in Hebrew וַיֵּצֵא (vayyetse), in verse 18: “Yahweh’s glory went out from over the threshold of the house, and stood over the cherubim.” Please compare it with verse 4. Verse 4 says the glory “mounted up,” וַיָּרָם (vayyaram), and stood over the threshold. Chapter 9 verse 3 also has it rising to the threshold. But verse 18 uses a different verb: went out. First it rose and stood at the threshold; then it went out. Leaving does not happen all at once; it has steps: rising, stopping, going out, standing at the east gate. Each step is a threshold, and each step is a chance to turn back.
@@4552@@
This gives us a very practical reminder: between people and God, it is rare that anyone leaves “suddenly.” It is one time without prayer, one time skipping a meeting, one time thinking in the heart: forget it. Each one is a threshold. When the glory stands on the threshold, that is the best time to turn back. You do not need to wait until the door is shut to ask: is he still there? Today is the threshold.
@@4553@@
The third is “spirit,” in Hebrew רוּחַ (ruach), in verse 17: “the spirit of the living creature was in them.” (The CUV has “in the wheels”; the Hebrew has the pronoun “in them,” and the “them” are the wheels.) This word can be translated spirit, and it can also be translated wind or breath. In Genesis, God’s Spirit hovered over the waters; later in Ezekiel, the Spirit will enter the dry bones. The wheels can go, can stand, can rise together, not because the wheels have some ability of their own, but because a Spirit is in them. We do not move ourselves by our own effort. Every morning, you can ask one question: Lord, who is in my wheels today? Is it your Spirit, or my impatience, my fear, my comparing?
@@4554@@
Joining the three words together: glory (kavod) has weight, so when he leaves, the temple suddenly becomes light; he went out (vayyetse) step by step, so at every step there was a chance to turn back; and what drives everything is the Spirit (ruach), not the strength of human hands.
@@4555@@
“Glory has weight”: how can this idea be used in my life today?
@@4556@@
It can be used on one very small question: what do I value most today? Weight is not what your mouth says, but where your time, your money, and your heart are truly placed. If you find that the heaviest thing is not he, you need not blame yourself, nor make a big decision right away. You can start with one small act: set aside ten minutes today, turn off your phone, read 10:4 and 10:18, and say to God: Lord, where does your glory stand in my life now? Do not rush to answer; be quiet for a while first. When the glory stands on the threshold, it is an invitation. If you feel far from him, you can also find a companion to be quiet together for ten minutes.
@@4557@@
IV. The Glory Leaves the Temple (10:18–22)
@@4558@@
At last we come to the heaviest verse of this chapter: “Yahweh’s glory went out from over the threshold of the house, and stood over the cherubim.” In chapter 1, the glory came to the prophet; in chapter 9, the glory rose to the threshold; here, the glory goes out. The prophet does not speak. He does not cry out, and he does not ask. There is only one picture: the cherubim lift up their wings and mount up from the earth, the wheels beside them, and together they stand at the east gate of the temple.
@@4559@@
But please notice, he has not vanished. Verse 19 says they stood at the door of the east gate of Yahweh’s house, and the glory of the God of Israel was over them above. He stands at the door; he has not gone far. The east gate faces the direction of the sunrise; the glory goes toward that direction, not into darkness. The last sentence of verse 22: “They each went straight forward.” They have a direction. Even in leaving, he has a direction: the throne has wheels, he is not driven out or trapped; he rose by himself.
@@4560@@
Why does he go out? The previous chapter has already said it: the city pushed him out again and again. Idols came in, bloodshed came in, and even words like “Yahweh doesn’t see us” came in. The glory was not defeated, but was pushed farther and farther away by people, again and again, and only then did it gently go out from the threshold. He did not kick people away with one foot. He waited a very long time.
@@4561@@
Some will ask: since he left the temple, has he abandoned his people? These notes’ view is that this is spoken to a temple and to a people who had long pushed him out. It is not a sentence on grieving believers. If you are very tired now and feel that he has left you too, please do not use this verse to judge yourself. The pain in your heart is real; first let someone walk with you, and do not hurry to be persuaded. The main line of this book has never been “he has gone,” but “for the sake of my name, I will come back myself.”
@@4562@@
In Luke 13:34, Jesus stood before the same city and said: “How often I wanted to gather your children together, like a hen gathers her own brood under her wings, and you refused!” When the glory left, the Lord’s heart did not leave. He is the Lord who is at the door, under the wings, always waiting for people to turn back. If we want to be like him, we should not be the first to shut the door when others leave.
@@4563@@
Lately I feel that God is very far from me, and I feel nothing when I pray. Has he left me?
@@4564@@
Many people have this feeling, including many who love the Lord very much. First, please do not use chapter 10 to judge yourself: this chapter was spoken to a temple that had long refused him; it is not a sentence on you. Second, feelings and facts are not always the same: he is standing at the east gate; he has not gone far. Third, you can start with something very small: you do not need to pray long, just say one sentence: Lord, I do not know where you are, but I am still here. Fourth, tell someone. If this feeling lasts a long time, and you cannot sleep or eat, or you have thoughts of hurting yourself, please quickly find a pastor, someone you trust, or professional help; do not carry it alone. He still remembers you. The deepest part of this chapter is that his mark came before the coals.
@@4565@@
The first deviation is to draw the wheel within a wheel as an elaborate diagram, claiming to have cracked this vision, and using it to prove that the speaker knows a lot. The vision is meant to make people bow down, not to give an exam. To force a match for every detail is to show off learning. If those who listen admire the speaker’s diagram more, but do not bow down more or love the Lord more, then this chapter has been taught wrongly.
@@4566@@
The second deviation is to fix the four faces, one for one, as a particular system of doctrine, and make it a condition of faith. The views of the different schools should be set side by side: some say they represent created beings, some say they are God’s attributes, and some say they cannot be matched one for one. If a speaker wants to put forward his own view, please say that it is one of them, and acknowledge that brothers and sisters who differ are still companions.
@@4567@@
The third deviation is to say to those who grieve: “The glory has left, which means God has abandoned you.” Chapter 10 speaks of a temple and of a people who had long pushed him out; it is not a sentence on anyone who grieves. If, after hearing this, a grieving person is even less willing to open their mouth and pray to God, it has been taught wrongly. We want people to still dare to say: Lord, I am in great pain, please do not go far.
@@4568@@
The fourth deviation is to use this chapter to name some place or some person today, saying that they will be judged by the “coals,” or to use “the glory has left” to measure other churches and judge that he has already left. This chapter is about a particular temple and a particular moment; it is not a ruler for us to measure others. The use of this passage is to let people turn back and examine their own temple, not to let people point at others and say: he has gone.
@@4569@@
The fifth deviation is to teach “the spirit of the living creature is in the wheels” as “everything is arranged by God, so there is no need to grieve,” or to make this chapter a horror film and stir up panic. A Spirit in the wheels does not mean people cannot weep; the prophet himself fell on his face. We want those who have lost a loved one or are seriously ill to be allowed to weep first, and then slowly be comforted; and we want the congregation to walk out loving the Lord more, not fearing this world more.
@@4570@@
The first question: we are very used to “I must understand before I dare to worship.” When we do not understand, either we feel we are not spiritual enough, or we hurry to find an answer and draw it as a diagram or record it in a chart. The wheel within a wheel in chapter 10 is exactly this kind of chapter: those who cannot understand it feel ashamed; those who can understand it feel proud. But the prophet himself did not explain it. He only said: I saw, and I bowed down. You can begin like this: read 10:9–17, then take a sheet of paper and write two lines: one place I do not understand; one sentence I do understand. Live with the second sentence for a week. Not understanding is not shameful.
@@4571@@
The second question: we are very used to “leaving quietly.” A husband and wife stop quarreling, not because they are reconciled, but because they no longer open their mouths; a friend stops sending messages, and no one asks; someone in the church has not come for several months, and everyone says: he’s probably busy. A person stops praying to God, and no one knows. The glory in chapter 10 went out from the threshold step by step, quietly, and no one stopped it. You can begin like this: this week, take the initiative to ask someone you have not been in touch with for a long time: how have you been lately? Give no advice, ask nothing of him, just let him know there is still someone at the door. And at the same time say to God: Lord, if I have a threshold I am about to leave from, please let me see it.
@@4572@@
Someone close to me has not come to church for a long time. What should I do so that it does not feel like blaming him?
@@4573@@
Do not first think about how to “bring him back”; first think about how to let him know someone is still at the door. You can do three small things. First, greet him with one simple sentence, without mentioning church or meetings, for example: I thought of you, how have you been lately? Second, do not rush to ask why, and do not say “you should come back”; if he wants to talk, he will say so himself. Third, pray for him, and let him know you are praying, but do not turn it into pressure. In Luke 13:34, Jesus described his own heart with the picture of a hen sheltering her chicks: he waits, he does not force. If his leaving is connected with hurt, loss, or depression, please do not press him with questions, and at the right time, invite a pastor or a professional to care for him together with you.

VII. Living Out 321—The Spirit in the Wheels
@@4575@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He stood before this city and wept over her, and said: How often I wanted to gather your children together, like a hen gathers her own brood under her wings. When the glory left, the Lord’s heart did not leave. Those who follow him learn his way: when others leave, we do not shut the door first; when we ourselves are heartbroken, we do not shut our hearts first.
@@4576@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 10 not to draw diagrams, not to work out timetables, and not to measure others, but to let the text ask us: in my temple, on which threshold is he standing? Every time you read about the wheels, please also read about the Spirit; every time you read about going out, please also read about the east gate.
@@4577@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. “The spirit of the living creature is in the wheels”: the wheels can stand and can rise because there is one Spirit. We too do not move ourselves by ourselves. Every morning, before going out, be quiet for one minute and ask: Lord, today are my wheels moved by your Spirit, or by my impatience?
@@4578@@
Let Jesus be King. The Spirit in the wheels is not I. What this chapter asks us to hand over is not more effort, but the position of “I will manage.” Selflessness is not needing to understand every wheel, but recognizing who sits on the throne: the throne is above the expanse, in the sapphire sky; he is seated, and I can let go.
@@4579@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. Glory has weight, and this weight does not belong to me, nor to any speaker, nor to any church. His leaving is his sovereignty; his returning is also his sovereignty. If we have ever been shown mercy by him and found by him, please do not say “I came back”; say: he waited for me. All the glory belongs to the Lord who stands at the door and waits.
@@4580@@
To build God’s system. The wheels and the cherubim stand together and mount up together, because there is only one Spirit. God’s system is not a machine piled up from parts, but a life moved by one and the same Spirit. The wheels of home, workplace, and church must all return under this one Spirit. In such a community, someone who slows down is not thrown off; someone who leaves is not judged; and at the door, there is always someone.
@@4581@@
What does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with “the throne has wheels” in this chapter?
@@4582@@
Very closely related. The throne having wheels means: his kingship is not held in by a temple, and his presence is not tied to one place. If he leaves, he rises by himself; if he returns, he decides it himself. We often think of “letting Jesus be King” as something we have to work very hard to do, but more often it is an act of letting go: I do not have to understand everything, I do not have to control everything, I only need to recognize who is sitting on the throne. You can practice this: every morning, say one sentence: Lord, you are King; today I will not manage all the wheels, I will only follow your Spirit. After a while, you will find that your heart slowly grows lighter.

VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Threshold
@@4584@@
The proud person says: “I have understood it; I can draw this vision for you.” Or says: “That church, he left it long ago.” The text says to him: after the prophet saw, he bowed down. Pride is leaving one’s position as a human being, grasping God’s authority, and wanting to take God’s place; it includes announcing for God where he is and where he is not. To return to our position is to admit: I do not need to understand, I only need to bow down. You can start by saying one sentence to God: Lord, I am willing to be more humble than my diagram.
@@4585@@
The fearful person says: “If he leaves, what will I do? Will I be left behind too?” Chapter 10 is spoken to a temple; it is not your sentence. He stands at the east gate; he has not gone far; and the main line of the whole book is that he will return for the sake of his own name. The fearful person does not need to check every day whether he has left; you can start by telling him your fear directly: Lord, I am afraid, please do not go far.
@@4586@@
The ashamed person says: “It was I who pushed him out; I do not deserve for him to come back.” Shame says: you pushed him away; the gospel says: he is still standing at the door. The glory standing on the threshold is an invitation, not a judgment on you as a person. The ashamed person does not need to clean himself up first; today, just step one step closer to the threshold and say: Lord, I am here.
@@4587@@
The wounded person says: “Someone left me, and I feel that he has gone silent too.” Your feeling is real, and no one can tell you it does not count. He does not blame you for being hurt. Please first let someone walk with you; do not hurry to be persuaded, and do not hurry to forgive anyone. The one who accompanies should not rush to say “he has not left”; just say: I am here, I am with you.
@@4588@@
First, this week, every day, spend three minutes reading 10:17: “When they stood, these stood. When they mounted up, these mounted up with them; for the spirit of the living creature was in them.” Then say to God: “Lord, please let your Spirit move my wheels today.” And write down the date.
@@4589@@
Second, take a sheet of paper and write two lines: one place I do not understand; one sentence I do understand. Live with the second sentence for a week, and say which companion you plan to tell this sentence.
@@4590@@
Third, think of someone you have not been in touch with for a long time, or who has quietly left, and this week send him one sentence: “I thought of you, how have you been lately?” Say his name and the date you plan to send it.
@@4591@@
Fourth, examine one of your own “thresholds”: is there anything from which you have quietly stepped back, in prayer, in meetings, in a relationship? Write down which one, and choose one small step you can take back this week, and write down the time.
@@4592@@
Fifth, pray by name for the home, workplace, or church where you are: “Lord, please do not go far; please let your Spirit be in our wheels.” Say which place and which people you will pray for, and on which day.

Do not add anything else, do not mention church, and do not say “you should come back.” After you send it, do a second thing: write his name on a piece of paper, and spend thirty seconds saying one sentence for him: Lord, please do not go far. You do not need to say what it was, and you do not need to say the result. That is enough.
@@4598@@
What is most distinctive about chapter 10 is that it begins with coals and ends at a door. It begins with a man clothed in linen carrying fire taken from beside the throne; in the middle are the wheel within a wheel, the Spirit in the wheels, and eyes that cannot be counted; at the end, the glory goes out from the threshold and stands at the east gate. From the fire to the wheels, from the wheels to the gate, it is a heavy journey, but not an endless dark road.
@@4599@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see: his throne has wheels, so he is not held in; his glory has weight, and when he leaves, he leaves step by step; he stands at the east gate and has not gone far. That God, in Jesus, came among us. He is the Lord who waits at the door, the Lord who said, “How often I wanted to gather your children together.”
@@4600@@

The focus of this lesson is not how much you understood, not whether he has left, and not how others are doing, but Jesus, who sits on the throne and waits for us at the door.

① Teaching “This city will not be your cauldron” as a general “God will protect you”
The cauldron in this chapter is people placing their security in the position of the city and the temple; God’s word takes away this false sense of security, and does not promise freedom from all disaster.
Test: after hearing it, does the congregation trust him more, or become more sure that they have a cauldron to protect them?
@@4654@@
② Teaching the two readings of 11:3 as “only one is right”
Some read it as “the time is not near to build houses,” and some read it as a rhetorical question, “is it not near?” Both point to the same thing: treating the city as a protective charm.
Test: does the speaker explain both readings, and acknowledge that those who hold a different view are companions?
@@4655@@
③ Teaching Pelatiah’s death as “those who offend God will die suddenly”
The text records his death in only one sentence, and gives no explanation of the cause; do not use one person’s passing as a teaching example, and do not enlarge the picture.
Test: when there are bereaved people present, does the manuscript first protect them, and not ask them to “be strong”?
@@4656@@
④ Using the promise of a new heart and a new spirit to rebuke the weak: “Why haven’t you changed your heart yet?”
The new heart is something he “gives”; it is not something people change out by effort (make, put, remove, give, and the subject of all is he).
Test: after hearing it, do people know better that they need him, or do they blame themselves more for not trying hard enough?
