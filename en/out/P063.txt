I. Water Flows Out from Under the Threshold (47:1–2)
@@21662@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever been through a water outage? The water tower of an old house broke, and for several days the faucet could only cough. You turn it on, and first there is a dull thud, air spits out, then one drop, two drops, and then, whoosh, the water comes. At that moment the whole family stands around the sink, and no one says a word. They just watch. Only when the water comes do you know how long you have been thirsty. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 47, the second to last chapter of this book. What the prophet sees in this chapter is not a wall, not measurements, but a stream of water.
@@21663@@
Let us first set the background. Chapters 40 to 48 are the vision of the new temple that the prophet saw. There are different views in the church about how to read this whole section. Some read it literally, hoping that one day there will really be such a temple. Some read it symbolically, seeing it as a picture of God dwelling with his people. Others read it spiritually, seeing Christ and his church. These notes set the views side by side, do not draw up a timetable, and do not judge brothers and sisters who see it differently. We ask only one thing: what does the prophet want us to see? In this chapter, he wants us to see water.
@@21664@@
Verse 1 says: “He brought me back to the door of the house; and behold, waters flowed out from under the threshold of the house eastward.” Please notice, where does the water come out from? Not from the throne, not from the roof, but from under the threshold. The threshold is the lowest place in the whole temple, the place where people’s feet step every day. The water of life does not come crashing down from on high. It seeps out softly from under the feet, from the crack of the door. God’s work is often like this. He does not frighten you first, he does not make a noise first. He starts to flow first in the lowest place.
@@21665@@
There is also the direction. The water flows east, because the front of the house faces east. When the glory returned, it came from the east (chapter 43). Now life goes out, and it also goes east. The way the glory came in is the way the water goes out. It is a very quiet reversal: the temple in this chapter is not a place where people go to take something, it is a place from which water goes out. God’s house is not a reservoir. It is a spring.
@@21666@@
In verse 2, the guide led the prophet out by the north gate, and then around to the outer gate that faces east, and the water ran out on the right side. He went a long way around, and the water was still there, still coming out softly from the same place. It does not disappear because you change your angle, and it does not dry up because you take the long way.
@@21667@@
Does the water really flow from under the temple? Is this literal, symbolic, or a spiritual picture?
@@21668@@
There are serious readers of Scripture in all three camps. Those who read literally see this as something God will bring about on the earth in the future. Those who read symbolically value the picture that God’s presence brings life. Those who read spiritually see Christ and his church. These notes do not make any one of the readings a condition of faith, and do not set a timetable for any of them. The three readings share one thing, and it is also what the prophet wants us to see: life flows out from the place where God is, and is not made by people. You do not need to settle this argument first before you can receive this promise. Today you only need to ask yourself: lately, have I bent down and touched that little bit of wetness under the threshold?
@@21669@@
II. One Thousand Cubits, One Thousand Cubits (47:3–6)
@@21670@@
In verse 3, the guide went out eastward with a line in his hand, measured one thousand cubits, and caused the prophet to pass through the waters, and the water was to the ankles. One thousand cubits is a little over four hundred meters, a stretch you can walk to the end. What depth is water to the ankles? It is a depth from which you can step back at any time. Cool and clear, you are still your own master, and as you walk you still have the room to look at the scenery.
@@21671@@
In verse 4, he measured another thousand cubits, and the water was to the knees. He measured another thousand, and the water was to the waist. At the waist, things are different. Your feet begin to be unsteady, you have to adjust your stance, and you need a little strength so you won’t be carried away. You begin to know that this water is stronger than you are. Please notice one detail: every time it is the same distance, one thousand cubits. No one adds water along the way, and no tributaries flow in. The water still comes out from that one threshold, but it rises all the way.
@@21672@@
In verse 5, he measured another thousand, and the water became a river, and the prophet could not pass through it. The meaning of the original is: this is a river that cannot be walked through. He stood on the bank, looking at the river, and only then understood what he had just been walking through. He thought he was only measuring distances, but in fact he was being carried by the water, step by step, into a place he could not control.
@@21673@@
Some people, hearing this, immediately start ranking in their hearts: I am probably at the knees, and he has probably already reached the waist. Please do not think this way. Ankles, knees, waist, river are not grades of spiritual life, not a report card, and no one is handing out certificates. It is a picture of gradual progress: he leads people step by step into deeper life, and each step is measured by his line, not by you pushing yourself. Please also do not hurry others, and even less hurry yourself: go down quickly, go a little deeper. The depth is measured by the one who leads, not forced by the person himself.
@@21674@@
In verse 6, God asked a question in the deepest place: “Son of man, have you seen?” He did not ask: are you a good swimmer? He did not ask: are you afraid? He asked: what have you seen? At the place where the water rises and you cannot stand, he does not want you only to be surrounded by water. He wants you to lift your head and look. See where this water comes from and where it is going. And then the guide brought him back to the bank of the river. He did not stay in the water. He was brought back to the bank, because on the bank there was something to show him.
@@21675@@
When we are at the ankles, we can still say: I will come. When we are in the river, we can only say: you come. There was a father whose son was learning to swim for the first time. He stood in the water with his arms open and said to the child: Jump, Daddy is here. He just kept standing there, his arms kept open. When the child finally jumped, it was not because he was not afraid, but because Daddy never walked away.
@@21676@@
When I read “the water to the waist,” I feel uneasy. Does that mean my faith is not enough?
@@21677@@
No. Waist-deep water is a depth that naturally makes people uneasy, and even the prophet was brought back to the bank after this. The text does not say he did wrong because he was afraid, nor that he needed to be stronger. You can understand it this way: unease is a very honest reaction when a person faces something greater than himself. At such a time you do not have to force yourself to be braver. Just remember: the one who measures the water is right beside you, and he has the line in his hand, and he knows you can stand at this depth. If you are going through the loss of a loved one, serious illness, or a long season of weakness, and your heart feels as if you are drowning and gasping for breath, please do not hold on alone, and do not be hurried toward the deep. First find someone who can be with you, sit by the river, and say: I am very tired right now. When needed, please also seek counseling or medical help.
@@21678@@
III. Light from the Original and Everything Will Live (47:7–12)
@@21679@@
Back on the bank, the prophet turned around and saw what he could not see in the water: “on the bank of the river were very many trees on the one side and on the other.” Sometimes, when you are in the water, you cannot see anything. Only when you stop, climb onto the bank, and look back do you find that both sides have been filled with trees. What the water brings can often only be seen from the bank.
@@21680@@
Then the man told him where this water was going: “These waters flow out toward the eastern region, and will go down into the Arabah. Then they will go toward the sea; and flow into the sea which will be made to flow out; and the waters will be healed.” The Arabah is a low, dry, desolate valley. The Salt Sea is the saltiest water, where fish cannot live and trees cannot grow, the deadest place on earth. Where does the water flow? Not to the places with fine scenery, but to the deadest place. Grace always goes downward. It does not first look for the deserving. It first goes to where the need is greatest.
@@21681@@
In this passage there are three words in the original that deserve a closer look. The first is “becomes sweet.” That is how the CUV renders it, and the Hebrew word is רָפָא. Verse 8 says וְנִרְפּוּ הַמָּיִם, literally “and the waters will be healed.” This is a word of healing, not a word of seasoning. The Salt Sea is not sweetened with sugar. It is healed. This is the gentlest verb in the whole chapter. What God wants to heal is not only people. Even the saltiest, deadest water, he says: I will heal it. The place you think “cannot be changed” is not excluded in front of this verb.
@@21682@@
The second is “live,” the Hebrew חָיָה. Verse 9 has יִחְיֶה, which means “will live.” Verse 9 says: “every living creature which swarms, in every place where the rivers come, will live”; and also: “everything will live wherever the river comes.” This word “live” does not mean barely staying alive. It means to truly have life, to move, to multiply. In Genesis, this is the word for what happened after God breathed breath into the man’s nostrils, and the man became a living person. Ezekiel 47 uses the same word as the ending of this vision. What God wants at the end is not whether the measurements of the temple are accurate enough. It is that everything lives.
@@21683@@
The third is “healing,” the Hebrew תְרוּפָה. Verse 12 says: “Its fruit will be for food, and its leaf for healing.” It comes from the same root as the earlier רָפָא. It is the noun of healing, meaning medicine, something that makes a person well. Please note that this leaf is not saying that believers can do without doctors. God also uses medical care, companionship, and human hands. What the leaf means here is: what the water brings is not only fruit. Even a single leaf has power to make people well.
@@21684@@
Verse 10 says: “fishermen will stand by it. From En Gedi even to En Eglaim will be a place for the spreading of nets. Their fish will be after their kinds, as the fish of the great sea, exceedingly many.” In the place that was once the deadest, fishermen now spread their nets.
@@21685@@
But verse 11 has a sentence we cannot skip: “But the miry places of it, and its marshes, will not be healed. They will be given up to salt.” Wherever the water comes, everything lives, except that some places are not healed. There are different views on how to read this sentence. Some say the salt ground is left for the people to make salt. Some say it is an honest reminder that not every corner gets well at once. Others say it is the result of refusing the water. The text does not explain the reason, so we will not find a reason of condemnation for it either. And we must not say that the people or places that are “not healed” have been abandoned by God. What this verse leaves us with is honesty: some corners stay salty for a very, very long time. If there is such a corner in your life, please do not think you have been abandoned. First bring it to the water’s edge, and sit down.
@@21686@@
Finally, verse 12 says: “By the river on its bank, on this side and on that side, will grow every tree for food, whose leaf won’t wither, neither will its fruit fail. It will produce new fruit every month, because its waters issue out of the sanctuary.” Please notice that last sentence. Why does the tree not wither? Not because it is strong, not because it works hard, but because its waters issue out of the sanctuary. The tree only needs not to leave the water.
@@21687@@
Why are some places “not healed”? Does this mean God has given up on some people?
@@21688@@
No. Verse 11 says only that the miry places and the marshes will not be healed and will be given up to salt. It does not say whose fault this is, and it does not say that these places have been abandoned by God. Some see it as salt ground left for the people, and some see it as an honest reminder: grace is real, but in some corners recovery is slow. We do not need to find a condemning answer for this verse. If there is something in your life now that has not gotten better for a very, very long time, you can think of it this way: not left behind, but waiting at the water’s edge. Please do not feel your faith is not enough because illness, depression, or a long difficulty has not changed. And do not say to a person who is grieving: you must have faith. First keep him company, and sit by the water.
@@21689@@
The leaf is for healing. Does this mean believers don’t need to see a doctor?
@@21690@@
No. “Its leaf for healing” in verse 12 speaks of the life this water brings, that even a single leaf has power to make people well. It does not tell people to refuse medical care. God also uses doctors, medicine, rehabilitation, counseling, and the hands of those who keep us company. In the church there is sometimes an unhelpful saying, as if seeking a doctor were unbelief. We should correct it: prayer and seeing a doctor are not two opposing roads. You can take medicine and pray at the same time, receive treatment and say to the Lord: you are my source of water. If someone near you is sick, please also do not say to him: you only need to believe and you will get well. Going with him to see the doctor is a leaf.
@@21691@@
IV. The Four Borders of the Land and the Inheritance of the Stranger (47:13–23)
@@21692@@
In verse 13, the text suddenly turns from the river to a map: “The Lord Yahweh says: ‘This shall be the border, by which you shall divide the land for inheritance according to the twelve tribes of Israel. Joseph shall have two portions.’” From water to land, it reads as if the subject has changed, but it is really two sides of the same promise: water is life, land is a place to settle. The water of life flows down not so that people will float in the air, but so that people will have a place where their feet can stand.
@@21693@@
The reason in verse 14 is given by God himself: “for I swore to give it to your fathers. This land will fall to you for inheritance.” This was not earned by human effort. God swore first. The exiles had been away from this land for a long time, and when they heard this sentence, what they heard was a very old promise: you still remember. Verses 15 to 20 give the four borders, north, east, south, and west, each side with a description. This map is a promise spoken to the exiles, not any modern map today. We do not use it to endorse any claim to land today, and we do not point to any nation or people. What the text wants the exiles to remember is that God has not forgotten what he said.
@@21694@@
Next comes the most surprising sentence in this chapter. Verses 21 and 22 say: “So you shall divide this land to yourselves according to the tribes of Israel. It will happen, that you shall divide it by lot for an inheritance to you and to the aliens who live among you, who will father children among you. Then they shall be to you as the native-born among the children of Israel. They shall have inheritance with you among the tribes of Israel.” The stranger also has a portion. Not at the edge, not outside, but by lot, together with you. Casting lots is very fair: it does not look at your family background, and it does not look at how loudly you speak. It is the same jar of lots.
@@21695@@
Verse 23 adds one more sentence: “in whatever tribe the stranger lives, there you shall give him his inheritance,” says the Lord Yahweh. Not some other corner, but right in the tribe where he lives. Think of this picture: a person from another country has had children among the Israelites, and the children grow up here and speak the language here. He was always a guest, and now the text says: treat him as the native-born among the children of Israel. Even the one farthest out has a lot.
@@21696@@
The text says the stranger also has an inheritance. How can the church live this out?
@@21697@@
Start with the eyes. Think: at this week’s gathering, was there someone standing at the edge with no one speaking to him? Perhaps someone new, perhaps a family that just moved here, perhaps someone who speaks with a different accent. What verse 22 means is not only “accept” but “share together”: give him a place, a place where he can take part, a seat where he does not need to prove who he is. The small things you can do are simple: greet him first, remember his name, invite him to sit at your table, introduce him to a friend. Do not give advice first, and do not first ask him to fit in. Some strangers come wounded, and what they need is time.
@@21698@@
The first deviation is to take Ezekiel 47:9, “everything will live,” and preach that if you believe in God you will surely be healed and your problems will surely disappear, or that if you are not yet well, your faith is not enough. This verse is a promise of renewed life, not a guarantee of no suffering. We want people in illness, depression, and long hopelessness to feel accompanied, not to feel it is their own fault.
@@21699@@
The second deviation is to take verse 11, “not healed,” and preach that certain people or places have simply been abandoned by God. The text does not explain the reason, and does not say it is anyone’s fault. We want the congregation to see God’s honesty and mercy, not to add a reason of condemnation to the text.
@@21700@@
The third deviation is to preach chapter 47 as a prophecy that can be fitted into a timetable, pointing out when the temple, the river, and the borders will appear, or to take the borders of verses 13 to 20 and use them to endorse any land claim today, pointing to a particular nation or people. This is a promise to the exiles, not a modern map. We want the congregation to love the Lord more and to be more patient, not to panic, and not to quarrel.
@@21701@@
The fourth deviation is to preach ankles, knees, waist, and river as grades of spiritual life, using them to compare who is more spiritual, or to hurry people to go deeper quickly. The text is a picture of gradual progress. The depth of the water is measured by the one who leads, not forced by the person himself. We want people to follow him with peace, not with more anxiety.
@@21702@@
The fifth deviation is to equate this river directly with the Holy Spirit, or with some specific church experience, and then use “do you have this water?” to judge others; or to preach “its leaf for healing” as if believers do not need to see a doctor. The text puts the cause in the water, in the sanctuary, and God also uses medical care and companionship. We want people to draw nearer to him, not to compare more.
@@21703@@
I am very tired. This chapter says to “flow out.” Should I keep pushing on and giving?
@@21704@@
No. The water of chapter 47 flows out from the sanctuary. The tree does not bear fruit by its own effort, but by not leaving the water. For a very tired person, the first thing is not to flow out, but to sit down at the water’s edge. You can honestly say to the Lord: I am tired, please let me drink first. You can also find someone you trust and say: I need rest. If serving becomes a way of proving yourself, it is time to stop. Rest is not laziness. It is returning to the water’s edge. If your tiredness is affecting your sleep, your mood, or your daily life, please do not hold on alone. Talk with a pastor or a trusted companion, and when needed seek professional help. The text puts the cause of the fruit in the water, not in how strong you are.
@@21705@@
The first question: we often store up the grace we receive. In Chinese church culture, thrift is a virtue, and that is good, but sometimes we become thrifty even with grace. We hear a very good message and keep it in our hearts first, afraid it will be used up. We receive a little peace and guard it, not daring to share, afraid people will say we are showing off. There is also the other kind: always giving out, giving until we are dry, and still thinking this is love and that we may not rest. Verse 12 of chapter 47 says: “because its waters issue out of the sanctuary.” Water that stops becomes dead water, but a person who is already very tired and has no strength to give out should not be blamed. You can do this: first go to the water’s edge, sit down, and do just one thing, say to the Lord: I am tired, please let me drink first. When you have drunk your fill, then think about whom it will flow to.
@@21706@@
The second question: we are used to “our own people.” In Chinese society the circles of relationship are very clear: relatives, people from the same hometown, classmates, and people who have been in the same church for a long time are “our own people,” and newcomers, people with a different accent, and people from a different background are “outsiders.” The church sometimes does this without realizing it. Verse 22 of chapter 47 says: “Then they shall be to you as the native-born among the children of Israel.” You can start this week: at the gathering, find someone you do not know, say his name, and do not first ask what he does for work, but ask him first: how are you today? Remember this name, and say it again next time.

VII. Living Out 321: Staying at the Water’s Edge
@@21708@@
Jesus is my Role Model. On the last day of the feast, he stood and cried out in a loud voice: “If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me and drink!” (John 7:37) He is the source of the living water, and he himself went into the deadest places: he sat at the well with the Samaritan woman, ate with tax collectors, and sat at the table with those who were put outside. The water of chapter 47 flows downward, to where the need is greatest. This is his way. To follow him is to learn from him to go to the low places, to go to the side of the people most in need.
@@21709@@
The Bible is my Standard. Verse 12 of chapter 47 puts the cause of the fruit in one sentence: because its waters issue out of the sanctuary. The tree does not bear fruit because it tries hard, but because it is near the water. The Bible is my Standard means this: I do not first ask how much I can do. I first ask: have I stayed at the water’s edge? Reading a small portion of Scripture each day is not for completing a schedule. It is for going to the water’s edge and drinking a mouthful.
@@21710@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. Some see this water as the Holy Spirit, and some do not, and we do not use this to judge anyone. We say only this: it is the Holy Spirit who brings us to the water’s edge, and it is the Holy Spirit who reminds us not to leave. He does not hurry us to jump to the deepest place at once. He uses his line, measuring one thousand cubits at a time, and leads us along.
@@21711@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is not that I bear fruit by effort, but that I stay at the water’s edge. I am no longer the one who keeps trying to fix the desolate corner by my own strength. I bring it to the water’s edge and let Jesus be King. It is no longer I, but Christ.
@@21712@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The fruit on the tree is not the tree’s credit, and the fish on the bank are not the fisherman’s credit. Everything is because of the water. If we see life renewed, let us not say: I did well. Let us say: this water issues out of the sanctuary, and the glory belongs to the Lord who makes the water flow.
@@21713@@
To build God’s system. A community that belongs to God is a place where water flows. It does not let the water pool up in itself. It lets the water flow to where the need is greatest, to the stranger, so that every person has an inheritance and a place. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: first stay at the water’s edge, then share a portion together with others, and only last the service that flows out from here.
@@21714@@
What does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with “staying at the water’s edge”?
@@21715@@
The connection is very direct. The water’s edge is where the King is, and the tree not leaving the water is letting him be King. We often say with our mouths that Jesus is King, but when we meet a desolate corner, our first reaction is to do it ourselves: I will fix it, I will save it, I will hold it up. Selflessness is not nothing. It is putting this matter into his hands and staying at the water’s edge. You can practice this: every morning say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to control today is this; what I most want to hold up by myself today is this; today I bring them to your water’s edge. After a while you will find that bearing fruit is no longer pressure. The water comes, and the tree naturally lives.
@@21716@@
The proud type says: I bore this fruit, I worked hard, so I have it. The text says to him: because its waters issue out of the sanctuary. Pride is leaving one’s position as a human being, seizing God’s power, and wanting to take God’s place. The proud type needs to learn: the tree only needs not to leave the water. You can first say one sentence to the Lord: every fruit I bear is your water.
@@21717@@
The fearful type says: the water is so deep, will I be overwhelmed? The text says to him: every time it is one thousand cubits, and the one who measures is always at your side, with the line in his hand. <p><strong>God’s portion may not be sold, exchanged, or handed over.</strong></p><p>Three “nots” in a row, and each verb is different: not sold (יִמְכְּרוּ), not exchanged (יָמֵר), not transferred (יַעֲבִיר). Every possible exit is blocked.</p><p>Why so strict? Because in the human world, the holiest things are often the easiest to trade for something else. Sell a little today, exchange a little tomorrow, hand it over the day after, and in the end nothing is left.</p><p>The text gives only one reason: “for it is holy to Yahweh.” Not because it is valuable, not because it is important, but because it is his. What belongs to him is not for people to dispose of.</p><p>This verse also echoes chapter 45: first lift out a portion for God. What is lifted out is no longer your own. God does not only want you to lift it out first, he wants you not to take it back again.</p><p>Landing it: is there something you once gave to God and then quietly took back? Time, money, a decision, a service. “Not to be sold, not to be exchanged”: this is God’s valuing of his portion, and also a kind of protection for us.</p>

<p><strong>① Why is the city “common use”?</strong></p><ul><li>The distinction between holy and common: the city does not belong to the holy land of the priests and the sanctuary</li><li>“Common use” is not a put-down, but shows that this is where ordinary people live and work day to day</li></ul><p>The root חֹל means “common, not holy,” set in contrast with קֹדֶשׁ (holy). In Ezekiel this distinction is meaningful, and God wants people to see the difference between holy and common (44:23).</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: The city is an everyday place, not a sanctuary. It is to be beside the holy land, but it is where ordinary people live ordinary days.</strong></p></blockquote>
@@21808@@
<p><strong>The city of daily life sits right beside the sanctuary.</strong></p><p>This long section has many measurements, and it is easy to get dizzy. We will hold on to just a few pictures.</p><p>The first picture: the city is called “common use” (חֹל). This word means ordinary, usual. Not holy, and not unclean, only everyday.</p><p>This is a very interesting design. The sanctuary has the standing of a sanctuary, and the city has the use of a city. People do not live in the sanctuary every day. People live in the city. God drew a city for people to live their days in: with dwellings, with pasture lands, with fields where food can be grown.</p><p>The second picture: “Those who labor in the city shall cultivate it.” The ones who work eat what they themselves have cultivated. People of different tribes work on the same land.</p><p>The third picture: the prince’s land is on both sides. The prince does not take the center. He is at the side. In the middle is the sanctuary, and around it are the priests, the Levites, and the city.</p><p>Landing it: has your “common use” life been treated as the part that has nothing to do with God? Going to work, cooking, raising children, these are the “city.” God drew it beside the sanctuary so that you would know your daily life is not far from him.</p>
