I. First Turn Your Face Toward Those Who Do Not Want to Listen (25:1–5)
@@11127@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Does your family have one of those relatives’ group chats? Usually it is only rows of good-morning pictures, and people really talk only at New Year. One day a message pops up in the group: your cousin’s shop has closed, and he owes a lot of money. The screen goes quiet for about three seconds. Then someone sends a smiley face, someone sends “I told you so,” and someone sends nothing at all and quietly turns the phone face down. Those three seconds are very short, but the voice in everyone’s heart shows itself in them. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 25, and what the text describes is exactly those three seconds. Only the group chat is replaced by four neighboring nations, and the cousin is replaced by Jerusalem.
@@11128@@
At the end of chapter 24, the prophet’s wife died, and he was not even allowed to weep aloud, and the people in the city were waiting for bad news. In chapter 25, the text suddenly turns its head. For the next several chapters God does not speak to Jerusalem, but to the neighbors gathered around her. The first one named is Ammon, the second and third are Moab and Edom, and the last is the Philistines. Please notice this first: when God’s people fall, who is standing beside them and what they say in their hearts, God keeps all of it in mind.
@@11129@@
Verse 2 says: “Son of man, set your face toward the children of Ammon, and prophesy against them.” The first action the prophet is to take is to “set his face.” The literal sense of the original is to turn the face toward them. Have you ever tried to speak to someone with their back turned to you? It is tiring, and what you say feels like it was never said. But God told the prophet to turn his face, to face a group of people who had no intention of listening. Ammon lived east of the Jordan, a distant relative of Israel, and their capital was Rabbah. They never imagined that the things they said outside the wall had been heard by someone.
@@11130@@
The first line of verse 3 is the one I like best: “Hear the word of the Lord Yahweh.” Please look at the order. God does not shut the door first and then pronounce sentence; he first has people stop and listen, and only then gives the reason. In his judgment there is always a door that has not been shut tight.
@@11131@@
The picture in verses 4 and 5 is plain. People from the wilderness pitch camp in your land, eat your fruit, drink your milk; Rabbah becomes a stable for camels, and the land of Ammon a resting place for flocks. Who “the people of the east” are, the text does not say, and we do not supply a name for it. You only need to see this: what one family worked hard to grow, in the end someone else sits in the courtyard eating. This is not fire and lightning; it is being slowly replaced. The city is still on the ground; it is just no longer a city.
@@11132@@
Why does God first say “Hear” to Ammon, instead of pronouncing sentence directly?
@@11133@@
Because this God’s way is to invite first and reason afterward. The call in verse 3 is “Hear the word of the Lord Yahweh,” and only then come the “because” and the “therefore.” This order reminds us that behind judgment there is still a hope: if people will stop and listen, there is still room to turn. It is the same for us. When you are about to say something unflattering in your heart, before it leaves your mouth, perhaps that moment is God saying: listen first. You can practice one small thing: before you comment on someone, pause three seconds in your heart and say to the Lord: I am listening. Those three seconds are often the beginning of a person standing up from the judge’s seat.
@@11134@@
II. One “Aha,” and Clapping and Stamping (25:3, 6–7)
@@11135@@
Verse 3 states Ammon’s charge. What they said was: “Aha!” This is the shortest sentence in the whole chapter, less than one word. But please count what is behind it: the sanctuary profaned, the land of Israel made desolate, the house of Judah taken into captivity, three losses, answered only with one “Aha.” They did not draw a sword, did not set a fire, did not even step closer. There was only one sound from the mouth, a snort with a laugh in it.
@@11136@@
It is like a family whose roof was torn off by a typhoon, and a neighbor standing under the arcade says: “Well, it should have been repaired long ago.” The sentence is so light that the speaker himself does not notice. But the family it was said about may remember it for years. We think that once words leave the mouth they scatter, but God says: I heard. He writes this one sound in the reasons column of the record of judgment.
@@11137@@
Verse 6 enlarges the picture: “Because you have clapped your hands, stamped with the feet, and rejoiced with all the contempt of your soul against the land of Israel.” Hands clapping, feet stamping, hearts rejoicing, the whole body taking part in this celebration, released frame by frame like slow motion. But the heaviest part is the phrase in the middle: “all the contempt of your soul.” This is not ordinary happiness; the meaning in the Hebrew is scorn, looking down on someone. They did not merely see others fall; they placed the fallen beneath themselves, and in doing so looked down also on the One who is with this people.
@@11138@@
God’s response is symmetrical. “Therefore, behold, I have stretched out my hand on you”: your hand clapped, my hand stretches out. Please notice that this verse does not say thunder falls from heaven. Verse 7 says “deliver you for a plunder to the nations,” that is, the position this people once held is taken by others. The excitement you gained at someone else’s breach, one day it will be your own breach’s turn.
@@11139@@
I must stop here and say something important about these verses. If you have been deeply hurt, and later that person ran into trouble, and your heart suddenly felt lighter, this verse is not rebuking you. You do not need to think you are bad because of that one breath. What this verse rebukes is the celebration with clapping and stamping, full of contempt; it is making another person’s pain your own show. A wound is a wound, a show is a show, and the two are not the same.
@@11140@@
Today we do not need to clap and stamp; holding a phone is enough. When someone is in trouble, we forward a post, leave a sarcastic remark, and everything is perfectly safe: no one hits you, and no one scolds you. But where did that remark come from? It came from the heart. What chapter 25 wants us to see is exactly this “where it came from.”
@@11141@@
I was hurt very deeply, and later that person ran into trouble, and I felt it served them right. Am I like Ammon?
@@11142@@
No. Please put down this condemnation first. What verse 6 rebukes is “clapping and stamping” and “all the contempt of your soul,” that is, celebrating another’s fall with scorn. For a deeply wounded person to feel a sigh of relief when the one who hurt them runs into trouble is a very real reaction after being hurt, and God will not use this verse to strike you. But you can honestly ask yourself two things: Am I breathing a sigh of relief, or am I laughing at him? Am I willing to hand this person, together with my wound, over to God? There is no need to hurry, and no need to pretend you have already let go. If the wound is deep and keeps being hard to put down, please find a pastor or a trusted companion to walk with you, and seek professional help if necessary.
@@11143@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Aha, Contempt, Vengeance (25:3, 6, 12)
@@11144@@
This chapter has three Hebrew words worth a close look, and each of them can be brought back into your own days within thirty seconds. The first is “Aha” in verse 3, the Hebrew הֶאָח (he’ach). It is not a sentence, only a sound, like our “Ha!” or “Serves them right!” The weight of this word is not in its meaning but in its speed: the mouth is faster than the heart, with no time even to explain. So you do not have to wait until you “want” to gloat; once that sound leaves your mouth, it already counts. Today’s practice is very simple: in the first second you hear bad news, see which word comes out of your mouth first.
@@11145@@
The second is “contempt” in verse 6, the Hebrew שָׁאט (sha’at), meaning scorn, disdain. This is the root of this section. “Aha” is the leaf, “clapping and stamping” is the branch, and this word is the root: looking down on people. You can tell the difference this way: disliking a person is my feeling about that person; despising a person is that I have arranged a position for him, and arranged it beneath me. Feelings we can honestly tell God, but positions are not ours to arrange.
@@11146@@
The third is “vengeance” in verse 12, the Hebrew נָקָם (naqam). In Edom’s charge, this root appears again and again within one verse, like someone reciting the same old account over and over. But by verse 14 the same root has changed its subject: “my vengeance” (נִקְמָתִי), and God says that Edom will know it is I who take vengeance. Vengeance was never placed in human hands. In other words, in this chapter the word goes full circle: from Edom’s pocket back to God’s hand.
@@11147@@
Put the three words together: one “Aha” is the mouth; “contempt” is the angle from which the eyes look at a person; “vengeance” is the old account clutched in the hand. From the mouth, to the eyes, to the hand, the route of a person’s gloating is laid open by the text. And at every stop God says: I heard, I saw, this is in my hand.
@@11148@@
What is the difference between “contempt” and ordinary “dislike”? How do I tell which kind I have?
@@11149@@
You can tell with three questions. First, am I talking about the matter, or about the person? “I do not agree with this matter” is dislike; “that kind of person is just like that” is contempt. Second, when this person falls, is there in my heart a feeling of “See, I told you”? If so, be careful. Third, can I pray for him, even one sentence? Even for someone we dislike, we can still ask for peace; for someone we despise, we do not even want to mention the name. Once you have told the difference, there is no need to blame yourself; just say to the Lord: Lord, I see it, please replace my eyes with your eyes.
@@11150@@
IV. Moab, Edom, Philistia: Three Kinds of Turning Away (25:8–17)
@@11151@@
The second neighbor is Moab, and verse 8 records one of their sayings: “Behold, the house of Judah is like all the nations.” It sounds like the tone of a news anchor, only a comment: Judah fell, like other countries, nothing special. But this sentence has two layers. The first layer is looking down on people; the second goes deeper: Judah was chosen by God, and to say they are “like all the nations” is to say that the One they believe in is nothing special either. On the surface it is said about Judah; in fact it is passing judgment on God.
@@11152@@
You have probably heard something similar: “Look, people who believe in Jesus are nothing special either.” God’s response is very interesting: he will open Moab’s “side.” Verse 9 uses a word for the body, the “shoulder,” where the line of defense is first exposed, and also Moab’s three proudest cities: Beth Jeshimoth, Baal Meon, and Kiriathaim. God begins from the place they were proudest of. This is not telling believers to put on a brave face when they fall; pain is pain, and you may cry, you may ask, you may say you do not know. Only in weakness, we do our best not to say “he cannot be trusted,” and not to add a stain to God’s name.
@@11153@@
The third neighbor is Edom. Edom was Israel’s kin, brothers from Jacob and Esau on. Verse 12 says they took “vengeance” and “greatly offended.” People of the same house know best where it hurts most when they wound; the betrayal of kin cuts deeper than the hostility of strangers. They kept the old account, and when Judah fell, they added to it. Old grudges in the human heart are the hardest to let go, and the moment there is a chance, the hand itches.
@@11154@@
What does God say? He says: this account is mine. The end of verse 14 is “Then they will know my vengeance.” The New Testament makes this meaning clearer: “Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay” (Romans 12:19). This sentence goes in two directions. To the one who wants revenge it says: let go, this is not your job. To the one who was hurt it says: God has seen, you do not have to wait for a chance to get it back, because he settles it himself. Letting go of revenge does not mean the harm does not count; it is exactly that you believe there is One who takes this more seriously than you do.
@@11155@@
Verse 14 has one more line that must be read carefully: God says he will lay his vengeance on Edom “by the hand of my people Israel.” Some read this as a historical arrangement, and some read it as God showing his judgment before them; the schools differ, but there is a common bottom line: that is “my anger” and “my wrath,” God’s, not Israel’s own. So no one can take this verse and lay a hand on anyone.
@@11156@@
Last are the Philistines. Verse 15 says they acted with “perpetual hostility.” This is hostility passed down through generations, like an old piece of furniture nobody wants but nobody dares to throw away: grandfather leaves it to father, father leaves it to the child, and the children do not yet know each other and have already learned to frown. God says, “I will execute great vengeance on them.” Please notice that this sentence is aimed at a long, unrelenting evil, not at someone’s anger in one moment. You do not need to fear that God will send disaster because you lost your temper once yesterday. Four nations, and every section ends with “know,” so that people will know him: this God sees, remembers, and deals with it himself.
@@11157@@
God says he will take vengeance on Edom “by the hand of my people Israel.” Can believers today act on God’s behalf?
@@11158@@
No. Verse 14 says clearly that it is “my anger” and “my wrath,” God himself taking vengeance; it is not authority handed to people. Romans 12:19 is even more direct: “Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay.” If this verse is used to defend revenge, it is read completely backward. If you are being hurt right now, handing the case to God does not mean you must endure the harm; you may seek appropriate protection, help, and just handling, and you do not have to carry it alone. Put the account in your heart into God’s hand, and your hand will be free to do what you really should do: protect yourself and accompany the wounded.
@@11159@@
Our family has an old feud of several decades, and I do not even know the reason. Does it have to do with “perpetual hostility”?
@@11160@@
It does, but please put down self-blame first. This kind of old feud was not made by you alone, and it does not need to be untangled by you alone. What the text points out is the unrelenting evil of “perpetual hostility,” not that you were dragged into a quarrel you did not choose. You can start with the smallest step: stop passing that old saying to the next generation, or say one sentence to God: I do not know how to settle this account, please take it over. There is no need to force yourself to reconcile right away. If this feud involves violence, threats, or harm, please first take care of your own and your family’s safety, and find a pastor or a professional for help.
@@11161@@
The first deviation is to preach chapter 25 as God’s condemnation of a certain group or a certain country, even using it to set a timetable or name some side in the modern world. This chapter is what God said to four neighboring nations of ancient times; it is not for pointing at others and saying “You are Ammon, you are Edom.” We want the congregation to turn and look at their own hearts, not to count other people’s sins.
@@11162@@
The second deviation is to preach verse 14, “by the hand of my people Israel,” as if believers may take revenge for God. The text says “my anger” and “I am the one who takes vengeance,” and Romans 12:19 also says vengeance belongs to God. We want the congregation to put down their hands and hand the case to God, not to want to act even more.
@@11163@@
The third deviation is to say to someone who has been deeply hurt: you felt a sigh of relief, so you are Ammon. Verse 6 rebukes the celebration with clapping and stamping, full of contempt, not the complicated feelings of the wounded. We want the wounded to be comforted, not condemned.
@@11164@@
The fourth deviation is to hurry a victim to “let go of revenge right now” or “forgive right now,” as if not letting go means not being spiritual enough. Letting go is handing the case to God; it does not mean the harm does not count, and still less does it mean staying in danger. We want the wounded to have time, protection, and company, not one more burden.
@@11165@@
The fifth deviation is to preach “they will know that I am Yahweh” as God intimidating people, or to use “like all the nations” in verse 8 to rebuke believers who have stumbled in weakness. The fruit of the former should be knowing God better, not fearing him more; the latter accuses the onlookers’ scorn, not the one who fell. We want the congregation to love the Lord more, and to dare more to come before him in weakness.
@@11166@@
The first problem: we are very used to making other people’s falls a topic of conversation. At the dinner table, in group chats, in the break room, “I am only concerned about him” is said very politely, and by the end it is often a gathering of clapping and stamping, just without the sound. The reminder of chapter 25 is: God heard that sound. You can start like this: the next time someone mentions that so-and-so is in trouble, first say, “Shall we pray for him for a moment?” even if it is only ten seconds. You will find the topic changes direction, and the position in your heart changes with it.
@@11167@@
The second problem: Chinese families often have old accounts, two branches not in contact, not sitting at the same table at New Year, and the children do not yet know why, yet have already learned not to say hello. The Philistines’ “perpetual hostility” is speaking of exactly this hostility passed down. You can start like this: there is no need to reconcile right away; first, before God, say which account this is and who it is, and then say: this account I hand to you. At least you can decide not to pass it on to the next generation again. If this feud involves harm or threats, please protect yourself first and find a pastor to walk with you.
@@11168@@
I do not want to have contact with the relative who hurt me. Does that count as “perpetual hostility”?
@@11169@@
Not necessarily. “Perpetual hostility” means treating hostility as nourishment, feeding it continually, passing it on to others, even hoping the other person falls. But “no contact” may be a form of protection, especially when the other person has caused harm. You can discern: am I keeping my distance for safety, or so that he will never have a good day? The former is wisdom; the latter needs to be brought before God. Handing the case to God does not equal restoring relationship with the one who hurt you, and certainly does not equal going back into danger. What you need is a safe place to tell the hurt, perhaps a pastor, perhaps professional counseling; go slowly, and you do not need to walk it all at once.
@@11170@@
VII. Living Out 321: Leave the Judge’s Seat, Hand the Old Account to Him
@@11171@@
Jesus is my Role Model. Luke 19:41 says: “When he came near, he saw the city and wept over it.” This city would later reject him, and when he saw it, he did not clap; he wept. Where others fall, he crouches down first. We who follow him learn this order: first see the pain, then the fault.
@@11172@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 25 not to rank four neighboring nations, but to let God’s word speak to us first: “Hear the word of the Lord Yahweh.” God first has people listen, and we listen first too. Only after listening does it become our turn to ask: where did the “Aha” in my heart come from?
@@11173@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. That light little thought is often so fast we cannot stop it; at that moment it is the Holy Spirit within us who lights up that sound. He does not come to condemn; he comes to remind: Do you see it? Each day, quiet yourself for a few minutes and ask: Lord, what sound in my heart today needs to be heard by you?
@@11174@@
Let Jesus be King. The root of pride is leaving man’s position, seizing God’s authority, and wanting to take God’s place. Clapping and stamping is wanting to sit in the judge’s seat and declare that this person got what they deserved. Selflessness is not having no feelings; it is that I no longer sit in that chair: let man be man, and let God be God. When Jesus is King, the old account in my heart has its true owner.
@@11175@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we learn to crouch down when others fall, instead of clapping, we must not say, “I have finally become more loving.” That is his heart in me, and the glory belongs to him. At the same time, God himself carrying out vengeance, and himself making people know that he is Yahweh, is also his glory, and does not need us to do it for him.
@@11176@@
To build God’s system. A community that belongs to God is a place that does not treat a brother’s or sister’s fall as a show. Here, when someone fails, there is no gossip and no secret delight; when someone is hurt, they are first accompanied and not hurried. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: if the “Aha” in our life is not dealt with by the Lord, there will be sneering in our relationships and comparison in our ministry.
@@11177@@
Does “not sitting in the judge’s seat” mean I cannot point out other people’s faults?
@@11178@@
No. Not sitting in the judge’s seat does not mean not telling the truth. In the text God himself states sin very clearly, the prophet is also commanded to “prophesy against” them, and God states the reasons one by one. The difference is in position and heart: am I standing beside the wounded, speaking with love and responsibility, or sitting up high, enjoying watching someone fall? When you need to point out a person’s problem, you can first ask yourself three things: Is this for his good, or for my satisfaction? After I have spoken, am I willing to walk with him for a while? Can I pray for him first? If the answers lean toward the former, this is a reminder to crouch down, not a judgment of clapping and stamping.
@@11179@@
The proud type says: “Of course I would not gloat; I was only telling the truth.” The text says to him: Hear the word of the Lord Yahweh. The proud put a coat of “the truth” on their contempt. Returning to one’s position is admitting: I have that sound too. You can first say to God: Lord, I see it.
@@11180@@
The fearful type says: “If God remembers even one ‘Aha,’ am I about to be judged at any moment?” Please read it once more: God first has people listen, and then states the reason. He remembers because he is with those who hurt, not to catch you. The fearful type needs to learn to replace “afraid of being caught” with “willing to be lit up,” beginning with the smallest matter, confessing it to him.
@@11181@@
The shame type says: “I have had that kind of thought in my heart; am I completely rotten?” Shame says: you are that kind of person. The gospel says: you saw it, and this is the starting point of turning back. That sound is in human hearts to begin with; the point is not that it is absent, but that it is brought to him. You do not have to clean yourself up first before coming to him.
@@11182@@
The wounded type says: “They hurt me so deeply, and now that they are in trouble, I only feel they deserve to die.” God does not rebuke your wound. He knows what you have been through, and he also says “Vengeance belongs to me.” The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell the story. The one accompanying should not rush to say “You must forgive,” but only say: “I am here, I will stay with you.”
@@11183@@
First, this week notice once: when you hear someone is in trouble, what is the first thought that comes up in your heart? No need to polish it; write down one sentence, and write down the date. Then say to the Lord: Lord, I see it.
@@11184@@
Second, choose the person or topic you most often use as conversation material, and this week, in one conversation, say one sentence: “Shall we pray for him for a moment?” Name that person and the time.
@@11185@@
Third, write down one old account you have been clutching in your hand: who it is, which matter. Each day for three minutes, say to God: this account is in your hand. Write down the day you start.
@@11186@@
Fourth, find someone you trust and say in one sentence one hurt you cannot put down, without details. Ask him only to listen, not to give advice, and to pray for you; name him and the time you agreed on.
@@11187@@
Fifth, this week do one practical small thing for someone who is falling: a message, a cup of tea, a time of company. No comment, only ask: “How can I help you?” Name the person and the date.
@@11188@@
I declare: God hears every sound in my heart; he is not here to condemn me, but to make my heart soft.
@@11189@@
I declare: I do not sit in the judge’s seat; when others fall, I crouch down, and I do not clap.
@@11190@@
I declare: vengeance belongs to God, I hand the old account to him; I will not pass hatred on to the next generation.
@@11191@@
I declare: they will know that he is Yahweh; I want to know him more and love him more.
@@11192@@
The simplest thing is to take a piece of paper today and write: The “Aha” in my heart lately was toward ______. No need to show it to anyone. Then say to God: Lord, I see it. Next choose one small action you can do today: delete a piece of gossip on your phone that you were about to forward, or send a sentence to someone who is sad: “I thought of you; I am here when you need me.” At the next small group, just say: I did one thing this week, and no need to say what it was. The answer of chapter 25 often begins with such a small turn: not settling all the old accounts at once, but saying one less sentence today and listening one more time.
@@11193@@
Chapter 25 has a very quiet structure. Every neighbor has a “because,” and only then a “therefore”; every section ends with “know.” God does not judge arbitrarily; he states his reasons: I heard you clap, I heard that line of yours, “like all the nations,” I saw your old account, I saw the hatred you passed down. He remembers everything. This is a warning and also a comfort: a warning to those who treat other people’s pain as a joke, and a comfort to those who have been laughed at. Your pain, God has seen; others’ laughter, he has also heard.
@@11194@@
So today’s lesson is not about counting the neighbors’ sins, but about turning this mirror toward ourselves. Where others have fallen, have we crouched down? Where we ourselves are wounded, have we handed the case to him? Jesus, who wept outside the city of Jerusalem, is our Role Model, and also our Lord.
@@11195@@
Please take three sentences home with you: listen first, speak afterward; the old account is in his hand; when others fall, crouch down. The focus of this lesson is not who is Ammon or who is Edom, and not how others laugh at me, but Jesus, who hears everything and yet did not come to condemn.
