I. The Ship Is Still on the Water, and the Song Has Already Begun (27:1–11)
@@11939@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. A brother of mine opened a shop, and I went to congratulate him. There were twenty-some flower baskets at the door, red and green, lined up all the way to the edge of the road. He stood at the entrance and read them out to me one by one: who sent this one, who sent that one. When he came to the last, he smiled and said: Pastor, I built this shop from nothing, all by myself. I nodded and said nothing. I knew how many nights he had stayed up. But I also remembered that the signboard over the door was written for him free of charge by a friend; the money for the first batch of goods was lent by his father-in-law; and the craftsman who did the fitting out was introduced by an old brother in the church. When he said “all by myself,” there was no ill will in it at all. People are like this: the work of many hands gets gathered under one name, and we do not even notice. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 27, the story of a ship.
@@11940@@
Verse 1 says, the word of Yahweh came to me again. In verse 2 God commands: “You, son of man, take up a lamentation over Tyre.” Please notice the timing. The ship is still on the water, the sails are full, the harbor is bustling, the hold is packed, and no one can see anything wrong. Yet God says: sing a lament. A lament is a song sung at a funeral. He asks the prophet to start singing while the ship is still afloat. People see the present; he sees the end of the sea road. And he does not look coldly. He looks with an aching heart.
@@11941@@
In verse 3, Tyre says: “I am perfect in beauty.” Who says this? There are two ways to read it. The punctuation of the CUV makes it sound as if the Lord Yahweh is speaking. Not a few scholars, following the tone of the original, read it as Tyre’s own words, meaning “Tyre, you are the one who said it,” the mark she gave herself. These notes take the second reading, because 28:2, “I am a god,” is written the same way: first the other party’s words are quoted, then God answers him. Neither reading leads you astray; the second just makes the sentence more like a mirror.
@@11942@@
Is “I am perfect in beauty” Tyre’s own saying, or God’s? Does the difference between the two readings matter for us?
@@11943@@
The difference is small. What matters is that neither reading should become “God finds her beautiful as a fault.” The first reading takes verse 3 as God quoting Tyre’s boast, and then answering in verse 4; the second takes it as Tyre’s verdict on herself. Both admit that the beauty was real, and God did not call her ugly. If you meet different readings in your small group, there is no need to argue. You can close like this: the point of this sentence is not who said it, but whether something follows it that names the one who made her. You can also ask yourself: what is the “I am perfect…” I most often say about myself? Can I add after it: this is what you gave me?
@@11944@@
Verse 4 is the quietest sentence in the whole chapter: “Your borders are in the heart of the seas. Your builders have perfected your beauty.” The beauty was real, and God did not call her ugly. But the beauty was made by “your builders.” The original word for “your builders” means those who build ships: carpenters, shipwrights, pairs of hands with calluses. Her beauty was given to her by others from the first day. She looked at her own reflection on the water every day, and after looking so long, she thought it had grown out of herself.
@@11945@@
Verses 5 to 9 are a bill of materials for building the ship. Planks of cypress from Senir, a cedar from Lebanon for the mast, oars from the oaks of Bashan, benches of ivory inlaid in cypress wood from the islands of Kittim. The sail was fine embroidered linen from Egypt, and the awning was blue and purple from the islands of Elishah. The rowers were the inhabitants of Sidon and Arvad, and the repairers of the ship’s seams were the old men and wise men of Gebal. Count them: not one plank of this ship grew on Tyre’s own mountains. The wood came from the mountains, the cloth from Egypt, the rowers from Sidon, the menders from Gebal. One ship was the hands of a whole coastline, yet the nameplate on the dock bore only two words: Tyre.
@@11946@@
Verses 10 and 11 add armies: “They were in your army, your men of war. They hung the shield and helmet in you. They showed your beauty.” “They have perfected your beauty.” Even her “honor” and her “beauty” were hung up for her by others. This is the finest point of self-centredness: not stealing, but not mentioning other people’s names. In the heart we feel that all this is only right, and the thanks grow fewer and fewer.
@@11947@@
II. A Very Long List of Cargo (27:12–25)
@@11948@@
Verses 12 to 25 are the most ledger-like passage in the whole book. Tarshish brought silver, iron, tin and lead; Javan, Tubal and Meshech came; the house of Togarmah brought horses and mules; Dedan brought ivory and ebony; Aram brought emeralds, fine linen and coral; Damascus sent wine of Helbon and white wool; the Arabs and the princes of Kedar sent lambs; the traders of Sheba and Raamah sent the best of spices, precious stones and gold. By the end you get dizzy. That is exactly the effect the text wants. The whole world was sending its best to this one ship. Verse 25 sums it up: “The ships of Tarshish were your caravans for your merchandise. You were replenished and made very glorious in the heart of the seas.”
@@11949@@
Let us not rush to call this a bad thing. Trade is not a sin, cargo is not a sin, wealth is not a sin. The text does not rebuke anyone for doing business, and verse 17 says that Judah and the land of Israel are on this list too; they came to trade with wheat, honey, oil and balm. This is an honest reminder: believers are also in this network, and also have to earn a living and get through their days. The question has never been whether there is cargo, but whom we are thinking of once the cargo is loaded on the ship.
@@11950@@
Once at an airport, I saw a woman crouching in front of the counter, her face flushed red. Her luggage was overweight, and the staff asked her to take a few things out. She took out the clothes one piece at a time, and could not bear to part with any of them. In the end she said: But I will use every one of them! But a ship, like a suitcase, has a size. You can load more and more cargo, but the ship is only so big. However much you load, it will not make a ship unsinkable; loading more only makes it sink heavier.
@@11951@@
If I do business and have an income, am I less spiritual?
@@11952@@
No. Chapter 27 does not say a single word that doing business is a sin, and not a single word that poverty is holiness. What the text rebukes is calling what others and God have given “mine,” and then leaning on it. You can check yourself with three questions. First, of what I have earned, can I name the people who helped me? Second, in all my busyness, do I still have a stretch of time that belongs only to God? Third, if half this cargo were gone tomorrow, would my peace still be there? Psalm 24:1 says: “The earth is Yahweh’s, with its fullness; the world, and those who dwell in it.” The cargo is his, the ship is his, and we are stewards. Doing our work well and giving the glory to him do not conflict.
@@11953@@
There is one word in the list that we cannot skip over quickly. Verse 13 says: “They traded the persons of men and vessels of bronze for your merchandise.” People were put on the list of cargo. I do not intend to enlarge that picture. I will only say this: the text records it, but not in approval. A person is not cargo, not a number, not a price; every person was made in the image of God. If you, or someone you know, has ever been treated as something to be traded, this verse is not speaking about your worth. It is saying that God saw. You do not need to share any details, and you do not need to be told to be strong.
@@11954@@
Jesus said: “For what will it profit a man, if he gains the whole world, and forfeits his life?” These words do not tell us not to work. They remind us that a ship has a size, and a life has weight. A ship that is full is not the same as a life that is full.
@@11955@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Lament, Perfect, Broken (27:2, 3, 26)
@@11956@@
This chapter has three Hebrew words, and each of them can be taken home and used within thirty seconds.
@@11957@@
The first is “lament,” the Hebrew קִינָה (qinah), in verse 2. This is a lament in the strict sense, sung at funerals, when mourning the dead. Some scholars say the rhythm of this kind of poem is “a long beat, a short beat,” like walking and gasping at the same time, a sigh that cannot be finished in one breath. God told the prophet to sing a funeral song over a city that was still alive. That gives us a use for daily life: some words must be said before things go wrong. On the day a child gets into a good school, besides saying congratulations, we can also say: do not forget the people who stayed up late with you. We do not have to wait until we have lost something before we learn to give thanks.
@@11958@@
The second is “perfect,” the Hebrew כְּלִילַת (kelilat), the “perfect in beauty” of verse 3, meaning complete, entire, without a single lack. This same root is also used for the sacrifice burned “whole” on the altar, offered up entirely, with not one piece kept back. When a person says he is “perfect” in something, be careful, because “perfect” is a verdict that suits only God. Our everyday versions are: I have it all handled, I have no problem, I understand my children best. Every one of them is giving ourselves full marks.
@@11959@@
The third is “broken,” the Hebrew שָׁבַר (shavar). Verse 26 says: “The east wind has broken you in the heart of the seas.” The “broken” in verse 34 is נִשְׁבֶּרֶת. This is the word for a bow snapped, a vessel smashed, a bone broken. What is interesting is that Psalm 34:18 says: “Yahweh is near to those who have a broken heart.” The “broken heart” there uses the very same root in the original, meaning “those whose hearts have been broken.” The same word: in this chapter it is the ship broken by the east wind; in the Psalms it is God drawing near to those whose hearts are broken.
@@11960@@
Put the three words together and you have the road of this chapter: God first sings the lament (qinah), because a ship that thinks itself perfect (kelilat) will one day be broken (shavar); but the God who sings the lament is also the God who is near to those whose hearts are broken.
@@11961@@
Does the lament mean that God has already decided this ship must sink?
@@11962@@
This chapter does declare that the ship will sink, but the sound of the lament is not gloating. In Ezekiel 18:23, God says, “Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked?” and 33:11 says: “I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live.” So you can hold two things at once: God is righteous, and a proud ship will not float forever; God is also merciful, and he weeps over a ship that is going to sink. A lament is sung to people while there is still a chance. The position of these notes is: the lament shows God’s heart, not God’s cold eye. If you have heard this song, there is still a chance to turn around and hand the helm to him.
@@11963@@
IV. The East Wind, the Deep Waters, and the Weeping on the Shore (27:26–36)
@@11964@@
Verse 26 is one sentence, and very quiet: “Your rowers have brought you into great waters. The east wind has broken you in the heart of the seas.” Please notice that no enemy sank this ship. It was its own rowers, stroke by stroke, who rowed it out into water too deep. Then came the wind, the east wind. This wind is not something we can arrange.
@@11965@@
Many people are like this: rowing very hard, rowing very successfully, rowing until they do not know themselves how far from shore they have come. It is not that they stopped believing; they just had no time to pray, no time to ask: Lord, is this the place you want me to go? Then the wind comes: an illness, a layoff, a phone call in the middle of the night. At that moment some people find out that they were sitting on a ship, not standing on the shore. This discovery is very painful, but it may also be the beginning of grace.
@@11966@@
Verse 27 is another list: “Your riches, your wares, your merchandise, your mariners, your pilots, your repairers of ship seams, the dealers in your merchandise, and all your men of war, who are in you, with all your company which is among you, will fall into the heart of the seas in the day of your ruin.” You see, this list is exactly the same as the list that praised her earlier. When they came up, it was splendor; when they go down, it is these same things. The cargo and the people are on the same ship.
@@11967@@
I want to pause here. If you are losing something right now: work, health, a business, a loved one, this passage is not saying that you deserve it. This is a lament sung over a proud city, not a judgment on those who suffer. You do not need to search these verses for your own fault. You can say not one word, and just sit. God is often nearest to us at our most painful moments.
@@11968@@
Verses 28 to 32 move the picture to the shore: the pilots cry out, the mariners come down from their ships and stand on the land, some cast dust on their heads, gird themselves with sackcloth, weep bitterly, and then take up a lamentation over you. You see, someone later took up that song. These people used to do business with her, and now they weep for her. There is real affection even in a relationship of buying and selling. Verse 33 adds: “When your wares went out of the seas, you filled many peoples. You enriched the kings of the earth with the multitude of your riches and of your merchandise.” Her wrong was not in supplying others, but in gathering the glory of supplying under her own name.
@@11969@@
Verses 34 to 36 are the end of this city: broken by the seas in the depths of the waters, the merchandise and all the company falling within her, the inhabitants of the islands astonished at you, the merchants hissing at you. This is a declaration against this one city. We will not use it to set any timetable, and we will not use it to point at any modern harbor. We only ask one plain question: where is my ship right now? Is it near the shore, or has it already been rowed out into great waters?
@@11970@@
If I am going through loss right now, what can this chapter say to me?
@@11971@@
First, a word: you do not need to feel, because of this chapter, that you are the sinking ship. This is a lament over a proud city, not a rebuke of those who suffer. Second, in this chapter there are people who weep for her, and God sings a lament for her; your tears are not weakness, and they are seen. Third, there are three words you can take with you: God is near to those whose hearts are broken. You do not have to get better first before you may come to him. This week you can do just one very small thing: say to God one sentence: I am in great pain right now, I do not know what to say, please come near to me. If this pain is already affecting your sleep, eating or work, please talk with a pastor or someone you trust, and if necessary ask for professional help too.
@@11972@@
The first deviation is to preach chapter 27 as “doing business and having wealth is a sin.” What the text rebukes is calling what others and God have given “my own,” and leaning on it. We want business people and working people to be more thankful and more humble, not to stick a label on anyone.
@@11973@@
The second deviation is to say to someone who is unemployed, bankrupt or sick: you are that sinking ship. Chapter 27 is a lament over a proud city, not an accusation against those who suffer. We want the suffering to be comforted, not condemned.
@@11974@@
The third deviation is to pass lightly over the “persons of men” in verse 13 as an ordinary item of trade. The text puts people on the list of cargo, and God saw it. We want the congregation to value every person more, and to be more alert to those around us who are being treated as tools.
@@11975@@
The fourth deviation is to enlarge the pictures of sinking, wailing, dust and ashes, so that those who have lost loved ones or been through trauma are struck again. The pictures are meant to bring people to fear God, not to frighten people. We want people to know God’s mercy better, not to be more afraid.
@@11976@@
The fifth deviation is to preach the “lament” as God gloating, or to treat this chapter as a timetable predicting some modern harbor. That God commanded a lament shows his heart that takes no pleasure in people sinking; these notes set no timetable, count no days, and name no modern country. We want the congregation to love the Lord more, not to panic more.
@@11977@@
Some say this chapter is urging people “not to succeed” and “not to work hard.” Is that right?
@@11978@@
No. The ship in chapter 27 is very beautiful, and the craftsmen who built it did their work well; the text does not say these things were wrong. What it rebukes is calling all of it “mine,” and, out in the deep water, not even asking who made you. So we need neither belittle labor nor worship results. You can discern it like this: when I work hard, are the words in my heart “I am doing this well for God,” or “I want people to see how good I am”? The first gives glory to him; the second gathers the glory under my own name. Work done well is something to be thankful for; only, before saying “this is mine,” say one sentence first: this is what you gave me.
@@11979@@
The first question: we are very used to saying our achievements were “fought for by myself.” In Chinese culture, building up from nothing is a glorious story, and we also teach our children: relying on yourself is the most reliable. There is nothing wrong with that, but said for long, it is easy to forget the hands behind us: the money lent by the father-in-law, the client introduced by an old colleague, the wife who never complained. You can begin like this: this month, find someone who once helped you, and say a specific thank-you face to face, saying which matter and which year. Do not only say “thank you for your help”; say the name.
@@11980@@
The second question: we are very used to using cargo, face and show to prove that we are doing well. The number of tables at a wedding banquet, the neighborhood where we live, the school our children attend, often become a silent list. The list in chapter 27, in the eyes of others, is splendor; in the eyes of God, it is a lament not yet finished. You can check yourself like this: this month, was there an expense made mainly so that others would see it? If so, you can choose one and spend it instead on someone truly in need, without letting others know.
@@11981@@
My family expects me to be very successful. How can I work hard and yet not be filled up with “cargo”?
@@11982@@
First, discern: is what your family expects your success, or your peace? Very often they are afraid that you will not be doing well. You can find a time to tell your family one true thing: I very much want to do things well, but I also want to keep some time for God and for family. Then set yourself one very small boundary, for example one evening each week without overtime, ten minutes of quiet each day. In this way you are not giving up hard work, but remembering: a ship has a size, and a life has weight. If under this pressure you have long had insomnia or a low mood, please talk with a pastor or someone you trust, and if necessary seek professional help.

VII. Living Out 321: Hand the Helm to Him
@@11984@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the Lord who created all things, yet he said: “I am gentle and humble in heart.” He did not say, “I am perfect in beauty”; he sat in a small fishing boat, even the pillow borrowed from others, and gave the glory to the Father. Those who follow him learn this pattern: to speak out the hands of others and the hand of God.
@@11985@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 27 not to study ancient trade routes, but to let the text ask us: whose tree is the plank of my ship? Whose cloth is my sail? Have I taken “your builders” out of my own story? The one sentence in verse 4, “Your builders have perfected your beauty,” is the standard by which I check myself.
@@11986@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. The Holy Spirit makes us willing to stop for a moment when the rowing is going best; makes us ask, before the wind comes: Lord, is this the place you want me to go? Every day, in a few quiet moments, ask him to show you: today, which thing that others gave have I called my own?
@@11987@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness means letting go of “this is mine”; it does not mean there is no me. The one who lets Jesus be King lets him sit at the helm of my ship, and no longer rows alone into great waters; when the wind of verse 26 comes, the helm is not in my hands, and I am not afraid.
@@11988@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. Every plank on the ship, every sail, every pair of hands on the oars is grace; when I succeed, I do not write only my own name on the nameplate. I give the glory to him, and to those who gave together with me.
@@11989@@
To build God’s system. A group that belongs to God is not the biggest ship, nor the dock with the most cargo, but a place where everyone is entrusted in his hand: thanking one another, speaking one another’s names, giving glory to God together. Such a church is a ship built by many hands, with the helm in the hand of Jesus.
@@11990@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “hand the helm to him”?
@@11991@@
The relationship is direct. The helm is the power of deciding direction. With our mouths we say Jesus is King, but when we are busy, our first reaction is to row by ourselves. To hand the helm to him is not to stop rowing, but at every moment of decision to ask first: Lord, is this the place you want me to go? You can practice like this: before leaving the house each day, say three sentences: what I most want to control today; what I am most afraid of not having today; today I place them in your hands. One day you will find that handing over the helm is not losing, but entering the limitless: no longer only my strength, but his road.
@@11992@@
The proud type says: “All of this I fought for by myself.” The text says to him: your builders perfected your beauty. A proud person leaves his position and gathers what others and God have given under his own name; to return to one’s position is to say the names: this is so-and-so, this is God. You can begin with one sentence: “This is what you gave me.”
@@11993@@
The fearful type says: “I have to load more cargo to feel safe.” The text says to him: however much cargo, the ship is only so big. The fearful type often uses more and more cargo to press down the unease in his heart. You can learn from one thing: load one less unnecessary piece of cargo, and keep that time for God.
@@11994@@
The ashamed type says: “I am that sinking ship, I deserve it.” The text does not say that. This is a lament over a proud city, not a condemnation of you. Shame says: you have already sunk; the gospel says: he is near to those whose hearts are broken. You do not need to get better first before you may come.
@@11995@@
The wounded type says: “I gave everything, and what I got in return was storms and waves. I do not want to believe anymore.” God does not blame you for being wounded; he wept over the ship, and he has wept over you. The wounded can slowly find someone trustworthy and tell a small part of the story; the one accompanying should not hurry to say “you must forgive,” but only say: “I am here.”
@@11996@@
First, write down three things you most often call “mine,” and beside each one, write the name of a person who helped you obtain it.
@@11997@@
Second, to one of them, say a specific thank-you: which matter, which year, and write down on which day you said it.
@@11998@@
Third, each day for three minutes, say to God: “This is what you gave me, and it is yours.” Place one thing you own before him.
@@11999@@
Fourth, choose one expense, not for the sake of face, and spend it on someone in need, without letting many people know.
@@12000@@
Fifth, on your busiest day, set aside ten minutes and ask: “Lord, is this the place you want me to go?” and write down the first thought you hear.
@@12001@@
I declare: the one who made me made me beautiful; everything I have is created, and entrusted to me.
@@12002@@
I declare: I will not call what others and God have given mine; I am willing to say the names.
@@12003@@
I declare: my ship has a size, and my life has weight; I do not need cargo to prove my worth.
@@12004@@
I declare: I hand the helm to Jesus; when the wind comes, he is on my ship.
@@12005@@
The simplest way is to take a piece of paper today and write this sentence: the one thing I have most often called “mine” lately is ______, and the person who helped me is ______. No need to polish it, and no need to show it to anyone. Then, right now, send a short message to the person who helped you: I thought of you today, thank you for helping me back then. It does not need to be long, and it does not need a reason. If you cannot think of anyone, then say first to God: this is what you gave me, thank you. This action is the answer of chapter 27: say the name out loud, instead of leaving only the two words “Tyre.” At the next small group meeting, just say one sentence: this week I said thank you to one person, and there is no need to say the details.
@@12006@@
Chapter 27 is one very long lament. It begins with a ship that is perfect in beauty and ends with a silent stretch of sea. At the beginning, God tells the prophet: take up a lamentation over Tyre; in the middle is a long list of the wood that built the ship and the cargo brought in; at the end is the east wind, the deep water, the people on the shore casting dust on their heads. From the dock to the deep, from splendor to silence, God was not for one moment a cold onlooker.
@@12007@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that all my beauty and cargo are created, and entrusted to me; God also wants us to see that he is a God who takes no pleasure in people sinking, and so while the ship is still afloat, he begins to sing a lament. That God, in Jesus, stepped into our ship and said: I am gentle and humble in heart.
@@12008@@
So please remember the three key points of today’s lesson: ship, cargo, sinking. The focus of this lesson is not how big your ship is, not how full your cargo is, and not how great the storm is, but Jesus, who sits at the helm.
@@12009@@
Lord, thank you that the one who made us made us beautiful. Forgive us for calling what others and you have given ours; and draw near to every person whose heart is broken. Let us hand the helm to you and give the glory to you. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, amen.

The pride that is hardest for people to discover often does not grow in the place of failure, but in the place of success: the thing gets done, the talent is noticed, the family business is secure, the position is won, and the heart quietly lifts itself up. Ezekiel chapter 28 puts this in a king: he had wisdom, trade and treasure, and finally said: “I am a god, I sit in the seat of God, in the middle of the seas.” God’s answer is very short: “You are man, and not God.” Then he sings a lament for him, counting up all that he had received in the garden of God, and pointing out that the fire came out from his own middle. This chapter is not meant to make us laugh at an ancient king, but to make us ask: where has my chair been moved to? At the end, God says again: “I will be glorified among you,” and promises that those who were scorned will dwell securely.

<p><strong>① What does “I am a god” mean?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>A literal claim to deity</strong>: the king of Tyre posed as a god, and in religious language called himself a god</li><li><strong>An exaggerated self-description</strong>: a man who dominates a region sees himself as equal to God (“I sit in the seat of God”)</li></ul><p>The מוֹשַׁב in אֱלֹהִים אֱלֹהִים can mean “God” or “gods”; so can “setting the heart as the heart of God.”</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: whichever it is, he put himself in the place that belongs only to God, and God’s answer is only one sentence: “You are man, not God.”</strong> This is the same thing that the issue “The King of Tyre and Satan: Who Is Chapter 28 About?” says: the root is “your heart is lifted up, and you have said, ‘I am a god.’ ”</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>“Yet you are man, and not God, though you set your heart as the heart of God”: the shortest diagnosis in the whole chapter.</strong></p><p>In the last chapter, Tyre was a ship. In this chapter, the ship has an owner: the prince of Tyre. God has the prophet speak to him, and the first sentence is not “you will be punished,” but “because your heart is lifted up, and you have said, ‘I am a god.’ ”</p><p>The original is very clear: the problem is in the “heart.” גָּבַהּ לִבְּךָ literally means “your heart rose high.” It was not that his wealth was too great, not that his ship was too big, but that his heart slowly lifted itself up. It lifted until he sat in “the seat of God,” and even picked a place for himself: “in the middle of the seas.”</p><p>God’s answer is also very short: וְאַתָּה אָדָם וְלֹא אֵל, “you are man, and not God.” This is not humiliation, but restoration. A person can be clever, can be rich, can be honored by others, but he is still a man. No one has ever crossed that line.</p><p>Landing it: few of us would ever say “I am a god.” But we say in our hearts: I have the final word on this, that person must listen to me, I do not need anyone. Each thought moves a little closer to that chair. There is only one way out: humility, setting down the seat we want to sit in, returning to the human position, letting man be man and letting God be God.</p>

<p><strong>Wisdom itself is not the problem; “relying on yourself” is.</strong></p><p>God first acknowledged his wisdom: “you are wiser than Daniel.” The sentence sounds like praise, going along with his boast and continuing from there.</p><p>Then look at the chain of verbs: by your wisdom and understanding you have gotten riches, and have gotten gold and silver into your treasures; by your great wisdom and your trading you have increased your riches; and because of your riches your heart is lifted up. This is a chain: wisdom turned into wealth; wealth became pride. Every link looks natural, and not one makes anyone feel: uh-oh.</p><p>Notice the preposition: “by your own” (בְּ). The text does not say he should not have had wisdom, and does not say he should not have had wealth. The text only says that he decided it was “I” who made it.</p><p>Landing it: the most dangerous thing is not that we have no talent, but that we have a great deal of talent, and gather the credit into our own pockets, one inch at a time. If you have some ability today, please say one sentence quietly before you sleep: all this is what you gave me.</p>

<p><strong>He said “I am a god.” God asks him: in front of the one who kills you, will you still say it?</strong></p><p>God’s answer is a symmetry. Verse 2: you set your heart as the heart of God; verse 6: therefore. Because he put himself in God’s place, God let him walk the road that place leads to: strangers drawing swords against “the beauty of your wisdom” that he had won, his brightness defiled, he himself brought down to the pit, dying in the heart of the seas that he boasted of.</p><p>Verse 9 is the sharpest sentence in the passage: “Will you yet say before him who kills you, ‘I am God’?” It is a question, yet needs no answer. A man under the sword is a man. All his titles, his treasury, his wisdom, at that moment cannot speak for him.</p><p>Verse 10 says he will die “the death of the uncircumcised,” meaning he will die like those outside the covenant, not remembered, with no honor. He thought he sat in God’s place, yet died lonelier than anyone.</p><p>Landing it: everyone will have a moment when he has no choice but to admit he is “man”: illness, loss, old age, a diagnosis. If we are willing to bow our heads now, that day need not be an admission forced upon us.</p>

<p><strong>① חוֹתֵם תָּכְנִית (the CUV “you lacked nothing”)</strong></p><ul><li><strong>A perfect seal</strong>: “you were the seal of full measure,” like a signet, everything up to standard</li><li><strong>One who seals</strong>: reading חוֹתֵם as an action: “you seal up the sum of the measure”</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: a model of perfection.</strong> The wording is hard, but the direction is not disputed: this is someone who lacked nothing.</p></blockquote><p><strong>② תֻּפֶּיךָ וּנְקָבֶיךָ (drums and pipes):</strong> both words are uncertain.</p><ul><li><strong>Instruments</strong>: drums and pipes (the CUV, an old tradition)</li><li><strong>Jeweler’s craft</strong>: settings and sockets (the context is all about jewels)</li></ul><p>It does not affect the direction of the whole passage.</p><p><strong>③ In Eden, the garden of God:</strong> who this picture points to differs from one view to another; see the issue “Eden and the Cherub: How Should We Read This Picture?” for details, and refer back to the issue “The King of Tyre and Satan: Who Is Chapter 28 About?”.</p>
@@12086@@
<p><strong>The lament begins by counting out, one by one, what he once had.</strong></p><p>Verse 12 is one great sentence of praise: “You were the seal of full measure, full of wisdom, and perfect in beauty.” This is the way of a lament: the more completely it paints what was lost, the more it makes people ache.</p><p>Then comes “Eden, the garden of God,” nine kinds of precious stones plus gold as his adornment. This list brings to mind the garden of Genesis. The text puts a king into the picture of a “garden of God.” Who this picture points to is understood differently by different people (see the issue “The King of Tyre and Satan: Who Is Chapter 28 About?” and the issue “Eden and the Cherub: How Should We Read This Picture?”, and also refer back to the issue “The King of Tyre and Satan: Who Is Chapter 28 About?”).</p><p>However it is read, one word cannot be left out: הִבָּרַאֲךָ, “you were created.” The end of verse 13 says all of this was “prepared in the day that you were created.” His wisdom was prepared, his beauty was prepared, and even those settings, or instruments, were prepared.</p><p>In verse 2 he said: I am a god. Verse 13 answers: you were created.</p><p>Landing it: count what you have in your hands. Not in order to be proud, but to find the label: “prepared.” Your talents, your family, your peace, none of them were pieced together by yourself. Today, find one thing, and say just one “thank you” to the Lord who prepared it.</p>

<p><strong>① How should אַתְּ־כְּרוּב be read?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>“You were the anointed cherub”</strong> (the Hebrew manuscripts, the CUV): compared to the cherub who guards what is holy. But אַתְּ (you) is feminine and כְּרוּב is masculine, so grammatically they do not quite agree</li><li><strong>“You were with the cherub”</strong> (the Septuagint and others): reading אַתְּ as אֵת (with), meaning he was placed beside the cherub</li><li><strong>Who the protagonist is</strong>: this king (in the manner of mythic symbolism), the first man, a fallen angel, or several images stacked together; views differ (see the issue “The King of Tyre and Satan: Who Is Chapter 28 About?” and the issue “Eden and the Cherub: How Should We Read This Picture?”)</li></ul><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: lay the views side by side, do not settle it; speak of pride first.</strong> Whoever stands behind this picture, what the text makes clearest is one road: someone entrusted with much, going from “perfect” to “unrighteousness was found.” This is what the issue “The King of Tyre and Satan: Who Is Chapter 28 About?” says: the root is “your heart is lifted up, and you have said, ‘I am a god.’ ”</p></blockquote>
@@12089@@
<p><strong>“You were perfect in your ways from the day that you were created, until unrighteousness was found in you.” The hinge of the whole passage is in this “until.”</strong></p><p>Verse 14 pushes the picture to its highest: cherub, anointed, covering; the holy mountain, stones of fire. This is one of the positions nearest to God in the Old Testament. The cherubim are the guardians who cover the ark (Exod 25:20).</p><p>Who the protagonist of this picture is, views differ, and we will come back to it in the issues section. Here, first grasp the turn that the text itself makes clearest: the “perfect” (תָּמִים) of verse 15, and “unrighteousness was found” (נִמְצָא עַוְלָתָה).</p><p>Look at that verb: “found” is Niphal, “was found out.” It did not come from outside; it was “in you.” Unrighteousness was not a break-in from outside, but slowly came to light in one who was perfect.</p><p>And one more word: the CUV has “afterward,” but the Hebrew is עַד, “until.” To be perfect does not mean to be perfect forever; perfect is not immune. The highest position is not the safe position.</p><p>Landing it: if today you are someone who is “doing quite well,” you need all the more to remind yourself: no starting point can guarantee the finish. Let the Lord examine our hearts at any time, not because we fear being found out, but because he sees it before we do.</p>
