I. A Closed Gate (44:1–3)
@@20242@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. In many people’s old family homes there is a front door that is not usually opened. Everyone comes and goes by the side door: the one who buys vegetables, the one who delivers letters, the children coming home from school. The front door is opened only at the New Year or for a big occasion. An outsider might ask, “Is this door broken?” It is not broken. Everyone in the house knows: this door is the one an important person walked through, so it is not opened casually.
@@20243@@
Today we read Ezekiel chapter 44, and it also begins with a gate. Chapter 43 was the return of the glory, filling the temple, and when we read it our hearts were warm. But the very first sentence of chapter 44 makes us stop. Verse 1 says: “Then he brought me back by the way of the outer gate of the sanctuary, which looks toward the east; and it was shut.” The prophet had just seen the glory come in through this gate, and now this gate is shut.
@@20244@@
Verse 2 gives the reason: Yahweh said to me, “This gate shall be shut. It shall not be opened, no man shall enter in by it; for Yahweh, the God of Israel, has entered in by it. Therefore it shall be shut.” Please notice that this reason is very unusual. Usually a door is closed to keep something outside from coming in. This gate is not shut to guard against anyone. It is shut because he walked through it. A gate that his feet have crossed is no longer a road for people to use as they please.
@@20245@@
It is a quiet picture: the glory went in, and the gate was shut. It does not lock people out. It keeps his presence well inside. The shutting here is a kind of protection, protecting his presence, and it has not shut anyone out of grace.
@@20246@@
Verse 3 goes on: “As for the prince, he shall sit in it as prince to eat bread before Yahweh. He shall enter by the way of the porch of the gate, and shall go out the same way.” This “prince” is a word that in the Hebrew means a leader, and the CUV renders it “king.” Even he can only sit in the porch of the gate, eating bread before Yahweh, entering from the porch and leaving by the porch. Power has to stop before the gate he walked through, and become a guest sitting down to a meal.
@@20247@@
Different traditions read this gate in different ways, and we will lay them out first: some see it as the holiness that guards his presence; some see it as a limit on the prince’s position; and some Christians see it as pointing to Christ, who is the one door and the one who has already completed the entering (John 10:9). These notes do not ask you to pick a side, and we do not judge brothers and sisters who see it differently. Only one thing is clear: the gate is shut because he walked through it; even the prince must eat bread before him.
@@20248@@
Does the gate being shut mean that God has turned people away?
@@20249@@
The text does not mean this. Verse 2 gives only one reason: because Yahweh has entered in by it. This says that the road he walked is no longer a shortcut for people. It does not say that people cannot draw near to him. Verse 3 even says that the prince may sit down in the porch and eat bread before Yahweh, which is a very close picture. So if you are afraid of being shut out, remember: a gate being shut and you being rejected are two different things. You can say a sentence to him: Lord, I am not asking to go around the road you walked; I only ask to sit before you. This is the way in.
@@20250@@
II. Set Your Heart on It, and Guard the Holy Things (44:4–9)
@@20251@@
In verse 4, the prophet is brought to the front of the temple again. “I looked, and behold, Yahweh’s glory filled Yahweh’s house; so I fell on my face.” This is already the second time he has fallen down since the glory came back. The prophet did not get used to it just because he had seen it in the last chapter. Being familiar is not the same as treating it lightly. Since the glory has returned, we must keep learning reverence over and over.
@@20252@@
In verse 5 he says: “Son of man, mark well, and see with your eyes, and hear with your ears all that I tell you concerning all the ordinances of Yahweh’s house, and all its laws; and mark well the entrance of the house, with every exit of the sanctuary.” The CUV has “set it on your heart.” Heart, eyes, ears: three actions, and the heart comes first. With only eyes and ears, this temple would be left as a heap of numbers, so he wants the prophet first to set his heart on it. He also specifically mentions the entrances and exits: the place where you come in and the place where you go out must both be watched. A worshiper cannot care only about the part inside the temple. How you walk in and how you leave are also part of worship.
@@20253@@
Then his tone changes. Verse 6 says: “You shall tell the rebellious, even the house of Israel, ‘The Lord Yahweh says: “You house of Israel, let that be enough of all your abominations!’” The words “enough” are spoken after being held back for a very long time. His patience is not bottomless, and it is not without feeling. He held back for a long time, and then he said: Enough.
@@20254@@
Then he points out two things. Verse 7 says: “in that you have brought in foreigners, uncircumcised in heart and uncircumcised in flesh, to be in my sanctuary, to profane it, even my house, when you offer my bread, the fat and the blood.” Verse 8 says: “You have not performed the duty of my holy things; but you have set performers of my duty in my sanctuary for yourselves.” Please notice where the weight falls: the ones rebuked are not the foreigners, but the people who led them in. The real problem is this: it was he who told you to keep it, and you said, “You watch it for me.”
@@20255@@
“Uncircumcised in heart and uncircumcised in flesh”: two phrases side by side, heart and body. He wants the whole person. Having only the outside in order while the heart has not turned to him is not enough; having a warm heart while the body is careless is not enough either. The ones who were brought in were people whose body and heart had both not turned to him, and they were put into the holy place because those responsible for guarding it were lazy. This verse is sometimes used to exclude people, so we must be careful: the weight of verse 9 is still on the duty of guarding the holy things; and in the New Testament, there are no more outsiders (Ephesians 2:19).
@@20256@@
Chapter 44 says foreigners may not enter the sanctuary. Is this excluding people? How should the church receive newcomers today?
@@20257@@
No. The weight is in verses 7 and 8: those rebuked are the Israelites who led people in but did not guard, and the standard in the text is whether “heart and flesh” have turned to him, not a person’s origin. Isaiah 56:6–7 also says that the foreigners who join themselves to Yahweh, he will bring to his holy mountain. The New Testament says even more: in Christ there are no outsiders. So the church’s door is open for everyone who turns to him. You can practice it this way: at the next gathering, take the first step to get to know someone you do not know, call him by name, do not ask about his background, and just say: “I’m glad you came.” Welcoming and guarding do not conflict.
@@20258@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Shut, Duty, Inheritance (44:2, 8, 28)
@@20259@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth stopping to look at. The first is “shut,” סָגוּר (sagur). Verses 1 and 2 say: “it was shut”; “This gate shall be shut.” This word does not mean “locked tight,” and it does not mean “rejected.” It means “closed,” like gently closing the front door at home. It is close to “protect.” Verse 2 says “shut” three times, emphasizing that this gate being shut is his decision; the place he walked through is to be kept well guarded.
@@20260@@
The second is “duty,” מִשְׁמֶרֶת (mishmeret). Verse 8 says: “You have not performed the duty of my holy things”; verse 14 says: “I will make them performers of the duty of the house”; verses 15 and 16 say: “who performed the duty of my sanctuary,” and “they shall keep my instruction.” The CUV renders this word “guard.” It comes from the verb “to keep,” and means to watch over, to hold on to, to take care of. It appears several times in this chapter, and it is the key to the chapter: whoever keeps it is the one who is seen. It is not only standing guard; it is a posture of “this was entrusted to me, and I will keep it with my own hands.” Handing the entrusted thing off to someone else is the problem in verse 8; still keeping it is the blessing in verse 15.
@@20261@@
The third is “inheritance,” נַחֲלָה (nakhalah). Verse 28 says: “They shall have an inheritance. I am their inheritance; and you shall give them no possession in Israel. I am their possession.” This word is a piece of land that is allotted, the share passed down to descendants. In Israel, each tribe received a piece of land, a mark of identity and security. But the priests had no land. He says: I am your piece. Put the three words together and you have the path of this chapter: a shut gate protects his presence, duty is a trust I keep with my own hands, and the final inheritance is himself. Today, ask first: what I am gripping tightly, is it the inheritance, or is it him?
@@20262@@
Chapter 44 says the priests’ clothes must not make the body sweat. Does this mean service takes no effort?
@@20263@@
No. The meaning of verse 18 is that we should not try to prove ourselves by struggling and overexerting. What he wants is service that is light, pure, and quietly standing, and he is not asking people to be lazy. The picture of the linen garment is wearing something light and still standing steady. You can examine yourself this way: is my service today done because I am loved, or because I am afraid of being looked down on? The first may tire you, but it will not leave you drenched in sweat; the second makes people stifled and bitter. If you find you are already exhausted, please stop for a while, tell a companion, and seek help if you need it. He also wants to carry the weight of service with you.
@@20264@@
IV. “Yet” and “Still,” and the Difference Between the Holy and the Common (44:10–31)
@@20265@@
Beginning at verse 10, he speaks about another group: the Levites. “But the Levites who went far from me, when Israel went astray, who went astray from me after their idols, they will bear their iniquity.” This sentence is heavy, and it is not watered down. Verse 12 goes on: “Because they ministered to them before their idols, and became a stumbling block of iniquity to the house of Israel. Therefore I have lifted up my hand against them… and they will bear their iniquity.” Verse 13: “They shall not come near to me, to execute the office of priest to me, nor to come near to any of my holy things.” The consequence is real, and their place has changed too: they lost the front row.
@@20266@@
But please look at the turn in verses 11 and 14. Verse 11 says: “Yet they shall be ministers in my sanctuary, having oversight at the gates of the house, and ministering in the house.” Verse 14 says: “Yet I will make them performers of the duty of the house, for all its service, and for all that will be done therein.” This “yet” is the heaviest turn in the whole passage. They no longer stand at the very front, but they are still in the temple: keeping the gates, slaughtering the offerings, standing before the people to serve them. The same verb, two directions: before, they ministered to idols; now, they minister to the people. The hands are still the same hands, the instinct to serve is still there, only the one served has changed. He did not say, “Nothing happened,” and he did not say, “You are finished forever.” He said: you will carry the consequences, and you will do the work.
@@20267@@
Verses 15 and 16 are about another group: the Levitical priests, the sons of Zadok. The reason is only one sentence: when the people of Israel went astray from me, they still kept my sanctuary (the CUV says “still guarded”; WEB: “who performed the duty of my sanctuary when the children of Israel went astray from me”). The heaviest word in this sentence is “still.” Everyone else had gone, and they were still there. No one applauded, no one saw, they just kept on keeping that duty. What he gives them is three verbs: come near, stand, minister. They may enter the sanctuary and come to his table. This position is not an honor of being lifted up; it is a kind of nearness. He does not choose people by talent or by birth; he chooses by “still.”
@@20268@@
How should we read this passage about the sons of Zadok and the Levites today? Different traditions see it differently. Some think it refers to an actual priestly system in the future; some think it is symbolic: those who stayed are able to come near, and those who left have consequences and also a place; and some read from the New Testament that all who trust in Christ are priests (1 Peter 2:9), and the distinction in the Old Testament foreshadows this identity. These notes set the views side by side and do not presuppose a system or a timetable. However you read it, one thing does not change: leaving has consequences, returning has a place, and that “still,” he sees.
@@20269@@
Verses 17 to 19 are about the priests’ clothing. To enter the inner court they wear linen, not wool, and nothing that makes them sweat. Then verse 19 says: “When they go out into the outer court, even into the outer court to the people, they shall put off their garments in which they minister, and lay them in the holy rooms. They shall put on other garments, that they not sanctify the people with their garments.” This sentence is very interesting. He does not want the priest to press the weight of the holy garments onto the people. Before God he is a priest; before people he is someone who walks up close.
@@20270@@
Verses 20 to 22 are about hair, wine, and marriage: “They shall not shave their heads, or allow their locks to grow long. They shall only cut off the hair of their heads.” “None of the priests shall drink wine when they enter into the inner court.” “They shall not take for their wives a widow, or her who is put away; but they shall take virgins of the offspring of the house of Israel, or a widow who is the widow of a priest.” Put together, these regulations describe a life that is not showy: not too much, not too little, standing before him with a clear mind. About the marriage regulation, please read carefully: this is a rule about setting apart the priestly office, not a judgment on widows or divorced women. They are cared for in the same way before God (Psalm 68:5).
@@20271@@
Then comes verse 23, the heart of this passage: “They shall teach my people the difference between the holy and the common, and cause them to discern between the unclean and the clean.” The priest’s first job is teaching. Why does this matter so much? Because people who have been in exile for many years can no longer tell the difference. The holy and the common get mixed together in a foreign land, and once you get used to it, you no longer feel any difference. The priest’s job is to give that feeling back. What follows is judging disputes, keeping the feasts and the Sabbaths, and being careful about the dead, and verse 25 even leaves a way out for close family. He knows that some grief cannot be avoided, and he does not want people cut off in their grief.
@@20272@@
Finally, verse 28 is the strangest sentence in the whole chapter: “They shall have an inheritance. I am their inheritance; and you shall give them no possession in Israel. I am their possession.” All the rules before it, garments, hair, wine, marriage, the dead, seem to be wrapped up in this one sentence. The priests have no land, no field, no piece of property to pass on to their sons. He says: you have none, but I have, and I am. He is not giving them a smaller version; he gave the biggest version: himself. Verses 29 and 30 say the offerings and the first fruits go to the priests, and this is a flow: the people supply, the priests serve. And there is one practical bottom line at the end: verse 31, the priests shall not eat anything that dies of itself or is torn. A high declaration and a daily bottom line are set side by side.
@@20273@@
People who have left God hear “they will bear their iniquity” and get very afraid. How should I read this passage?
@@20274@@
Please read two verses together: verse 10 says they will bear their iniquity, and verse 14 says “Yet,” I will make them performers of the duty of the house. He did not cancel the consequences, and he did not close the place. If you have ever left, do not read only verse 10; please read verse 14 too. The consequences are real, and perhaps you cannot go back to the position you once had, but your hands can still do the work that is to be done. You can say a sentence to him: Lord, I will carry what is mine, and please give me one thing to do today. Then go and do that one thing, however small. You do not have to wait until you are completely washed clean before you begin.
@@20275@@
The priests may not marry a widow or a divorced woman. I am that kind of person. Have I been demoted by God?
@@20276@@
No. This is a rule about setting apart the priestly office, not a judgment on you. Elsewhere he is “a father of the fatherless, and a defender of the widows” (Psalm 68:5), and he also commanded, “You shall not take advantage of any widow or fatherless child” (Exodus 22:22). You do not need to explain your story to anyone, and you do not need to feel put down because of this line. If someone uses this verse to speak to you, please remember that this verse was not written against you. If you are in the middle of a wound, please let a trustworthy pastor or companion walk with you, and seek counseling help if you need it.
@@20277@@
The first deviation is to preach “foreigners may not enter the sanctuary” (44:9) as exclusion, using it to push away newcomers or people of a different background. The text rebukes the Israelites who led people in but did not guard, and in Christ there are no more outsiders. We want newcomers to feel more welcome, and we want those who guard to be more faithful, not to make people feel they are not good enough.
@@20278@@
The second deviation is to preach the difference between the sons of Zadok and the Levites as a “spiritual class system,” saying some people are naturally higher; or, to those who have left, to preach only “they will bear their iniquity” and not the “yet.” The standard in the text is “still,” not talent or birth, and consequence and grace exist together. We want those who have left to dare to come back, and those who stayed to not become proud.
@@20279@@
The third deviation is to preach the linen garments and “not sweating” as meaning no need to try hard or give your heart, or, on the opposite side, to press the weight of the holy garments onto others. The text says not to prove yourself by struggling, and it is not encouraging laziness; taking off the holy garments is for walking up close to people. We want those who serve to be lighter and more real, not more tired or more lazy.
@@20280@@
The fourth deviation is to use the marriage regulation (44:22) or “do not go near a dead person” (44:25) to suggest that widows and divorced people are looked down on, or that the bereaved are unclean and should be avoided. The text is a regulation about setting the priests apart, and it leaves a way out for close family. We want them to be seen and accompanied, not hurt again.
@@20281@@
The fifth deviation is to use this chapter to draw up a timetable for the priestly system or the restoration of the new temple, to make one interpretation of the new temple a standard of faith, or to preach “the first fruits go to the priests” as a reason to demand from believers, to raise money for pastors or institutions, or to use “I am their inheritance” to persuade people to be content with poverty. The center the text gives us is that he himself is the biggest share, and the congregation should also support fairly. We want people to love the Lord more and to be more considerate of one another, not to be more anxious or more oppressed.
@@20282@@
I want to lead this chapter in my small group. How can I keep everyone from feeling we are discussing a system and rules?
@@20283@@
You can put three things first: the gate, “still,” and the inheritance. Start with a very everyday question: does your home have a door that is not usually opened? Then ask: is there something everyone else gave up on that you are still doing? Finally ask: what are you gripping the tightest in your hand? Not what is left over, but the portion lifted out first. With this portion, all the other shares have an order.</p>

<p><strong>① What are the Levites’ “twenty rooms”?</strong></p><ul><li>Twenty dwellings, for the Levites to live in</li><li>“Twenty” may be some kind of unit, such as towns or groups of rooms</li><li>Some ancient versions read it differently, and the meaning is uncertain</li></ul><p>The text says only “twenty (לְשָׁכֹת)” and does not explain the size. We do not need to work it out exactly.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: what we are to see is the order, not the numbers: the priests are near the sanctuary, the Levites guard the temple, and each has a place.</strong></p></blockquote>
@@20379@@
<p><strong>The ones nearest to God receive a place to live.</strong></p><p>What the priests receive is the piece of land next to the sanctuary. The text calls them “who come near to minister to Yahweh.” This “come near” (הַקְּרֵבִים) means ones who are near. The priest’s identity is not a title, but how close he is to God.</p><p>The Levites are beside them, and received twenty rooms. Compared with the fields the other tribes received, this is very small. But they do not have to farm, and they do not have to compete with others for land; their inheritance is serving in the temple.</p><p>In the Old Testament there has always been this arrangement for the priests and Levites: their blessing is not in how much they have on earth, but in having a place by God’s side. What was allotted to the priests was “a place for their houses and a holy place for the sanctuary,” the dwelling and the place of service together.</p><p>Nor does it say here that they may take one more portion. This order is steady: God’s people receive only what God gives them, and do not go out to expand for themselves.</p><p>Landing it: the most dangerous thing for those who serve God is not having too little, but wanting more. If you have a service in the church, remember that your inheritance is not the stage, but the place where you can come near to him.</p>

<p><strong>① Who is the “prince”?</strong></p><ul><li>It refers to the Davidic messianic king</li><li>It refers to a ruler in the new land who is not a descendant of David</li><li>It is an ideal picture of a leader, used to show the boundary of power</li></ul><p>The original uses נָשִׂיא (one lifted up), not the common מֶלֶךְ (king). The CUV renders both uniformly as “king.” This word is used of someone whose land has limits, who has responsibilities, and who must offer sacrifices, so its meaning differs from an ordinary king.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: we do not need to decide first who he is; first look at his limits: he has land, but he may not oppress; he has a position, but he must divide the land by tribes.</strong></p></blockquote>
@@20382@@
<p><strong>Even the prince’s land has a boundary drawn around it.</strong></p><p>The city land is beside the holy land and belongs to the whole house of Israel. The prince’s land is on both sides, stretching east and west. Everyone’s place has been measured.</p><p>The most important sentence in this passage is verse 8: “My princes shall no more oppress my people.” The words “no more” have a very long history behind them. Earlier Ezekiel said that the shepherds of Israel fed only themselves, and did not feed the flock. The prince has land so that he does not need to go and grab other people’s land.</p><p>Power with a boundary is safe power. A prince without a boundary will always reach out to the side. So God first draws his land, and then requires that he not oppress.</p><p>The text has one more sentence: “they shall give the land to the house of Israel according to their tribes.” The prince is not the final owner of the land; he is a channel in the middle. The land must finally return to the hands of the tribes.</p><p>Landing it: do you have power? Parents, bosses, pastors, and group leaders all do. The position God gives you also gives you a boundary. Keeping the boundary well is not limiting you; it is protecting those under you.</p>
