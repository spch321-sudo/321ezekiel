@@30@@
<p><strong>The explanation came a night late.</strong></p><p>In the morning they asked, “What are you doing?” The prophet’s action had stirred their curiosity. That question was the door opening.</p><p>God’s answer comes in two layers. The first: this burden concerns the prince in Jerusalem, and all the house of Israel who are with him. The second: “I am your sign. As I have done, so will it be done to them.” The prophet is a mirror, and his body is the written text of this sentence.</p><p>Look at the words in verse 10. “Prince” is נָשִׂיא, “one who is lifted up”; “burden” is מַשָּׂא, “a load,” and both come from the same root נשׂא, “to lift, to carry.” The one who is lifted up will have to carry the load himself. The root had already written the ending into the name.</p><p>Notice also that the hearers were exiles. They hoped Jerusalem was still standing, but this sign says that even the people in Jerusalem will be taken into captivity.</p><p>This explanation came only “in the morning” of the next day (verse 8). The day before, the prophet had carried out his baggage in daylight and dug through the wall to go out at night, and he had said not a word. For a whole night he was just a strange man, stared at and questioned. Only when “Yahweh’s word came to me” was the meaning spoken. Verse 9 says that those who asked “What are you doing?” were “the rebellious house.” They had watched all day, yet they were willing to listen only once someone had asked.</p><p>Bringing it home: when someone asks you, “Why do you live like this?”, your door has opened. Often a person does not need an answer first. He first needs something that makes him want to ask.</p>
@@31@@
<p><strong>“That is for later” is the safest sentence.</strong></p><p>The people in verse 22 said, “Every vision fails.” The people in this passage have changed their tune: the vision is true, but it is for a long time from now and has nothing to do with us today. The first is denial, the second is postponement. Postponement is harder to detect than denial.</p><p>When they talk about the prophet they say “he”: what he sees, what he says. It is like gossiping about someone who is not in the room. The word is moved far away, and the person is moved far away with it.</p><p>Yahweh’s answer says it once more: not one word will be put off any longer. It is the same word מָשַׁךְ, “to be prolonged, to be drawn out.” His word leaves no room for any sentence to go “far away.”</p><p>This passage is not giving us a timetable. It is a mirror, showing how we push back to later the word we should hear now.</p><p>The wording of verse 27 is very specific: “for many days to come” and “of times that are far off.” They are not saying “there is nothing.” They are moving the date to a place where no one can see it, so that today they can go on living as before. God’s reply in verse 28 begins with “Therefore,” and then comes a pair of matching sentences: “None of my words will be deferred any more,” and “the word which I speak will be performed.” One cancels “later,” the other cancels “far off.” He answers them word for word.</p><p>Bringing it home: which word that he is speaking to you today are you answering with “that is for later”? Forgiving someone, apologizing to someone, letting go of something. It is not for the future. It is for today.</p>
@@32@@
<p><strong>God himself cuts those knots.</strong></p><p>His response is one verb after another: tear off, set free, tear apart, rescue. The whole series is “I” will do it. The people who are bound do not have to untie themselves first.</p><p>He will “tear.” This is the word used for tearing clothes, the action of grief. He tears the pillows from their arms and tears the kerchiefs from their heads, not lightly, but to rescue the ones who are being hunted.</p><p>More beautiful still is שָׁלַח, “to let go.” This verb is used in Lev 14:7 for letting a living bird go. They hunted people with their charms the way one hunts birds; he will let these birds out, one by one.</p><p>Look once more at “my people.” He says that those who are hunted are his. The hunters think they are holding someone else’s lives in their hands, but he says: they are mine.</p><p>Verse 20 is very clear. The pillows are “with which you hunt the souls to make them fly,” and the prey is people’s lives. These women thought what they held was a skill, but God says, “I am against your pillows.” He first stands across from them, and only then goes to rescue the people they have trapped. The end of verse 21 is the familiar line again: “Then you will know that I am Yahweh.” Even those who set the nets will in the end come to know who he is.</p><p>Bringing it home: if you have ever been tied up in fear by a “spiritual person,” trapped by words like “if you don’t do this, disaster will come,” this passage is for you: he comes himself to cut the cords. You do not have to prove you have enough faith before you are set free.</p>
@@33@@
<p><strong>First a cry: turn back! Only then the heaviest word.</strong></p><p>Verse 6 is the turning point of the whole passage, and the gentlest place in the whole chapter. God has just said “I will answer him,” and in an instant his voice changes: come back, leave, turn your faces back. The Hebrew first says שׁוּבוּ (return), and then uses the Hiphil הָשִׁיבוּ (cause to turn), with your faces as the object. The heart must turn first, and then the face can be turned.</p><p>Verse 7 opens the door wider: even the strangers who live in Israel are included. Grace and responsibility are the same size. No one is excepted, and no one is shut out.</p><p>The “set my face” of verse 8 is, in the Hebrew, “I will put my face on that man.” God has not stopped looking at him. He faces him head on. The man who was taken over by idols becomes a “sign,” and those who come later point to him and say: do not be like him.</p><p>The man described in verse 7 does four things: he separates himself from God, takes false gods into his heart, puts a stumbling block before his face, and then goes to the prophet to inquire. The idol is not placed outside the temple but “taken into the heart.” With one hand he holds a false god, with the other he asks God, thinking he will offend neither side. God’s answer is: “I Yahweh will answer him by myself.” Notice “by myself.” He does not go through the prophet or through anyone else; he will face the man directly, face to face.</p><p>Bringing it home: the cry comes before the judgment. Remember this order. God never punishes first and then calls you back. He calls first, and keeps calling, until he has no choice but to speak the second sentence.</p>
@@34@@
<p><strong>The one who wants to hear lies and the one who speaks them carry the same guilt.</strong></p><p>The hardest line to read in this passage is in verse 9: “I, Yahweh, have deceived that prophet.” This does not say that God likes to deceive. In the same verse, the prophet is the one who is deceived (Pual), and God is the one who allows that prophet to be deceived (Piel), and verse 10 immediately adds the point: the iniquity of the prophet is the same as the iniquity of the one who seeks him.</p><p>It is a very honest picture. On one side are the people who want lies, on the other side the people willing to speak lies, and that is how the market forms. No one is only a victim, and no one is only a villain.</p><p>Verse 11 is the heart of the passage: the purpose is “that the house of Israel may no more go astray from me,” and “that they may be my people, and I may be their God.” At the end of the judgment lies this old line of the covenant.</p><p>Verse 9 says that if the prophet is deceived and speaks a word, God “will stretch out my hand on him, and will destroy him from among my people Israel.” Verse 10 says: “They will bear their iniquity.” Each side carries its own, and neither can say it was the other’s fault. Verse 11 makes the purpose plain: “that the house of Israel may no more go astray from me, neither defile themselves any more with all their transgressions.” At the end of all these hard words, on one side is leaving, on the other is returning, and God wants only one thing: that his people no longer go astray.</p><p>Bringing it home: have you ever picked someone who would only tell you what you wanted to hear? This verse is not asking you to inspect other people. It asks you to look at yourself: from whose mouth am I hoping today to get which answer?</p>
@@35@@
<p><strong>Your beauty was not yours; God put it on you.</strong></p><p>Verse 6 is “live,” verse 8 is “covered,” and verses 9 to 14 are “abundance.” God put the best on her, piece by piece: embroidered clothing, fine linen, silk, gold and silver, rings, a crown. Even her food was the best: fine flour, honey, oil. She went from a baby to a queen.</p><p>Something here deserves notice: the same materials also appear in the tabernacle. What God gave her carried his own fragrance. She became a person set apart, and this is what grace looks like.</p><p>Verse 14 is the key to the whole passage: “for it was perfect, through my majesty which I had put on you.” Her fame went out among all the nations, and not a bit of it was her own. Our own lives often go like this: what grace gave, we think is ours.</p><p>The order in verse 9 is: first washing, then anointing with oil. “Then washed I you with water. Yes, I thoroughly washed away your blood from you” picks up the earlier picture of the baby cast out in the field, covered in blood. Cleansing first, then adorning. Verses 10 to 12 put things on her one by one: clothing, sandals, a girdle, a covering, bracelets, a chain, a ring in the nose, earrings, a beautiful crown, from foot to head, with nothing left out. For this child who was picked up, even the sandals she walked in were put on her by God’s own hand.</p><p>Bringing it home: think of the part of your life that is praised most. Then ask yourself: is this mine, or was it “put on” me by him? The answer decides whether you give thanks or grow proud.</p>
@@36@@
<p><strong>She took God’s gifts and gave them to others.</strong></p><p>The turn is in the first verb of verse 15: וַתִּבְטְחִי, “you trusted.” Trusted in what? “In your beauty.” When her fame went out, she saw her own beauty, and put her heart on the beauty instead of on the one who gave it to her.</p><p>Then comes a series of “you took”: you took garments and made high places; you took gold and silver and made idols; you took oil, incense, flour, and honey and set them before other gods. The Hebrew repeats וַתִּקְחִי, “you took,” one action after another. Every one of these things was given by God first.</p><p>This is the most painful place in the parable. It is not that she stole things from somewhere. It is that grace she herself had received was handed over to someone else. God’s grief is that of one who gave everything and was treated as a supplier.</p><p>Verse 16 says she “took some of your garments, and made for yourselves high places decked with various colors,” and verse 18 says “You took your embroidered garments, covered them,” and the very same embroidered garments were the ones God put on her in verse 10. Verse 17 puts it even more directly: “your beautiful jewels of my gold and of my silver, which I had given you,” and she made images of men from them. The food in verse 19 is the same: “My bread also which I gave you, fine flour, oil, and honey, with which I fed you,” she set before them “for a pleasant aroma.” What God gave to nourish her, she turned around and offered to others.</p><p>Bringing it home: the best gifts, relationships, and achievements in your hand right now, whom are they glorifying? Sometimes we use what God gave us, and what we build is our own name.</p>
@@37@@
<p><strong>God says: even they will return to their former place.</strong></p><p>This passage is a bridge from judgment to restoration. God says first: I will bring them back. Notice that the first ones named are not Jerusalem but Sodom and Samaria. She is the last. It is the gentlest reminder for someone used to standing at the front.</p><p>In the middle is a verb that appears again and again: נָשָׂא, “to bear.” “Bear your own shame” is said several times. God does not say the shame does not count. He says: you must see it, and then there is a way forward. This is not punishment. It is helping her stand upright.</p><p>The word קַדְמָתָן in verse 55 means “their former estate.” Each one returns to the place at the beginning: Sodom, Samaria, Jerusalem. No one is higher than another, and no one is left behind.</p><p>Verse 56 has a very fine observation: “your sister Sodom was not mentioned by your mouth in the day of your pride, before your wickedness was uncovered.” At that time she did not even want to mention Sodom, because she felt superior. By verse 57, the daughters of Syria and the daughters of the Philistines, whom she had once looked down on, despise her all around; she went from being looked up to, to being looked down on by those around her. Verse 58 sums up the passage: “You have borne your lewdness and your abominations.”</p><p>Bringing it home: are you waiting for someone to apologize first before you are willing to go back? The order God speaks of is: I will first bring them back, and then you together. Grace is not handed down from the high to the low. We are all brought back together to the beginning.</p>
@@38@@
<p><strong>She broke the covenant, but God says: I will remember, and I will establish an everlasting covenant.</strong></p><p>Verse 59 is a fair statement: “I will also deal with you as you have done.” But the first word of verse 60 is “Nevertheless,” and that word is the turning point of the whole chapter: “Nevertheless I will remember my covenant with you in the days of your youth.”</p><p>Notice who remembers: it is “I.” She forgot, but he did not. At the beginning of the chapter God said, “I passed by you”; at the end of this chapter God says, “I will remember.” He is the God who always remembers.</p><p>Then comes the “everlasting covenant,” בְּרִית עוֹלָם. It is not according to the old one, and it does not depend on her keeping it well; God himself establishes it. Verse 61 adds one more detail: Samaria and Sodom will be given to her as daughters. She had always wanted to go forward, or go back, and the result is that she is brought into a larger family.</p><p>Verse 59 says she “despised the oath in breaking the covenant.” The covenant in this parable is the one God made when she was young, naked and bare. The words “days of your youth” in verse 60 remind us that what he remembers is not her glory later on, but the time when she was most helpless. Verse 61 also has a line that seems contradictory: God will give her sisters to her as daughters, “but not by your covenant.” This new arrangement is not on the terms of the old covenant but comes from God’s free grace, and it makes her “ashamed,” yet she is not rejected.</p><p>Bringing it home: today you may feel you have ruined everything. This verse says: the weight of the covenant is not on your side, it is on his. He remembers.</p>
@@39@@
<p><strong>The prophet’s body is the first sermon.</strong></p><p>God told him to sigh, and to sigh bent over. The Hebrew is בְּשִׁבְרוֹן מָתְנַיִם, “with the breaking of the loins.” It is not a light sigh. It is the whole person folding over, right before their eyes.</p><p>Someone asks: why do you sigh? His answer is a single word: שְׁמוּעָה, “the news,” a report. The bad news has not yet arrived, but the prophet has already collapsed on their behalf. Every heart will melt, all hands will be feeble, every spirit will faint, and all knees will be weak as water. These verbs are lined up like watching a person slowly give way before your eyes.</p><p>Notice that the prophet does not stand high on a judgment seat. He first uses his body to feel the pain, on behalf of people who do not yet feel it.</p><p>This is the hardest lesson for God’s shepherds: not only to pass on the word, but to be broken together with the word.</p><p>Two things in verse 6 are worth seeing: “before their eyes,” and “with bitterness.” This was an act meant to be seen, not private sadness. Verse 7 writes in advance the question they are sure to ask: “Why do you sigh?” Before the prophet opens his mouth, the answer is already waiting. There is not a word of blame in his answer, only a picture: every heart will melt, hands will be feeble, spirits will faint, knees will be weak as water. The last line is “Behold, it comes, and it shall be done.” This states a fact. It is not a threat.</p><p>Bringing it home: when you see a danger others have not yet seen, it is easiest to turn it into blame. The order God gives is: first sigh, then speak. If you are worried about a person or a family, first bend over for them once, and then go and speak.</p>
@@40@@
<p><strong>She saw, but did not stop.</strong></p><p>These three verses are short, but they are the watershed of the chapter. The younger sister “saw this,” the elder sister’s road and the elder sister’s end, she saw it all. Normally seeing makes a person stop. But the text goes on: “yet she lusted still more.”</p><p>Knowing and turning back are two different things. Jerusalem did not lack information; she lacked a turning. This is a common trap in the spiritual life: we think that once we understand, we will change. But the verb the Hebrew chooses here is a Hiphil: she made herself go deeper.</p><p>Verse 13 has a short sentence: “I saw that she was defiled.” The one who saw was not only the younger sister, but also God. He had long seen it and did not walk away.</p><p>“They both went the same way.” Two kingdoms, one direction. The only difference is who arrives first.</p><p>Look again at verse 12: what she lusted after were “governors and rulers, clothed most gorgeously, horsemen riding on horses.” What drew her was the shine of pomp and power. What God says here is first of all heartache: his tent was meant to be with her, but her eyes passed over him and looked toward the cavalry far away.</p><p>Bringing it home: has someone else’s ending really stopped you in your heart? Or have you already told yourself: “I am not like them”?</p>
@@41@@
<p><strong>What she remembered was a time she should not have longed for.</strong></p><p>The verb “remember” in the Bible is usually a good one. God remembers his covenant; God remembers Noah. But here it is Oholibah who “remembers” her days in Egypt.</p><p>The same word, pointing in the opposite direction. What she remembered was not the rescue of the Exodus, but the scars Egypt left behind. This parable is a heavy one: when people look back at history, they can see grace, or they can see only old thrills; which part you choose to remember decides where you go.</p><p>Verse 21 says she “called to memory” the lewdness of her youth. This verb carries the flavor of “going to look for it.” She was not simply trapped by memory; she went back to find it herself.</p><p>The picture of this passage is not for us to examine the details, but to see a cycle: every new alliance replays an old wound.</p><p>In verse 20, about the vigor of those lovers, God does not admire it. He uses only a simile, “like donkeys and like horses,” to lay bare the coarseness of her infatuation: what she lusted after was strength and show, and what she got in exchange was emptiness. God says these things not to describe, but because his heart is hurt: his people took the days of their youth in Egypt as something worth savoring.</p><p>Bringing it home: the “old days” you keep going back to, do they bring you closer to God, or farther away? Whatever you remember is where you will be taken.</p>
@@42@@
<p><strong>At the end of the road is a cup.</strong></p><p>Verse 28 has an irony: God delivers her “into the hand of them whom you hate.” These are the very people she loved earlier and then grew tired of. She left them, and now she is to return into their hands.</p><p>People caught up in infatuation seldom think about the ending: when they leave they think it is over, but the other end is still waiting for them.</p><p>Verse 29 says they will “take away all your labor.” This is a very concrete loss: not only dignity, but everything gathered through a lifetime of toil. What she thought getting close to a strong nation would protect was taken away together.</p><p>Then verse 31 is the key to this passage: “You have walked in the way of your sister; therefore I will give her cup into your hand.” First the “way,” then the “cup.” The cup is the end of the road. The way and the cup are the two ends of one thing.</p><p>Verse 30 says: “because you have played the prostitute after the nations, and because you are polluted with their idols.” Notice that what polluted her was the idols, not only people. She wanted the nations’ backing, but what stuck to her was the nations’ gods. God’s judgment begins by measuring what she truly leaned on.</p><p>Bringing it home: there is no road that has only a road and no end. At the end of the direction you are walking now, what is there?</p>
@@43@@
<p><strong>God’s incense and God’s oil were set on someone else’s table.</strong></p><p>The picture of this passage is two sisters preparing a feast: washing, painting the eyes, putting on ornaments, setting the table. The details are exquisite, all to welcome those “who come from far away.”</p><p>The line that cuts deepest is in verse 41: “whereupon you set my incense and my oil.” That was the incense and oil belonging only to God in the tent of meeting. What God gave for worship was taken to decorate another relationship.</p><p>This is often the real problem: it is not that we have nothing, but that what God gave is taken to serve other lovers. Gifts, time, talents, peace: to whom were these things originally offered?</p><p>“Righteous men” appear in verse 45. They will judge as the law requires. The righteousness here is a legal standing: doing what ought to be done. Do not take it as an approval of these men’s character.</p><p>Verse 42 adds one more sound: “The voice of a multitude being at ease was with her.” The feast is noisy, but on the table are God’s things, and beneath the merriment is a God pushed out the door. Verse 43 says “Now they will play the prostitute with her”: she is already old, and is still taken casually by anyone, and in the end all that noise leaves only exhaustion.</p><p>Bringing it home: what do you hold that was originally God’s “incense” and “oil” for you, and on which table is it set now?</p>
@@44@@
<p><strong>From far away, he wrote down a day.</strong></p><p>Ezekiel was in the land of exile, far from Jerusalem. He could not see the city, and no one gave him news. But God said, “write the name of the day,” because on this very day the king of Babylon drew close to Jerusalem.</p><p>This is the first quiet but weighty fact: God’s word arrives before the news. Later, one who escapes will come with the report (24:26), and then people will know whether the day the prophet wrote down was right. That day became the proof that the words from the prophet’s mouth came from God.</p><p>Listen also for the echo: the word for “this same day” is the word for “bone.” We have not yet seen the cauldron, and the bones are already in the text.</p><p>Verse 1 gives the date very precisely: “in the ninth year, in the tenth month, in the tenth day of the month.” What God wants is not a vague impression, but a date that can be checked later. When the prophet put pen to paper in the land of exile to write down this day, the king of Babylon was closing in on Jerusalem, and the people in the city were still living their ordinary lives. The same day, in two places: on one side calm, on the other a siege that had already begun.</p><p>Bringing it home: in the places you cannot see, God is not absent. He knows that day, and he remembers that day. There are things we cannot understand at the moment, and we can only “write them down” first, and wait for God’s time to give them meaning.</p>
@@45@@
<p><strong>This parable was originally someone else’s words.</strong></p><p>In chapter 11, some people said: this city is the cauldron and we are the meat, meaning, we are inside the walls, we are safe. God now takes that saying and says it again.</p><p>The saying stays the same: the city is the cauldron, the people are the meat. But the ending is different. The meat is not protected, it is cooked. The cauldron is the wall, true, but the wall has become the vessel in which they are cooked.</p><p>Notice the details in verses 4 and 5: “good,” “choice,” “the choice of the flock.” What is chosen to go into the cauldron is not spoiled meat but the finest. This line is sharp: people think their advantages will keep them from judgment, and in this parable the advantage only puts you nearer the middle of the pot.</p><p>Verse 3 says “pour water into it,” and verse 5 says “a pile of wood for the bones under the cauldron.” The parable is very homely: stove, water, wood, meat, things in every kitchen. God uses the most everyday picture to say the least everyday thing: that familiar cauldron is today not for cooking dinner, but a vessel of judgment. This parable was told to the rebellious house. The meaning of the cauldron is not hard to understand; what is hard is admitting that the cauldron is talking about yourself.</p><p>Bringing it home: it is very easy to take “I am inside” as a guarantee: I am in the church, I am in this family, I am in this circle. But belonging to a place is not the same as standing right before God.</p>
@@46@@
<p><strong>The blood people thought no one knew about was placed on a bare rock.</strong></p><p>This passage speaks first of the cauldron, then of blood. The cauldron is one “whose rust is in it”: the rust grows on the body of the cauldron. It is not an impurity that happened to fall in; it is the condition of the vessel itself.</p><p>Then comes the blood. The law says that when blood is shed it should be covered with dust (the principle of Lev 17:13). But here the blood is put “on the bare rock.” Smooth, without a crack, without dust, no one can hide it. God says: this is from me, I will not let it be covered.</p><p>The meaning is: some blood, people think, once buried is gone. God says, no. The first blood in Genesis cried out from the ground (Gen 4:10).</p><p>For the one who did the harm this is a heavy word, but for the one who was hurt it is a comfort: your pain has not been quietly covered over; God has seen it.</p><p>Verse 6 has one more line: “Take out of it piece after piece. No lot is fallen on it.” Taken out piece by piece, no drawing of lots, no picking and choosing, leaving no gap to escape by luck. This thoroughness shows that God sees each person, and does not deal with them in one lump.</p><p>Bringing it home: what are the things we work so hard to cover with dust? God places blood on the rock, not to shame people, but because he does not allow the wounded to be buried in silence.</p>
@@47@@
<p><strong>First cook the meat, then burn the cauldron.</strong></p><p>The second round of fire is not like the first. The first round was cooking the meat, cooking it until it fell apart and charred. In the second round, the meat is poured out, and the empty cauldron sits on the coals until the bronze glows red. The aim is not food but the cauldron itself: to melt the filthiness and burn off the rust.</p><p>This is the picture in the judgment with the most “shape of hope”: the purpose of the fire is to cleanse. But verse 12 goes on to say: the rust is so deep that even fire cannot remove it.</p><p>The cauldron is very tired. “Weary with toil,” it has used up all its strength, and the rust is still there. This does not mean God’s fire is not hot enough; it means the rust and the bronze have grown into one.</p><p>The action of verse 11 is “set it empty on its coals.” Emptying is the quietest step of the whole process: the meat has been taken out, and only an empty cauldron is left, waiting on the coals to be heated red. In verse 10 the fire is still cooking the meat; in verse 11 the fire is already turned on the cauldron. The two layers of fire have two objects. What God deals with is first what people have done, and then the root that grew out of this vessel.</p><p>Bringing it home: some habits and some roots can no longer be handled by “adjusting a bit.” God’s fire is not too weak; some things need a brand-new vessel, not a fiercer fire. The answer to that will not come until chapter 36.</p>
@@48@@
<p><strong>One verse holds his whole night.</strong></p><p>“So I spoke to the people in the morning; and at evening my wife died.” The text says only this. No name of an illness, no account of the process, nothing of what he said. One verb, “died,” and it was over.</p><p>Then: “So I did in the morning as I was commanded.” This is the heaviest line in the whole book. He obeyed, and the text does not say how he got through it.</p><p>This silence is not coldness. It is so that we do not look at this night as a story. And we do not need to fill in details for him.</p><p>Then the people come and ask: “Won’t you tell us what these things are to us, that you do so?” They saw a man who had lost his wife and did not weep, and found it strange. People are always like this: the first question is “what does this have to do with me?”</p><p>God had already said in verse 16 that he would take away “the desire of your eyes with a stroke,” and had commanded him: “you shall neither mourn nor weep, neither shall your tears run down.” Verse 22 says the people will also do as he has done. His quietness became a sign, not because he felt no pain, but because God wanted his silence to speak on behalf of the people.</p><p>Bringing it home: when we see someone else’s pain, what is our first thought? Is it “what does this mean for me,” or do we first quietly keep them company? These two questions do not necessarily exclude each other, but there is an order.</p>
@@49@@
<p><strong>They did not lift a finger, only said “Aha.”</strong></p><p>At the end of chapter 24, the prophet’s mouth was shut, waiting for the one who escapes to bring the report. The next few chapters are words to the nations, placed in this silent gap.</p><p>The first one named is Ammon. The text says God told the prophet to “set your face” toward them. The action is concrete: the prophet turns his face to face a group of people who are not listening.</p><p>The charge is not attack but reaction. The sanctuary was profaned, and they said “Aha!”; the land was made desolate, and they said “Aha!”; people were taken into captivity, and they still said “Aha!” Three losses, one cry of satisfaction.</p><p>God heard it. This is worth remembering: he hears every word people say, especially the cry that comes from the side at the moment when someone else is in the most pain.</p><p>Verse 2 says “set your face toward the children of Ammon, and prophesy against them,” and verse 3 says “Hear the word of the Lord Yahweh!” The prophet was sent to speak to them directly. The Ammonites had not invited him, yet before the announcement God still told them to listen. Even to those cheering on the sidelines, God first gives a chance to hear. This makes the cry of “Aha” weigh all the more.</p><p>Bringing it home: when you see someone else fall, what is the first sound that rises in your heart?</p>

<p><strong>A city that in the end became a pasture for camels.</strong></p><p>God’s response is a reversal. They laughed over someone else’s ruins, and God says: all right, your land will be taken by others.</p><p>The picture is plain: people from the wilderness camp in your land, eat your fruit, drink your milk. What was planted with the hardest toil became someone else’s food and drink.</p><p>Rabbah was the capital of Ammon, once a place full of the noise of people, and now a pasture for camels, a place where flocks lie down. It was not burned to charcoal; it was quietly “replaced.”</p><p>This kind of judgment has a particular flavor: not loud and dramatic, but slowly forgotten. The city is still on the ground; it is just no longer a city.</p><p>The “children of the east” in verse 4 have no name, and no army is described; they just come and “set their encampments.” To set up camp is the beginning of living there, not passing through. They “eat your fruit and drink your milk,” and every action is so ordinary, as if the life that originally belonged to Ammon is being taken over, stroke by stroke. The end of verse 5 is still the same line: “Then you will know that I am Yahweh.” Even in loss, what God finally wants to say is that people would know him.</p><p>Bringing it home: what we build for ourselves, if it has no name of God on it, may in the end leave not even ruins, and become only someone else’s pasture.</p>
@@51@@
<p><strong>Their whole bodies joined in the celebration.</strong></p><p>Hands clapped, feet stamped, hearts rejoiced. The text sets these actions out one after another, as if in slow motion.</p><p>The heaviest phrase is “with all the contempt of your soul.” The Hebrew word שָׁאט means scorn, looking down on. This is the root: they did not merely see someone fall, they looked down on the fallen, and even looked down on the God who was with that people.</p><p>God says: I will stretch out my hand against you. The judgment here is from hand to hand: your clapping hands, my hand.</p><p>Please notice one distinction. If you have been deeply hurt by someone, and when that person fell you felt a sigh of relief in your heart, that is not what this verse is rebuking. What it rebukes is the celebration with clapping and stamping, done with contempt.</p><p>Verse 7 uses three verbs in a row: “stretched out my hand,” “cut you off,” “destroy you.” Each one is God acting himself. The people who clapped and stamped thought it was only a passing mood, but God treats it as something that must be answered for. The ending is still that one line: “Then you will know that I am Yahweh.”</p><p>Bringing it home: contempt is a very quiet pride. It does not need to be spoken; it only needs to place others below yourself in your heart. Let man be man, and let God be God: this also means not sitting on the judgment seat watching someone fall.</p>
@@52@@
<p><strong>The first to be afraid were those who had done business with her.</strong></p><p>The sound of Tyre’s fall reached the islands. The islands were her customers, her docks, her backing. The picture in verse 16 is concrete: the princes of the sea come down from their thrones, lay aside their robes, strip off their embroidered garments, clothe themselves with trembling, sit on the ground, and tremble every moment.</p><p>Notice the Hebrew of the change of clothes. What they lay aside is מְעִילֵיהֶם, the royal robes; what they put on is חֲרָדוֹת, “trembling.” It is not that they chose sackcloth; fear became their new uniform.</p><p>Were they really weeping for Tyre? The lament in the next passage will give the answer. These few lines before us say only this: when something you thought could never fall falls, the ground under your own feet shakes too.</p><p>Verse 15 says “when the wounded groan.” Behind those trembling kings there are people who are really hurt. God does not pass over this layer; he first speaks of the wound, and only then of the islands’ shaking. Behind judgment there are people groaning, and this must not be read as cold news.</p><p>Bringing it home: some sadness is in name for someone else, but actually touches our own fear. If you have a kind of unease you cannot explain, there is no need to rush to suppress it. First ask yourself: what is the pillar my heart is leaning on?</p>
@@53@@
<p><strong>A lament, sung for those who are still alive.</strong></p><p>A lament is usually sung at a funeral. But God told the prophet to sing it now, to a city that was the busiest and richest, whose fleets were still leaving harbor. This is the most discordant picture in the chapter: the port was still busy, and the song was already sung.</p><p>The song begins with Tyre’s own words: “I am perfect in beauty.” The punctuation of the Chinese Union Version makes one think God is praising her, but the Hebrew reads צוֹר אַתְּ אָמַרְתְּ, “Tyre, you are the one who said.” That was the score she gave herself.</p><p>The next verse adds a small detail: “Your builders have perfected your beauty.” Her builders were carpenters and shipbuilders, people. Her beauty, from beginning to end, was made by human hands. She loved looking at her reflection, but forgot that the reflection was given by the water.</p><p>The command of verse 2 is very clear: “take up a lamentation over Tyre.” The lament is a task God gave the prophet, not the cold eye of an onlooker. The prophet is to sing it, to put the ending ahead of time into the ears of this city, so that those who still have a chance to listen may hear first. Verse 3 says she “dwells at the entry of the sea,” standing between water and land, everything passing through her, and so she thought everything belonged to her.</p><p>Bringing it home: we all have one or two sentences that begin “I am perfectly…,” perhaps “I am so capable,” “I am so steady,” “I understand my children best.” Do not rush to argue with it. Just ask: who gave me this sentence?</p>
