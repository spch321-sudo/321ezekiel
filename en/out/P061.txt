<p><strong>① What is “the year of liberty”?</strong></p><ul><li>It refers to the Jubilee (the same word “liberty” as in Leviticus 25:10)</li><li>It refers to the Sabbath year every seven years</li></ul><p>The text only says “the year of liberty” and does not say which one. Either way, the main point is the same: the inheritance the prince gives to a servant is not forever; when the time comes, it must be returned.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: most commentators read it as the Jubilee. The point is: land given away is temporary, and is not transferred from the people’s hands to the royal house.</strong></p></blockquote>
@@20828@@
<p><strong>The king may give, but he may not take.</strong></p><p>These verses are a land law, yet they are a very old warning. In Israel’s history, too many kings took other people’s land for themselves. Ahab and Naboth’s vineyard is the most famous example.</p><p>Verses 16 and 17 first say what the king may do: he may give his own inheritance to his sons, and he may give it to a servant. What is given to a son belongs to him forever; what is given to a servant must be returned in the “year of liberty.”</p><p>Verse 18 is the main point: “the prince shall not take of the people’s inheritance, to thrust them out of their possession.” The word “thrust out” (לְהוֹנֹתָם) has the same root as “oppress” in 45:8. The king may use only his own land.</p><p>Why so detailed? The text gives its own reason: “that my people not each be scattered from his possession.” What God cares about is that everyone stays on the land he has received. This is not a policy; it is a kind of love: God does not want his people pushed away.</p><p>The land is given by God; when someone claims this land for himself, it does not hurt only one person; it hurts the roots of the whole nation.</p><p>Application: think about it. In a relationship or a group, have you used your position to crowd out someone else’s space? God says: you may give what is your own; you may not take what is theirs.</p>

<p><strong>① What does “sanctify the people” mean?</strong></p><ul><li>When holy things touch the people, the people are affected by them (compare 44:19)</li><li>If holy things are taken out, they will be contaminated by ordinary people and things</li></ul><p>This verb is in the Piel stem (to make holy), and here the sense is closer to “to make people affected by the holy things.” This verse only states the concern and does not explain the mechanism.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: read it as: the priests must handle holy things in the inner court, so that the holy things are not carried out among the people, who would then bear a holiness they cannot bear.</strong></p></blockquote>
@@20831@@
<p><strong>God has thought even of the priests’ kitchen.</strong></p><p>This section suddenly grows quiet. The prophet is brought to an unremarkable place: the priests’ rooms, at the back to the west, where things are boiled and baked.</p><p>Why should there be such a place? The text gives the reason: “that they not bring them out into the outer court, to sanctify the people.” Holy things must not be carried casually among the people. It is the same concern as the rules for the priests’ changing of garments in chapter 44.</p><p>Reading it today, it may feel like a very ancient rule. But look closely, and its starting point is to protect people: God’s holiness is real, and when people draw near they need a way to do so. The priests first handle things in the inner court, then bring the result to the people. It is a very careful arrangement.</p><p>Look from another angle: the priests boil the offerings and bake the meal offering in a hidden place where no one sees. This is the backstage of service, with no applause. God has drawn this backstage into the blueprint as well.</p><p>Application: if your service is backstage, in the kitchen, in the equipment room, in a place where no one sees, this passage was written for you. God drew this place, and he sees you.</p>

<p><strong>God has a place for the meat on the people’s table, too.</strong></p><p>This chapter begins at the east gate and ends in a corner of the court. The prophet is led out, passes through the outer court, and sees four corners, each with a small court, forty cubits long and thirty wide. Inside are boiling places.</p><p>There is one difference to notice. The holy rooms in the last section are where the priests boil the sin offering; this section is where “the ministers of the house” boil “the sacrifice of the people,” that is, the people’s own peace offerings. Peace offerings are meant to be eaten: the one who offers enjoys them together with God and with the family.</p><p>In the details of the building we see one thing: God’s house is not only a place that inspires awe; it also has a place for cooking. Worship and the table are joined together here.</p><p>The prophet is led along, through the gate and through the court, and the last picture of the chapter is a row of stoves boiling meat. This is the last stop of the new temple: not the throne, but the kitchen.</p><p>Application: can your family table also become an extension of worship? One meal, a table of people, one word of thanks. Such days God prepared long ago.</p>

I. Shut for Six Days, Opened for One (46:1–8)
@@21107@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. On your key ring, is there a key you do not know what it opens? I have one at home, small and copper-colored, hanging at the very back. Every time I change key rings, I want to throw it away, and I don’t dare, afraid that one day I will suddenly need it. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 46, and the prophet is also led to a door. This door is very special: one day a week, it is open. For the other six days, it is shut, and if you walked past outside, you would think it was broken, or that no one was inside.
@@21108@@
Verse 1 says: “The gate of the inner court that looks toward the east shall be shut the six working days; but on the Sabbath day it shall be opened, and on the day of the new moon it shall be opened.” This is not saying that God is sometimes there and sometimes not. It is saying that people need a day. “The six working days”: you and I know them very well. Meetings, rushing to finish, pick-ups and drop-offs, paying bills, answering messages. The work is never done, and the days are never long enough. In six days like these the door is shut; it is not that God has locked it, it is that we were so busy we forgot where the door is. When the Sabbath comes, the door is opened. Please notice that this “opened” is “is opened.” The door is not pushed open by your strength; the way of worship is opened by him. All you have to do is walk over.
@@21109@@
Then there is a picture. The king comes. He does not walk to the very front; he comes in through the porch and stands beside the gate post, the priests prepare his burnt offering and his peace offerings, he worships at the threshold, and then he goes out. The king goes only as far as the threshold. The threshold is the outermost point of the whole gate, and when the king reaches it, he bows his head. Verse 3 says that the people of the land shall worship at the door of that gate before Yahweh. The king is at the threshold, the people are at the door; the positions are different, but the action is the same: bowing down. Before him, no one stands higher than anyone else.
@@21110@@
Verses 4 to 7 are a long string of numbers: six lambs, a ram, and at the new moon a young bull as well; the meal offering is an ephah of fine flour, with a hin of oil. There are many numbers, and it is easy to grow weary. But inside them are two very human words. One is “without defect,” which stands before every offering. God wants what is whole, not what is left over. The other is “according to his ability,” which means according to what his hand is able to give. The meal offering for the bull and the ram has a fixed amount, but for the lambs it does not; a blank is left, so that people may give by what they have in hand. Quality must not be careless, and quantity is not forced. He is not asking you to give more; he is asking you to give your best honestly.
@@21111@@
There was a brother who, every month after receiving his pay, did one thing first: he put an envelope on the dining table. There was not much money in the envelope, but every bill was ironed flat. His wife asked him why he ironed them, and he said: I don’t know, I just feel that what is given to him should not be wrinkled. This is not superstition; it is a person’s plainest understanding of “without defect.” Verse 8 goes back to the king: he comes in by the way of the porch, and he goes out by the same way. The same road, in and out. The king has no private back door of his own.
@@21112@@
The numbers of offerings in verses 4 to 7 are many. Can the church today take them as the standard for giving?
@@21113@@
No. These numbers are rules within the vision of the new temple; they are not a proportion for the church to collect from believers, and “without defect” should not be used to measure whether anyone has given enough, much less to make poor people feel unworthy. What this passage wants us to see is two things: God wants an honest best, not leftovers; and God also recognizes that each person’s hand holds something different, “according to his ability.” You can ask yourself: is the time and energy I give to God the best my hand can give? There is no need to give more for other people’s eyes, and no need to be ashamed because you gave less.
@@21114@@
II. In Among the People, Out Among the People (46:9–12)
@@21115@@
Verse 9 has a strange rule: at the feasts, those who come in by the north gate to worship shall go out by the south gate, and those who come in by the south gate shall go out by the north gate. “He shall not return by the way of the gate by which he came in, but shall go out straight before him.” People come to meet God, and they do not retreat the way they came, but cross the court and go out at the opposite side. Why? The text does not explain. Some say it is for order, so that when there are many people they do not crush together. Some say it is a symbol: after people meet God, the direction in which they walk out is different. Whichever you take, this sentence has one main point: you do not go back the same as you came.
@@21116@@
Have you had an experience like this? When you go into church, your heart is full of things: a quarrel left unfinished, a bill not yet paid, your child’s report card in your bag. When the service ends and you walk out the door, the things are all still there, but the way you walk is different. The original door takes you back to the old road; the opposite door takes you into a new day. God does not want you to run away from your days; he wants you to take him with you, and walk back into your days from another direction.
@@21117@@
Verse 10 is the most beautiful sentence in this section: “The prince shall go in with them when they go in. When they go out, he shall go out.” The king does not walk in front of the people, and does not walk behind them; he walks in the middle. The feast crowd is packed tight, and he too must squeeze in. Reading through the whole prophetic book, we see how many times the shepherds feed only themselves and the leaders sit high above; here there is a king standing in the crowd, pushed and squeezed like everyone else. He is not on a platform watching people worship; he is one among those who worship.
@@21118@@
Verses 11 and 12 speak of the “free will offering.” The feasts have fixed offerings, but there is another kind: if the prince prepares a free will offering, a burnt offering or peace offerings as a free will offering to Yahweh, one shall open for him the gate that looks toward the east. No one asks for it; it is what he wants to give in his heart. In a new temple with such a complete system, God left a crack for those who want to give a little more. Notice the last sentence: when he has offered, he goes out, and after he goes out, the gate is shut. He leaves when he has finished, does not stay to see people’s reactions, and does not stop to receive praise. Worship is for God, not for being seen.
@@21119@@
The king going in and out among the people: is this asking us to learn “equality,” or is there another meaning?
@@21120@@
Both, but the center is humility and presence. The king being among the people does not cancel his position; it means his position is not for standing above others. If you have a position at home, at work or in the church, this picture can give you a question: am I walking in front, walking behind, or walking among the people? Sometimes what a leader most needs is not more instructions, but someone willing to squeeze into the crowd, listen to everyone’s voice, and sit down for a meal. This is not telling you to give up responsibility; it is so that your responsibility grows out from among the people, and carries the smell of people.
@@21121@@
III. Light from the Original: Opened, Bow Down, Continual (46:1, 2, 13)
@@21122@@
This chapter has three words worth looking at closely. The first is “opened,” in Hebrew יִפָּתֵחַ; verse 1 says that on the Sabbath the gate shall be “opened.” This word is passive: the gate “is opened.” Who opens it? The text does not say, because the point is not who pushes, but that the way is already open. You do not need to make yourself ready first before going toward him; he has already opened the door and is waiting for you to come. It can become a thirty-second action: in the morning, when you open your eyes, before you look at your phone, say one sentence: Lord, thank you, the door is open.
@@21123@@
The second is “bow down,” in Hebrew הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה; verse 2 says the king bows down in worship at the threshold, and verse 3 says the people use the same verb. Literally it means “to stoop” and “to bend down.” Before this word, king and people have no difference. This word is also the oldest picture of worship: the body is lowered first, and the heart follows. Many times, our hearts will not bow because our bodies will not bow. You can try it: when you pray, do not only bow your head; put your palms up on your knees. When the body has spoken, the heart finds it easier to speak too.
@@21124@@
The third is “continual,” in Hebrew תָּמִיד; verse 15 says the morning burnt offering is “continual.” The meaning of this word is “unbroken.” Not when there is time, not when there is a stirring, but every day. Look at it together with the “free will offering” (נְדָבָה) of verse 12, and it is very interesting: the free will offering is given as the heart wishes; the continual offering is given whatever the mood. Some people like only one of them. God wants both: when you feel stirred, you want to give; when you do not feel stirred, there is also a lamb prepared in the morning.
@@21125@@
Join the three words together: the door is opened, so we walk in; we bow down at the threshold, so we come down from our heights; we have an unbroken starting point every morning, so we do not live by our moods. Opened, bow down, continual: this is a very plain road this chapter gives us. It does not need great determination; it only needs to be walked once each day.
@@21126@@
On days with no stirring, do I still have to give the “continual” offering? Won’t that become a kind of law?
@@21127@@
The “continual” offering is an invitation to a starting point; it is not a ruler to measure whether you are spiritual. On days with no stirring, it can be just a very small action: one sentence, one bow of the head, one verse read. If you are sick, depressed, or so worn out from caring for others that you cannot get out of bed, then on that day God will not blame you; he knows your weakness. The point is not whether you managed “every single day,” but whether your day is willing to begin from him. If for a very long time you cannot find any strength at all, please let someone you trust know, and please consider seeking professional help. This is not a lack of faith; it is taking care of the body and heart that God has entrusted to you.
@@21128@@
IV. The Lamb, the Inheritance, the Kitchen (46:13–24)
@@21129@@
Verses 13 to 15 speak of “every morning.” These three words appear three times in a row. A nation’s day begins here: before work has started, the lamb has already gone up on the altar. A lamb a year old, without defect, with the meal offering and oil, every day, “a perpetual ordinance.” The lamb comes before the day’s business, and the order is: him first, then me. This is not to please God, but to give our day a place that does not move. People’s days are often pushed along by other people’s affairs, and that small morning moment, not pushed away, can help you stand a little steadier all day.
@@21130@@
Verses 16 to 18 turn from the altar to the land, and this is the part of the chapter most like law. The king may give his own inheritance to his sons, and it is theirs forever; if he gives to his servants, it must be returned in the year of liberty. But verse 18 is the main point: “the prince shall not take of the people’s inheritance, to thrust them out of their possession.” The king may give, but he may not take. He may use only his own possession. Why so detailed? The text gives its own reason: “that my people not each be scattered from his possession.” In Israel’s history, too many kings took other people’s land for themselves; Ahab and Naboth’s vineyard is the most famous. This verse is very short, but it says: if worship stays only at the threshold and does not go out to the fields, it is only half a worship.
@@21131@@
To drive a person out of what he has received does not always take a sword. You can push someone out with a sentence, you can fill up someone else’s space with a position, you can make a decision that leaves others no place to speak. After that old gentleman retired, the living room at home was still his, the seat at the dining table was still his, and his daughter-in-law had turned around in the kitchen for twenty years without a drawer of her own. He meant no harm; he had simply never thought of it: the place where he stood was someone else’s inheritance. The first question for a person with a position is not “how much may I take,” but “where I stand, am I crowding anyone?”
@@21132@@
Verses 19 to 24: the prophet is led to the last place: the priests’ holy rooms, at the back to the west, where the trespass offering and the sin offering are boiled and the meal offering is baked, “that they not bring them out into the outer court, to sanctify the people.” Then to the four corners of the outer court, each with a small court, and in it a row of boiling places: “the ministers of the house shall boil the sacrifice of the people.” This chapter begins at the east gate and ends in the kitchen. In places with no applause and where no one sees, God has drawn them into the blueprint too.
@@21133@@
We easily think worship happens only on the stage: songs, preaching, prayer. But the last picture of chapter 46 is a stove, smoke, pots. Someone is washing cups backstage, someone goes out before dawn to buy breakfast, someone stays behind after the meeting to sweep the floor, someone checks the slides once more at the computer. God sees. This is not saying that backstage is more important than the front, and not saying that the priests are above the people; it is saying that he cares about more than the part that is seen. Worship goes from the threshold to the field, and to the table.
@@21134@@
Is this kitchen passage saying that only the priests may approach the things of God, and ordinary believers may not?
@@21135@@
No. The starting point of this passage is protection: holy things are not casually carried among the people, so they are first handled in the inner court, and then the result is brought to everyone. It is not saying that the priests are nearer to God than the people, and much less that ordinary believers may not touch the things of God. The New Testament tells us that all believers are priests (1 Peter 2:9). The real center of this passage is the row of stoves in the corner of the outer court: the people’s sacrifices are boiled there and shared. God’s house has a place for cooking. You need not feel excluded because you hold no office.
@@21136@@
The first deviation: preaching the king at the threshold and the people at the door as a difference of rank, as if those below must not come near. The point of this passage is that the king and the people bow with the same verb, equally low before God. We want the congregation to dare to come nearer, not to feel more unworthy.
@@21137@@
The second deviation: taking the numbers of offerings in verses 4 to 7 as the standard for giving in the church today, asking believers to give by proportion, or using “without defect” to measure people’s giving. These are rules within the vision of the new temple, and not a basis for asking people for money. We want the congregation to become more honest, not more ashamed or more indebted.
@@21138@@
The third deviation: preaching chapters 40 to 48 as prophecy that can be put on a timetable, or declaring that in a certain year and month the temple will be rebuilt and sacrifices restored. These notes set the literal, symbolic and spiritual views side by side, set no dates, and pass no judgment on brothers and sisters who see it differently. We want the congregation to have more patience and love the Lord more, not to be more afraid.
@@21139@@
The fourth deviation: using “the king must not seize the people’s inheritance” to point at some particular leader, institution or government and say they are the “king” here, putting labels on people. This verse speaks to everyone who holds a position, and starts by asking ourselves; and do not use “must not seize” to demand that wounded people forgive at once or not keep score. Protect first, listen first.
@@21140@@
The fifth deviation: preaching the continual offering of “every morning” as a law, saying that without a quiet time there is no grace, making those who are sick or depressed and have no strength to rise blame themselves more; or treating the kitchen as an unimportant detail, or as evidence that the priests’ standing is above the people’s. The center of the text is that he cares about the start of every day, and also cares about the people backstage and at the table. We want the weak to be caught, not condemned.
@@21141@@
It is hard for me to be quiet every morning, and I often feel I am falling behind. How should I face this gap?
@@21142@@
First, put down the word “behind.” “Every morning” is a picture, not a report card. You can shrink the goal to one minute: before you look at your phone, say just one sentence. If you cannot do it today, come again tomorrow; there is no need to make it up. You can also change the time: for some people the morning is the most chaotic, but the first minute after coming home from work is the quietest. The point is to have a starting point that is not pushed away. If you have children or sick people to care for and the morning does not belong to you at all, let him come find you while you wash dishes or ride the bus. His door has never been open only in the morning.
@@21143@@
The first question: we are used to “elders first, those with position first,” and as a result, in worship and in the family, it is often those with a position who have the final say, and those without a position do not even have a drawer. Our respect for elders is a virtue, and we need not throw it away; but chapter 46 reminds us: before God, king and people share the same verb, and the king goes in and out among the people. You can begin like this: this week, in a setting where you have a position, first listen to someone who does not usually speak much, and keep the sentence you were going to say until after he has finished.
@@21144@@
The second question: we are used to “what is seen at the front is what counts,” and the people backstage are often taken for granted. In the church, those who wash the cups, switch on the lights and cook the meals may go the whole year without anyone saying thank you. If we think that only the platform is worship, we will make many people feel ignored beside the smoke and the stoves. At the end of chapter 46 the prophet is led to the kitchen, and it is saying: he sees. You can begin like this: find someone who serves backstage and say one specific word of thanks, saying what he did and what difference it made to you, with no conditions attached.
@@21145@@
I am someone who often serves backstage, and sometimes I feel no one sees me. What should I do?
@@21146@@
Your feeling is real; you do not need to suppress it, and you do not need to pretend not to care. First say it to God: Lord, sometimes I feel that no one sees me. Then keep verse 24 in your heart: God drew the kitchen into the blueprint, and he sees. At the same time, you can also bravely let one person you trust know your situation: not as a complaint, but telling him that you need to be seen, or that you need someone to share the load. The people backstage are the easiest to wear out, because no one asks. If you are already very tired, please rest as needed, and when necessary explain to your pastor and adjust your service; do not carry everything in silence.
