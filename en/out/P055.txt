I. The Man Who Is Led Along (41:1–4)
@@18756@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever gone back and stood in the lane of your old home after it was sold? I know a brother whose father passed away, and the old house was sold to someone else. One day he walked by, stood outside the red brick wall, took out a tape measure, and measured as he walked: how wide the doorway was, how deep the living room, which side the kitchen was on. Passers-by thought he was doing a survey. He was not. He was using the tape to take a home he could never return to, and gather it into his heart, inch by inch. Today’s chapter, chapter 41, is like this man. Only this time, the one holding the measuring tape is not the prophet, but someone else.
@@18757@@
Verse 1 says: he brought me to the temple and measured the posts. Please notice the subject. The one measuring is the man; the prophet is only led along. He does not hold the reed, he does not decide where to go, and he does not ask why. This son of a priest had been in exile for many years, and the temple he used to go in and out of every day had long since become ruins. Now someone stands in front of him and reports the numbers one by one: the posts six cubits thick, the entrance ten cubits wide, the temple forty cubits long and twenty cubits wide. These numbers are not easy to read, but they are the most tender part of this chapter: God remembers that home, down to how wide the posts were.
@@18758@@
Verse 1 also has one more line that is read in different ways: the width matched the tent. Some say it means the tabernacle of the wilderness days, with the posts of the new temple echoing the old tent. Some say the original has only the one word “tent” and does not point to any particular one. Others say the text has copying difficulties here and the original meaning is hard to settle. These notes’ view: this is a place to leave open, and not to insist on one answer. If it does mean the tabernacle, the meaning is beautiful: God’s dwelling place can grow larger, and the heart of the God who lives among his people has not changed.
@@18759@@
Then verse 4: he measured the inner room, twenty cubits long and twenty cubits wide. He said to me, “This is the most holy place.” Look at the small detail in verse 3: when he reaches the inner room, the text no longer says “he brought me,” but only that he went inward. The prophet stops at the doorway. He is not brought in, and the text does not explain what is inside. There is only one sentence: this is the most holy place.
@@18760@@
There are places where all we can do is stand at the door. A sister who accompanied her mother through her last stretch of road said that what she remembers most is the automatic door of the intensive care unit: once it closed, she could only stand outside, listening to the sound of the machines. Later she said that what she learned in those days was not to force her way in, but to stand outside the door and entrust her mother to God. The person standing at the door is not someone who has been shut out. She is someone who has been invited to see and invited to listen.
@@18761@@
Think again about why this chapter begins this way. In the previous chapters, what the prophet saw were walls, gates, and courts, going further in all the way. Here it is the inside of the temple, only one more door away from the most holy place. The nearer a person comes to God’s presence, the fewer the words: no adjectives, no exclamations, only measurements and one simple identification. Sometimes the deepest reverence is silence. When you come here today, you do not need to say much. Being quiet first is also a way of responding.
@@18762@@
The prophet was only brought to the doorway and did not go into the most holy place. Does this mean God does not let me come near?
@@18763@@
No. “He went inward” in verse 3 only says that the work of measuring was done by the man while the prophet stayed where he was; the text does not say the prophet was refused, and does not say he did anything wrong. For a priest’s son, the most holy place was originally a place even he could not enter casually, and this arrangement agrees with the old order. Please tell apart two kinds of doorway: one is the doorway of someone who has been driven out, and the other is the doorway of someone who has been invited to listen. This chapter is the latter. You can practice it this way: the next time you pray, do not ask first, but stand quietly for one minute, and only say to God: I am at the door, I am listening. God has measured it and prepared it, and people do not need to rush in to prove anything. If you are standing outside some door right now, waiting for someone, that one minute of quiet is itself a prayer.

II. The Side Rooms: Leaning on the Temple, Not Piercing It (41:5–15)
@@18765@@
Around the temple there is a ring of side rooms. Verse 5 says each was four cubits wide, and verse 6 says there were three stories, one above another, thirty rooms on each story. What were these rooms used for? The text does not say. Some say they were space for storage and service, some say they were rooms for the priests, and we do not need to guess. Please look at one key point: the side rooms were built around the temple. The temple is in the middle, and the side rooms are around it. Those who serve stand around God’s presence.
@@18766@@
Then comes the most wonderful sentence in verse 6: the beams of the side rooms rested on the ledges of the temple wall, so that they would not penetrate the temple wall. It means: the side rooms lean on the temple, but must not drive their own beams into the temple’s wall. The building principle is very simple: a ledge is left on the outside of the temple wall, and the beams rest on the ledge, carrying their own weight without piercing the wall. The side rooms have their own beams and their own weight, but they do not tear down the temple’s walls.
@@18767@@
I know a brother who served in the church for many years, and slowly that ministry became his name. When people asked who was in charge, everyone said it was him. Later he fell ill and was in the hospital for half a year, and the ministry also stopped for half a year. After he was discharged he said to me: I found out that I had driven my own beam into God’s wall, and I could neither pull it out nor hand it over. After that, little by little, he learned to rest the beam back on the ledge and let others take over. He said it was actually lighter this way.
@@18768@@
Verse 7 says the side rooms grew wider as they went up. Because the temple wall stepped in layer by layer as it rose, the side rooms became wider with each story. Please be careful: this is a description of a building, not a formula for success. It does not mean that the bigger the ministry, the more spiritual it is, or that the higher you climb, the better. Every story leans on the temple, and the higher it goes, the wider it is, and the more it surrounds the same center.
@@18769@@
Verses 8 to 11 talk about the raised platform, the outer wall, the open space, and the doors: the foundation of the side rooms was a full rod high, the outer wall five cubits thick, between the side rooms and the other rooms there was a space of twenty cubits, the open area around was five cubits wide, and the doors faced one to the north and one to the south. Verses 12 to 15 are about the building on the west side, seventy cubits wide and ninety cubits long; the temple, with the open space and that building, was one hundred cubits each way. Some see these measurements as symbols, some see them as an actual building plan, and these notes do not settle on either. What we see is this: God’s house has holiness inside and open space outside; between the space for service and the most holy place, a gap is left. This gap protects the holy, and it also protects those who serve.
@@18770@@
Please look at one more detail: the side rooms have three stories, thirty rooms on each story, ninety rooms in all, and not one of them pierces the temple wall. Ninety rooms, all leaning on the same temple. This reminds us that in the church there are many people and many ministries, each with its own room and its own beams; God’s temple is not held up by any one room, and every room stands by leaning on the temple. You do not need to be the biggest room. You only need not to tear down the temple.
@@18771@@
“The beam rests on the ledge and does not pierce the wall”: what does this actually mean for service?
@@18772@@
We can start with three pictures. First, service leans on God’s presence, not on our own reputation; the side rooms cannot exist apart from the temple. Second, service has its own weight, which we carry ourselves, but we cannot use God’s house as our own scaffolding. Third, if a ministry is something only you understand and only you can do, your beam may already have been nailed into the wall. You can ask yourself three questions: if I left for three months, could this still run? Does anyone know how I do it? In my heart, is there a little fear of being replaced by someone else? If the answers disturb you, do not blame yourself. Just begin with one small thing: teach one step to a companion.

III. The Cherubim and Palm Trees on the Wall (41:16–26)
@@18774@@
Verses 16 and 17 say that from the ground up to the windows, the walls inside and out were covered with wood paneling. The windows were closed windows with coverings. This is an enclosed, quiet space: the sounds from outside cannot get in, and the light inside is not scattered about carelessly.
@@18775@@
Then comes the picture on the wall. Verse 18: on the wall were carved cherubim and palm trees. A palm tree was between every two cherubim, and every cherub had two faces. Verse 19: on one side the face of a man toward the palm tree, on the other side the face of a lion toward the palm tree, all around the inside of the temple. Please picture this wall: cherub, palm tree, cherub, palm tree, all the way along. The man’s face toward the tree, the lion’s face toward the tree. The two faces do not look at each other; together they look toward that tree.
@@18776@@
How should this carving be read? One reading: the man’s face stands for compassion and understanding, the lion’s face for majesty and authority, and God’s protection holds both. Another reading: this is an echo of the garden of Eden, where the cherubim guarded the tree of life, so guarding and life are put together again in one place. Another view: it is simply the decoration of the temple, the prophet does not explain it, and we do not need to say more than the text does. These notes set them side by side and settle on none.
@@18777@@
A sister who volunteers in a hospital said that when she accompanies people in the ward, sometimes she is like the lion’s face, wanting to protect and to stand in front of them; sometimes she is like the man’s face, unable to do anything and only able to sit beside them. She said: later I learned that whichever face it is, I turn toward the same tree. When I do not know what to do, I let myself turn toward that one tree.
@@18778@@
Verse 22 is the most special: the altar was of wood, three cubits high, and two cubits long. He said to me, “This is the table that is before Yahweh.” The altar is not stone but wood; it is not an altar, but a table. Scholars read it differently: some say it is the altar of incense, some say it is the table of the bread of the Presence, and some say it is a symbol of the two together. These notes do not settle it. What we see is that in front of the most holy place there is a table made of wood, and God calls it the table before Yahweh. Verses 23 to 26 add one more thing: each door had two leaves, folding ones, and cherubim and palm trees were carved on the leaves of the doors too. The doors can open and can close, not to keep people out, but so that people can slowly come near.
@@18779@@
Verse 20 adds a line: from the ground to above the door, there were cherubim and palm trees. It means the whole wall, from underfoot to above the door frame, was this picture. When people walk in, whether they look up, look down, or turn around, what their eyes see is protection and life. In our homes, if we have a wall, what do we see every day? One sister stuck a verse of Scripture on her kitchen wall and read it once each time she washed the dishes. She said that wall slowly became her cherubim and palm trees.
@@18780@@
The cherubim have the face of a man and the face of a lion. What is the spiritual meaning? Do I need to find the standard answer?
@@18781@@
No. The text only says that there were cherubim on the wall, with two faces, a man’s face on one side and a lion’s face on the other, both toward the palm tree; it does not say what they stand for. Bible scholars read it in different ways: some see compassion and majesty, some see the guarding of Eden, and some take it as only the decoration of the temple. The attitude here is important: what the text does not say, we should not turn into “the Bible says.” But one direction is steady: the faces are turned toward the palm tree, that is, toward life. You can take it as a meditation, not as doctrine: today, which of my faces has turned toward life? Has my compassion, or my stubbornness, turned toward the very thing God wants me to keep?
@@18782@@
IV. Light from the Original: Grasp, Holy, Table (41:6, 4, 22)
@@18783@@
This chapter has three original words that you can take home to your daily life in thirty seconds. The first is “grasp,” the Hebrew אֲחוּזִים (akhuzim). Verse 6 says the beams of the side rooms were “not to penetrate the temple wall”; the original, literally, is: so that they would not be grasped in the temple wall. The root אָחַז (akhaz) means to grasp, to hold, to possess; in Genesis, the land that Abraham bought is called a “possession,” using the same root. The beam is not grasped by the temple wall, and does not grasp the temple wall. It is the same for those who serve: if we do not grasp, we will not be bound by God’s house; if we are not grasped, we can freely come near.
@@18784@@
The second is “holy,” the Hebrew קֹדֶשׁ (qodesh). Verse 4 says “most holy place,” which in the original is קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים, literally the holy of holies. The meaning of this word is set apart, given to God. Holy is not cold; it is belonging to him alone. The walls of the most holy place are measured so clearly in order to say: this place belongs to God alone. Do you have a time, a place, that belongs only to him? It does not need to be big. Just one small corner, kept for him each day.
@@18785@@
The third is “table,” the Hebrew שֻׁלְחָן (shulkhan). Verse 22 says this is the table before Yahweh. In the Old Testament, a table is for setting out bread, for eating, for welcoming guests. At the door of the most holy place there is a table: God is the host who sets the feast. People are not only able to stand far off; he has a table, and he has a seat. An old pastor said that every time he thinks of this verse, he thinks of the dining table in his own home: that is the place where the whole family is most at ease. He said that God’s table should not be more frightening than a dining table.
@@18786@@
Put the three words together: do not grasp (akhuzim), because this is his house; holy (qodesh), because this place belongs to him; table (shulkhan), because he has prepared a seat. Not grasping is a lesson for ourselves; holy is reverence toward God; the table is God’s invitation to people.
@@18787@@
“Not grasping” sounds negative. Shouldn’t we actively strive and hold tightly to what God has entrusted to us?
@@18788@@
We need to tell two kinds of “grasping” apart. One is faithfully holding on to what has been entrusted: doing what we should do well, neither making light of it nor shirking it. The other is treating the trust as our own property: this is my ministry, my position, my territory. The side rooms in verse 6 have their own beams and their own weight, and they are not doing nothing; they simply do not grasp the temple wall. So faithfulness and letting go do not conflict. You can use a prayer to tell them apart: Lord, you have entrusted this to me to keep for a while; I am willing to keep it well, and I am willing to let go when you ask me to. Keeping it well and being able to let go are often the same heart.
@@18789@@
The first deviation is to treat chapter 41 as a blueprint from which dates can be calculated, saying that the day for building the temple is near. The text gives no date and does not ask anyone to draw up a timetable; on chapters 40 to 48, the readers hold different views, whether literal, symbolic, or spiritual, and we set them side by side without judging. We want the congregation to love the Lord more and to be more patient, and not to be more anxious and more fond of guessing.
@@18790@@
The second deviation is to preach the prophet’s not going into the inner room in verse 3 as God rejecting people, or people not being qualified. The text only says that the man went in to measure; it does not say the prophet was driven away. The person standing at the doorway is a person invited to listen. We want people to come near more quietly, and not to be more afraid of being excluded.
@@18791@@
The third deviation is to preach the side rooms getting wider as they go higher in verse 7 as “the bigger the ministry, the more spiritual,” or “the higher you climb, the more blessed.” This is the description of a building, not a formula for success. Every story leans on the temple, and the higher it goes, the wider it is, and the more it surrounds the same center. We want those who serve to draw nearer to the Lord, and not to care more about size.
@@18792@@
The fourth deviation is to preach the cherubim and palm trees on the wall as the only correct meaning, and to use it to judge other people’s interpretation. The text does not explain it, and the readings are set side by side; some see guarding and life, and some see only the temple’s decoration. We want people to be more humble before the text, and not more argumentative.
@@18793@@
The fifth deviation is to preach the table before Yahweh in verse 22 as something that must be restored as a certain institution, or to put down some other kind of worship. Whether it is the altar of incense, the table of the bread of the Presence, or a symbol of both, interpreters differ, and these notes do not settle it. We want the congregation to be more reverent and to draw nearer, and not to quarrel with one another over the table.
@@18794@@
Chapter 41 is just a pile of measurements. Why should I still read it?
@@18795@@
Because it is spoken to a people who have lost their home. Measurements slow people’s steps: number after number lets people know that God is not speaking casually; he has measured, and he has truly prepared a place. You can read it this way: with each number, pause for a second and say to yourself: God remembers. Then there are three more reminders: the side rooms lean on the temple and do not pierce it; the prophet stands at the doorway and does not rush in; on the wall, the cherubim and palm trees, guarding and life are together. You do not need to memorize these three things; you only need to slow down. Some people read this chapter for only ten minutes, and stayed on one of the pictures for a whole evening. That is enough.
@@18796@@
The first question: we are used to taking ministry, position, and title as our own name. In Chinese churches, “Deacon so-and-so” and “Group Leader so-and-so” often take the place of a person’s name. After a while we can no longer tell even ourselves: am I serving God, or guarding my position? The beams of the side rooms in verse 6 remind us: those who serve should lean on the temple but not pierce it. You can examine yourself this way: this past month, did I ever, on my own initiative, teach one step of something to someone else? Say the name, and say which task it was.
@@18797@@
The second question: we are used to using “rules” in place of “drawing near.” What to wear to the gathering, where to sit, what to say, all of it has a set pattern; sometimes we think that keeping the rules well is drawing near to God. But in chapter 41 there are measurements, doors, and wooden thresholds, and in the end a table, the table before Yahweh. Rules are there to let people come near, not to make people afraid. You can practice this way: this week, find one evening, and in the plainest words, as if sitting at a dinner table, tell God about one thing from today. You do not need a prayer tone.
@@18798@@
I have a very important role in the church. If I step back, I am afraid the ministry will stop, and I am also afraid I will have no value. What should I do?
@@18799@@
This worry is very real, and very honest. Please do not blame yourself. First tell apart two kinds of fear: fear that the ministry will be affected, which is love; and fear that you yourself have no value, which is a wound. The beam resting on the ledge in verse 6 does not mean pulling yourself out, but not letting the weight of the whole wall rest on one person. You can do three small things: first, write down your work; second, find someone willing to learn, and invite him to do it with you for a month; third, say one sentence to God every day: my value is not in what I have done, it is that you love me. If this sense of loss feels very heavy, or has already affected your sleep and your emotions, please talk with a pastor or someone you trust.
@@18800@@
VII. Living Out 321: The Order of the Temple and the Side Rooms
@@18801@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the true temple, and also the Lord who serves: the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve. He draws near to people, yet never nails his authority into someone else’s place. What we learn is his pattern: lean on the temple but do not pierce it; be willing to serve but do not possess.
@@18802@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 41 not to calculate measurements, but to let God’s word measure our hearts cubit by cubit: Have I nailed my beam into God’s wall? Have I treated God’s house as my own turf? What the text does not say, we do not add; what the text does say, we do not hide from.
@@18803@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. The prophet stood at the doorway and did not rush in; it is the Holy Spirit who leads us in when to draw near, when to wait, and when to let go. Each day, be quiet for a moment and ask one question: Lord, what is there today that I should set back on the ledge?
@@18804@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness does not mean having nothing; it means no longer saying, “This is my ministry.” Whoever lets Jesus be King rests his beam back on the ledge, lets him be the master of the temple, and is glad to be a side room.
@@18805@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. However many the side rooms, however high, however wide, the glory is still in the temple. If we are able to serve, it is not because we are capable, but because he prepared the place. The glory goes to the Lord who measured and prepared.
@@18806@@
To build God’s system is the order of the temple and the side rooms: the temple is God’s presence, and the side rooms are the space for service. Life first, then relationships, then ministry. Ministry leans on him and does not replace him; among people, each has his own beam and his own place. A community like this is a home with order, with room to spare, and with a table.
@@18807@@
What does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with “the beam rests on the ledge”?
@@18808@@
The relationship is very direct. Who is the temple and who is the side room is a question of order. To let Jesus be King is to let him be the master of the temple; if I drive my own beam into the wall, I am quietly putting myself in the temple’s place. Every time I say “this is mine,” it is a small usurping of the throne. But this is not asking us to deny our own contribution; it is returning to our position: I am a side room, I am important, but I am not the temple. You can practice this way: take the one thing you hold most tightly, say it out loud before God, and then say: Lord, this is yours, I am only keeping it for a while. When you have said it, perhaps your shoulders will loosen a little.
@@18809@@
The proud type says, “This ministry cannot go on without me.” The text says to him: the beams rested on the ledge so that they would not penetrate the temple wall. Pride is leaving one’s position and seizing God’s power; returning to one’s position is admitting that I am the side room and he is the temple. You can start by letting go of one small thing and letting someone else do it; you do not need to let go of everything at once.
@@18810@@
The fearful type says, “If I let go, this will collapse, and I will have no place to stand.” The text lets him see a ring of side rooms: each one leans on the temple, not on one person. The fearful type needs to learn to change “all depends on me” into “leaning on him.” You can start with one very small step, and let a companion join you.
@@18811@@
The ashamed type says, “It is enough for me to stand at the doorway; I do not deserve to go in.” The doorway is not a place of shame. The prophet also stood at the doorway, and God still spoke to him. The ashamed type can first pause at the doorway and listen to God say: this is the place I have prepared. You do not have to get well first before coming near; the table is already set there.
@@18812@@
The wounded type says, “I gave so much for this place, and was replaced, and I do not want to come near again.” God does not blame you for being hurt; he has measured this house, and he remembers every cubit of what you gave. Those who are hurt can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story; those who accompany should not hurry to say “you need to let go,” but only say: I am here, I am with you.

<p><strong>① “One cubit” and “one hundred cubits” of the passage</strong></p><ul><li>The Masoretic Text reads “a length of one cubit” (original אַמָּה אַחַת).</li><li>Ancient translations such as the Septuagint read “a length of one hundred cubits.”</li><li>The CUV follows the latter: “a length of one hundred cubits.”</li></ul><p>The original of this verse has a copying difference, and both readings have ancient witnesses.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: here we follow the CUV and read it as one hundred cubits long, matching the length of the holy rooms.</strong> This difference does not affect the meaning of this passage.</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>This chapter begins with walking north.</strong></p><p>In the previous chapter the prophet stood inside the temple; now the man brings him out, toward the north, to the outer court, and into a row of holy rooms. The original says “holy rooms,” that is, the rooms used by the priests.</p><p>This passage is long and also hard to read: one hundred cubits, fifty cubits, ten cubits, three-story galleries, a paved floor, inner court and outer court, layer upon layer. In fact we can look at just one thing: this is a row of rooms for “people.” The temple is for God, the court is for the people, and this row of rooms is for “the priests who are near to Yahweh.”</p><p>Between God and the people, the Bible arranged a row of rooms. This row of rooms is not far from the temple, and not far from the people; it is a place that both sides can reach. It is the place of the one in the middle.</p><p>This matters. Because those who serve often please neither side: before God they feel they are not holy enough, and before people they feel not close enough. This row of rooms tells them: you have a place, and you have a room, and he prepared it.</p><p><strong>Landing it:</strong> If you happen to be in that “middle” position and feel tired, remember: this position was measured out by God himself.</p>

<p><strong>No pillars, so the upper story is narrower.</strong></p><p>This passage gives an interesting reason: the holy rooms had three stories but no pillars, unlike the rooms of the outer court that had pillars, so the upper story was narrower than the middle and lower ones.</p><p>In plain words: if it cannot hold up, it has to shrink. A building without pillars cannot get wider story by story; it can only get narrower story by story.</p><p>This detail holds a gentle reminder. Both are three-story buildings; the side rooms of the previous chapter grew wider as they went up, and the holy rooms of this chapter grow narrower as they go up. Where is the difference? One had structural support, and the other did not.</p><p>People are the same. In the same service, some people grow wider as they go on, and some grow narrower. The difference is often not talent, but whether there are pillars: whether there is a support that is not yourself.</p><p>The text does not blame the lack of pillars; it only says: so the upper story is narrower. This is an honest way of writing, without scorn and without exaggeration.</p><p><strong>Landing it:</strong> Does your service have pillars now? It might be a companion, a fixed time of prayer, or someone who will tell you the truth. If you have none, start today to find one.</p>

<p><strong>The rooms on the south are the same as those on the north.</strong></p><p>These verses are short, and easy to skip. They mean only one sentence: the holy rooms on the south are the same as the holy rooms on the north, with the same length and width, the same exits, and the same style of doors.</p><p>Why mention it in particular? Because in a new temple, God shows no favoritism in the rooms the priests use: what the north has, the south has too; the rule on this side is the same on that side.</p><p>We are used to scoring different positions of service: some are in front, some in back; some face the light, and some face the cold north. But here, south and north are symmetrical, and neither side is nobler.</p><p>The place where those who serve are most easily hurt is comparison. Seeing that another person’s room is brighter, and your own is colder. But in this temple, both sides were measured by him, and both sides are “holy rooms.”</p><p><strong>Landing it:</strong> Today, if you cannot help comparing, stop and look at your own “door.” The place he gave you and the place he gave another were both measured by his own hand.</p>
