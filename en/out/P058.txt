@@19778@@
The first deviation is to preach “I will dwell here” (43:7) as if merely coming to church means everything is well, and we need not face the dead things in our lives. The text says both that he dwells and that people must put away what is detestable. We want the congregation to trust more that he lives among us, and to be more willing to move far away what they have placed beside his threshold, not to become more careless or complacent.
@@19779@@
The second deviation is to read chapter 43 as “the glory has returned, so all pain is over,” and to say to someone who is suffering, bereaved or seriously ill: “God has come back, so do not grieve any more.” His return is real, but the tears are also real. We want those who suffer to be accompanied first, not told to be well right away.
@@19780@@
The third deviation is to make belief in one particular reading of the new temple, whether literal, symbolic, the church or spiritual, the standard of faith, or to use this chapter to set a timetable for the restoration of the new temple and the sacrifices, counting days and sticking on labels. What the text gives us is the return of the glory, not a schedule. We want the congregation to love the Lord more and to be more patient, not more anxious or more argumentative.
@@19781@@
The fourth deviation is to turn “ashamed” (43:10–11) into a tool for shaming people, or to turn “cleanse the altar first” into a formula to be followed, so that if you are not better after seven days, something is wrong with you. Some even say to someone who has been hurt: “First move the dead things far away, and then God will come to live in you.” That puts the responsibility on the victim. We want people to dare to come toward God, not to want to hide more.
@@19782@@
The fifth deviation is to take “I will accept you” (43:27) to mean that there is no need to repent or be cleansed. Or the opposite: to frighten believers with the picture of the glory leaving, as if God will leave you if you do not obey. The text says: cleansed first, then accepted; the acceptance comes before the offering, yet it does not cancel the cleansing. We want the congregation to love the Lord more within his acceptance, not to live in the fear of being cast away.
@@19783@@
People in our group have very different views about the new temple, and it even turns into arguments. How should I lead?
@@19784@@
Lay them side by side first, then return to the text. You can invite each person to say their reading in one sentence, without evaluating, and then ask: whichever reading we hold, which sentence do we all agree on? The word “ashamed” in verse 11, “I will dwell” in verse 7, and “I will accept you” in verse 27 are things every view agrees on. Then remind everyone: these notes do not make any one reading a condition of faith, and they do not set a timetable or count days. If an argument makes someone feel looked down on, stop first, and care for that person privately after the meeting. The goal is that people love the Lord more and bear with one another more, not that anyone wins.
@@19785@@
The first question: we are used to getting ourselves in order before we dare to come in. When a guest is coming, we first sweep the floor, wipe the table and stuff the mess into the cupboard; with God it is the same. We feel we have not yet changed enough, or not yet made peace with someone, so we do not dare to pray or to walk through the church door. But the order of chapter 43 is that he comes first, he cleanses first, and he says “accept” first. This week, at a moment when you feel “not good enough yet,” open your mouth anyway and say just one sentence: Lord, I am not in order yet, but I have come.
@@19786@@
The second question: we are used to “worth comes from what we give,” using service, offerings and busyness to buy a little peace of mind, even something like peace. This easily gets tangled up with the thinking in our culture that “sincerity must be enough” and “blessing must be exchanged for merit.” The order in verse 27 turns it upside down: he first says “I will accept you,” and only then do people offer. Before your next time of service, take a minute and ask yourself: am I starting from his acceptance, or am I trying to use this service to buy acceptance? You need not stop serving right away; just let the question sit in your heart.

<p><strong>There is a gate that stays shut forever.</strong></p><p>The prophet is brought to the outer gate facing east, and that gate is shut. He says: “This gate shall be shut. It shall not be opened, no man shall enter in by it.”</p><p>There is only one reason: “for Yahweh, the God of Israel, has entered in by it. Therefore it shall be shut.”</p><p>This reason is very unusual. Usually a gate is shut to keep something outside from coming in. This gate is shut not to keep anyone out, but because he walked through. A gate his feet have crossed is no longer a gate for people to use at will.</p><p>It is a quiet picture: the glory went in, and the gate closed. It does not shut people out; it keeps his presence inside. No human road passes through here any more.</p><p>Look again at the figure in verse 3: that “prince,” though he may sit in the porch and eat bread before Yahweh, comes in only from the porch and goes out from the porch. Even he cannot walk through that gate. Power has to stop in front of the gate he walked through.</p><p><strong>Landing:</strong> Is there a place that “he walked through” that you want to go around and open again? Some gates are shut as a grace. Today, in front of that gate, learn to sit down and eat bread.</p>

<p><strong>What he rebukes is not those outside the gate, but those inside it.</strong></p><p>From this section the tone changes. It may be the teaching of your children, or your own devotions. Today, take it back and keep it yourself.</p>

<p><strong>① Why were the Levites demoted?</strong></p><ul><li>It is a punishment for idolatry: those who served idols can no longer draw near to him.</li><li>It is a reassignment of service: they still have a place, only they are no longer priests.</li></ul><p>The text’s own reason is: they ministered to the people before their idols and became a stumbling block of iniquity.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the consequence is real, yet the place is not removed.</strong> They lose the nearness but keep the service: “bearing their shame” and “performing the duty of the house” both hold at the same time.</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>What they lost was the front row, not the temple.</strong></p><p>The Levites once left him and followed idols. His handling has two parts.</p><p>The first part is the consequence: “They will bear their iniquity.” They may not come near to him to serve as his priests, and may not come near any of the most holy things. This consequence is not light, and it is not watered down.</p><p>The second part is the charge: “Yet I will make them performers of the duty of the house, for all its service, and for all that will be done therein.” This “yet” is the heaviest turn in the whole passage. Today, go and do that work well.</p>

<p><strong>① “I am their inheritance”</strong></p><ul><li>Literal: the priests are not allotted land, and live from the people’s offerings.</li><li>Spiritual: he himself is the priests’ inheritance.</li></ul><p>The two do not conflict. Numbers 18:20 has the same words.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: both are true at once.</strong> They have no land, but he has; he is both the giver and the gift.</p></blockquote><p><strong>② “The first of your dough”</strong></p><p>The original word עֲרִסוֹת means coarsely ground dough or coarse meal; the CUV translates it “the first of the wheat, ground into flour.” The meaning is close: the first portion belongs to him.</p>
@@19899@@
<p><strong>He says: I am their inheritance.</strong></p><p>The last section speaks of the priests’ life. His words are very striking: “They shall have an inheritance. I am their inheritance; and you shall give them no possession in Israel. I am their possession.”</p><p>This chapter begins with a shut gate, passes through a long list of rules, clothing, hair, wine, marriage, dead bodies, and at last lands on this sentence. All the restrictions before it seem to be wrapped up in this one sentence.</p><p>The priests have no land and no field, nothing they can pass on to their sons. He says: you have none, but I have. I am. He does not give them a smaller version, but the largest version: himself.</p><p>This arrangement has a very practical result: the priests’ life depends on the people’s offerings. The people must offer the first fruits and give the first of the dough to the priests, so that blessing rests on the home. Giving to the priests is giving to him, and blessing comes from this flow.</p><p>The last sentence is also gentle: what dies of itself or is torn, the priests must not eat. The bottom line is very concrete, yet it is only a small sentence, placed beside the great declaration before it. His people have not only lofty declarations but also daily bottom lines.</p><p>If a person takes him as an inheritance, he need not hurry to hoard for himself. The hands can open, because the greatest portion is already with him.</p><p><strong>Landing:</strong> Is there some “inheritance” you are afraid of losing, and so you grip it tightly? Today, try saying once: you are my inheritance. Then loosen your grip a little.</p>
