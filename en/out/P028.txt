I. First Hear the Sound of the Sharpening (21:1–7)
@@9246@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. In the alley where I lived as a child, every year before the New Year an old man came pushing a cart to sharpen knives. He turned a long bench upside down, set a stone on it, poured a little water, and the blade went back and forth on the stone. Shh, shh, shh. When the housewives in the whole alley heard that sound, they knew the New Year was coming, and it was time to kill the chickens. The sound of sharpening is not loud, but no one fails to hear it. Today’s chapter begins with this sound, only this time the one holding the blade is not the old man.
@@9247@@
In verse 2 God said to the prophet: “Set your face toward Jerusalem, and preach toward the sanctuaries, and prophesy against the land of Israel.” Please notice two place names: Jerusalem, and the sanctuary. These were the places where they felt most at ease in all their lives, the places they thought would shield them when trouble came. And God’s word falls precisely on these two places. This is not news that comes in from outside; it begins from the very inside.
@@9248@@
Verse 3 is the heaviest sentence in the whole chapter: “Behold, I am against you, and will draw my sword out of its sheath, and will cut off from you the righteous and the wicked.” Let us stop here a moment. It does not say “I will send someone.” It says “I am against you”; God himself stands in front of the city. And the sword is “my sword.” Babylon does not appear in this chapter until verse 19.
@@9249@@
Cut off the righteous together with the wicked? You may think at once of chapter 18: the one who sins will die; God takes no pleasure in anyone’s death. How do the two passages read together? Interpreters differ. Some say this describes the scale of a great disaster on the whole city, which falls without sorting people out; some say the “righteous” here are those counted righteous in human eyes; and some say chapter 18 answers an excuse, while chapter 21 describes the fall of a city, speaking on different levels. These notes do not make any one of them a condition of faith. We hold on to just two things: the scale of this sword is the whole; and God’s care for each person has not been changed by this sword, and the words of 18:23 still stand.
@@9250@@
Verse 3 says that God will cut off even the righteous. Does that mean believers will also be punished by God?
@@9251@@
No. This verse is spoken to a city that again and again refused to turn back. It speaks of the scale on the day the city falls, when disaster comes down and even good people can hardly escape it; it is not passing sentence on every suffering person. Interpreters differ on what “the righteous” means, and we do not need to make any one reading a condition of faith. What we can hold on to is 18:23: “Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked?” says the Lord Yahweh; “and not rather that he should return from his way, and live?” So if you are in the middle of a disaster, this verse does not say you are guilty; it says God is serious. You can say to God: Lord, I am afraid, please stay with me.
@@9252@@
Then God told the prophet to do one thing. Verse 6: “Therefore sigh, you son of man. You shall sigh before their eyes with a broken heart and with bitterness.” And if they ask why you sigh, you say: because of the news that is coming. Please notice the order: before the message was even delivered, the prophet first bent over. The bad news had not yet arrived, and his body had already given way. Have you ever stood on a station platform, taken a phone call, and after hearing it slowly held on to a pillar and crouched down? It is not for anyone to see; you simply cannot hold yourself up. When God’s servant delivers bad news, he does not throw the words down and walk away; he first carries the weight on his own waist.
@@9253@@
II. A Sword, a Sword: A Song Sharpened on the Stone (21:8–17)
@@9254@@
From verse 9 on, God’s speech suddenly gets shorter. A sword, a sword, sharpened and also polished. The sentences jump out one by one, like a blade going back and forth on a stone. This is a poem, not a disaster report. Read it slowly and listen to the rhythm: sharpened, that it may make a slaughter; polished, that it may be as lightning. The use of this song is to let people hear it while there is still time.
@@9255@@
There is one line in it that many translations read with struggle: “Should we then make mirth?” Verse 10. How to translate this line, and who is speaking, differ from one scholar to another. But however you read it, you do not hear any cheering. This is not a God who is pleased with himself, but a God who has no choice but to sharpen the sword. We often think of judgment as God’s bad mood, as if one day he is displeased and takes out the sword. But look back at chapters 12, 14, 17, and 18: he used parables, he used actions, he used riddles, he used arguments. The sword is taken out last.
@@9256@@
Then God gives the prophet four actions in a row: cry, wail, strike the thigh, strike the hands. Verse 12 says that what the sword falls on is “my people.” Please hold on to these two words, “my people.” God has the prophet wail because the ones who are wounded are his own. Verse 16 says: “Go to the right. Set yourselves in array. Go to the left, wherever your face is set.” The sword strikes wherever it faces. By verse 17, God says: “I will also strike my hands together, and I will cause my wrath to rest.” Even the resting of this sword is in his hand.
@@9257@@
This song of the sword is frightening. How do we preach such a passage without scaring people?
@@9258@@
First ask yourself: after listening, do people love the Lord more, or do they only fear more? This song of the sword is a poem, short, hard, rhythmic, and its purpose is to wake people up while there is still time, not to make a horror film. When you speak, do not enlarge the pictures of sword, fire, and blood, and do not add pictures to the text; if someone in the congregation has experienced violence or bereavement, protect that person first. You can lead like this: first let everyone hear God’s reluctance (verse 10), then hear that he does not let go (“my people” in verse 12), and only last speak of the resting of the sword (verse 17). Arranged this way, the congregation hears a God whose heart aches, not a God who rages.
@@9259@@
This section is easily preached as a horror film, and we will not do that. What we want to hear is this: when the prophet strikes his thigh and wails, he is hurting along with God’s heart. A hospital chaplain once told me that before telling a family bad news, he always stands in the corridor for one minute, not to rehearse his words, but to keep his hands from trembling. One who delivers heavy words is not a person without feeling.
@@9260@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Sword, Sigh, Overturn (21:3, 6, 27)
@@9261@@
In this chapter there are three words that we would miss a whole layer of if we did not look at the original. The first is “sword,” חֶרֶב (chereb), verse 3. This word appears again and again through the whole chapter and is the chapter’s main character. But look at the person in verse 3: it is not “the sword of Babylon,” it is “my sword,” חַרְבִּי. We are used to thinking that disaster is outsiders coming to harm us, but the text asks us to see that the owner of the sword is Yahweh. This sentence can become daily life within thirty seconds: when disaster comes, I can honestly say I am afraid, without having to say “everything is out of control.” The sword is in a hand.
@@9262@@
The second is “sigh,” אָנַח (anach), verse 6. When we say “sigh,” we mean a light breath let out. This word is not that. The text puts it together with “a broken heart,” literally “the breaking of the loins”: בְּשִׁבְרוֹן מָתְנַיִם, which means sighing as if the waist were broken. The place where strength lies breaks first. God told the prophet to sigh like this, and “before their eyes.” Sorrow may be seen. In our Chinese churches we often teach people to wipe away their tears and say “have faith.” This word reminds us that when God’s servant proclaims the truth, he may bend over.
@@9263@@
The third is “overturn,” עַוָּה (avvah), verse 27. This word is said three times in a single verse: עַוָּה עַוָּה עַוָּה. The CUV renders it “overturned, overturned, and overturned again.” Its root carries the sense of bent and twisted, like something turned over again and again. A position that people prop up with their own hands will be turned over, again and again. But look at the second half of the sentence: “until he comes whose right it is; and I will give it.” Being turned over is not for throwing away; it is for handing it to the one who should have it.
@@9264@@
Put the three words together and this is the road of the chapter: the sword (chereb) is held in God’s hand; the sigh (anach) is God’s servant bending over before the sword; the overturning (avvah) is the crown that people propped up being turned over. But the last sentence is not the sword; it is “I will give it.”
@@9265@@
What use is it to my Bible reading to learn so many original-language words?
@@9266@@
There is only one test: within thirty seconds, can this word become a way of living today? The person of “sword” tells me that disaster is not without someone in charge; the strength of “sigh” tells me that sorrow does not have to be pushed down; “overturn,” said three times and followed by “I will give it,” tells me that the turning over is not the end. You do not need to remember Hebrew; just remember this: the next time you face a hard time, first ask “whose hand is this sword in?” and then ask “may I weep now?” These two questions are the gift of this original-language section to you. The rest can stay in the study. If someone in the group gets nervous at the sound of Hebrew, you can speak only the Chinese meaning, one word and one sentence, and that is complete too.
@@9267@@
IV. The Lots at the Fork, and the Crown Taken Off (21:18–32)
@@9268@@
From verse 18, God has the prophet do something much like a child’s drawing: mark out two roads on the ground, branching from the same place, and draw a hand at the head of the road to the city. One road leads to Rabbah of the Ammonites, the other to Jerusalem, the fortified city of Judah. Have you ever stood in a bus terminal hall? The same platform, buses going two directions; get on the wrong one and your whole night is different. The king of Babylon was standing at just such a fork.
@@9269@@
Verse 21 says he was to use divination: “He shook the arrows back and forth. He consulted the teraphim. He looked in the liver.” Please notice that this is the way of the nations; the text does not say divination is reliable, and does not tell us to learn it. What the text wants to say is: the lot fell on Jerusalem, and the whole fork in the road is under God’s control. Even when a king who does not know him shakes the arrows, he is there. This sentence is not meant for us to explain every disaster. When we see others in trouble, the easiest thing to say is “he must have done something wrong”; Job’s friends said exactly that, and were later rebuked by God. This chapter only tells us: God is not far away.
@@9270@@
The king of Babylon used divination to decide where to go. Does that mean we may also seek God’s counsel through divination?
@@9271@@
No. Verse 21 records the practice of a foreign king; it is a description, not an approval. The whole point of the text is that God is in control of that fork in the road, and that even when a king who did not know him shook the arrows, he was there. Elsewhere the Bible clearly forbids seeking guidance through divination (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). So this passage cannot be used to encourage anyone to cast lots, read livers, or ask the stars. If you face a fork in the road in life, you can pray like this: Lord, I do not know which way to go, please show me; I am willing to listen to your word, and willing to ask the Bible and wise brothers and sisters.
@@9272@@
When the people of Judah heard this, they found it laughable. Verse 23 says, “It will be to them as a false divination in their sight, who have sworn oaths to them.” They had oaths, they had covenants, they had the temple, and one sentence in their hearts: it won’t be us. But a covenant has two sides; they were the first to break it, and then they used the covenant as an amulet. A certain kind of security is like a borrowed umbrella: you feel most at ease when you leave the house, and only when the heavy rain comes do you find it is broken.
@@9273@@
Verses 25 and 26 turn to the king of Israel: “whose day has come, in the time of the iniquity of the end. Remove the turban, and take off the crown. This will not be as it was. Exalt that which is low, and humble that which is high.” Who this refers to differs from one interpreter to another; most read it as the last king of Judah, and some read it more widely. The text does not give a name, and we do not apply it to anyone alive today. What we want to look at is the picture: the crown is not one’s own, so it can be taken off. In one family, after the father passed away, the head seat at the dinner table stayed empty for three years and no one dared to sit in it. Later one of the sons sat down in it, and from that day the tone in which he spoke to the whole family changed. A position quietly changes the one who holds it.
@@9274@@
The “overturn, overturn, overturn” of verse 27 sounds like a sentence with no way out. But the sentence is not finished: “This also will be no more, until he comes whose right it is; and I will give it.” The throne will not be empty forever. Who is the “one whose right it is”? Many interpreters read him as the Messiah, and some read him as a future king after God’s own heart. The judgment of these notes is this: read within the whole book, this sentence points to the shepherd God will set up (34:23); and readers of the New Testament see in Jesus the Lord who took the sword in his own body, and who is worthy of the crown. This is hope, not a timetable.
@@9275@@
Finally, verses 28 to 32 turn to the Ammonites. The sword appears again: “A sword! A sword is drawn!” Verse 30 says: “Cause it to return into its sheath.” The reading of this line is disputed: some read it as God speaking to the sword, others as speaking to the Ammonites. We do not add conclusions the text does not state, because the end of this verse is still judgment: “Your blood will be in the middle of the land.” We see only one thing: where there is a drawing out, there is a sheathing; the owner of the sword is also the one who decides when the sword rests.
@@9276@@
The first deviation is using chapter 21 on people who meet disaster, illness, or bereavement and saying: this is God’s judgment, you must be guilty. This chapter is spoken to a city that again and again refused to turn back; it is not spoken to a suffering person. The test is simple: after listening, was he accompanied, or condemned? Let the suffering first be accompanied.
@@9277@@
The second deviation is preaching “cut off from you the righteous and the wicked” in verse 3 as God not telling good from bad, or as standing against chapter 18. Read together with chapter 18, there are different understandings; 18:23 and 18:32 say God takes no pleasure in anyone’s death. We should honestly set the different views side by side, and also let the congregation hear that God takes no pleasure in anyone’s death, without adding conclusions the text does not state.
@@9278@@
The third deviation is enlarging the pictures of sword, fire, and blood to frighten the congregation. Chapter 21 is a poem and a warning, not a horror film. We want people to love the Lord more, not to fear more; if someone has experienced violence, the sermon must first protect him.
@@9279@@
The fourth deviation is preaching the divination of the king of Babylon as proof that divination is reliable, or encouraging people to cast lots, read livers, or ask the stars. The text says God is in control of the fork in the road; it does not affirm divination. We want people to be more willing to inquire of God and his word, not to depend more on omens.
@@9280@@
The fifth deviation is using verses 25 to 27 to point to a modern leader or a particular country, or to draw up a timetable, or preaching “overturn, overturn, overturn” as God hating people and never giving hope. The “one whose right it is” has a Messianic reading and other readings as well; setting them side by side is enough, and the text goes straight on to say: until he comes whose right it is. We want people to see the overturning, and also the giving.
@@9281@@
Who exactly is “the one whose right it is”? Can I say directly that it is Jesus?
@@9282@@
You can say so, but say honestly that this is one reading, not the only one. Many interpreters read him as the Messiah, and some read him as a future king after God’s own heart. These notes place it within the hope of the whole book: God will set up a shepherd himself. Readers of the New Testament see this fulfilled in Jesus. When you preach, watch two things: first, do not name any person or country today; second, do not draw up a timetable. The point is hope: the crown is taken off so that it may be given to the Lord who truly has the right to it. This hope is more solid than any calculation, and it can also make people wait more quietly.
@@9283@@
The first question: we are very used to giving an explanation at once in front of someone else’s disaster, and telling people to be strong. When we hear of illness, a funeral, or bankruptcy, we quickly say: this is God’s will, there must be a good purpose, you must have faith, don’t cry. The intention is good, but the prophet of 21:6 was bent over, sighing bitterly, and God did not tell him to dry his tears first. You can begin like this: the next time someone tells you bad news, do not speak at first; sit down and stay with him for three minutes; when he asks your opinion, then open your mouth.
@@9284@@
The second question: we are very used to defining ourselves by “position.” Eldest son, eldest daughter-in-law, director, deacon, boss, teacher; we take the words on our name card to be ourselves. Some of these positions are given by God and some by people, but verse 26 reminds us: the crown can be taken off, and the high can be made low. You can examine yourself like this: write down the title you value most, and beside it write one sentence: whose place am I holding this for? If the answer is “for myself,” today you can say to God: this is not my crown; it is yours.
@@9285@@
In a Chinese family, the elders’ position cannot be moved. Does this conflict with “the crown will be taken off”?
@@9286@@
No. The Bible does not tell us to dishonor our parents, and it does not tell us to disrespect office. Chapter 21 speaks of something else: it becomes dangerous when people take a position as their own, something God may not touch. Respecting elders and acknowledging that “this position is entrusted by God” can both be true at the same time. You can do this: at home, not by grabbing positions, but, in the position that carries the most weight, first acknowledge that you hold it for God, and before you speak ask: am I saying this to glorify myself, or to build up my family? When elders live this way, the younger ones can see it, and this is the best honor.
@@9287@@
VII. Living Out 321: Give the Crown Back, and Let the One Whose Right It Is Be King
@@9288@@
Jesus is my Role Model. As he drew near to Jerusalem, he saw the city and wept over it (Luke 19:41). He did not mock, he did not gloat; he wept first. The prophet in chapter 21 who bent over and sighed is a foreshadowing of this very heart. Those who follow Jesus, facing judgment and disaster, learn from him to weep first and not to draw conclusions first.
@@9289@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 21 not to find out who should be struck by the sword, but to let God’s word face us first: do I hold a crown that I think will never be taken off? The standard of verse 27 is: after the overturning there is a giving; people’s positions will be turned over, but God’s promise will not.
@@9290@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lets a person bend down before someone else’s suffering, and it is also the Holy Spirit who lets a person loosen his grip in his own position. Every day, take a quiet moment and ask: Lord, what am I holding on to most tightly today?
@@9291@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is acknowledging that the crown is not mine (verses 26 and 27). What I hand over is control, reputation, and the sense of security that “my position will not fall.” A person who lets Jesus be King does not need to guard the throne by himself, because the one who truly has the right to it is already on the throne.
@@9292@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. Verse 27 says the kingdom will be given to the one whose right it is. All the glory goes to him, not to our offices, achievements, and reputations. If we are used, let us not say “I got to this position”; if we are taken off, let us not say “I have lost everything,” because we never held it for ourselves.
@@9293@@

<p><strong>אִישׁ לִזְרֹעוֹ——What does “each according to his power” mean?</strong></p><ul><li>Each relies on his own arm (strength)</li><li>Each takes his own arm as his measure and acts according to his own strength</li><li>“Each to the limit of his arm”: as much as he can do, he does</li></ul><p>The three readings point the same way: power without brakes.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: each by his own strength.</strong> The role of “prince” was originally to protect the weak; now their strength is used only for their own hands. <p><strong>The way God audits this city: first look at those with the most power, then at those with the least.</strong></p><p>Verse 6 begins with the princes: “each according to his power,” each acting by the strength of his own arm (זְרוֹעַ), for the purpose of shedding blood. Power without brakes is the starting point of the whole list.</p><p>Then the camera moves down, all the way: parents treated with contempt, the foreigner oppressed, the fatherless and the widow wronged. These three kinds of people are a group God specially protected in the law of Moses (as in Exod 22:21–22). How a city treats them is how God measures the city.</p><p>Notice the small word that repeats again and again: בָּךְ, “in you.” Every one of these things happened inside the city; no one was far away. The city is not a passing victim; she is the scene.</p><p>The next item on the list is verse 8: “You have despised my holy things, and have profaned my Sabbaths.” This is vertical, toward God. Horizontal injustice and vertical irreverence are not two matters but one. When people do not treat God as God, they will not treat people as people.</p><p>Landing: the true condition of a community is seen not in what is said from its platform but in how it treats those with the least power. If you were once the one looked down on and wronged, these words are not for making you turn back and examine yourself; they are God’s testimony standing on your side: he saw, and he wrote it down.</p>

<p><strong>The last item on the list is not murder and not adultery; it is: “and have forgotten me.”</strong></p><p>Verses 9 to 12 are a very long list: slanderers, those who take bribes to shed blood, those who eat at idol feasts, those who hurt people in the most private places of the family, those who lend at interest. The text puts these together not to add shock but to explain one thing: this city’s sin has gone from the street into the home, into the money pouch.</p><p>Verses 10 and 11 touch the most hidden and most wounded matters inside the family. We do not enlarge those pictures and we do not add comments to them. Only this: the people who were harmed are not the ones the text accuses; these words are God speaking for them.</p><p>Then verse 12, a string of greed: bribes, interest, seizing, and the final sentence: “and have forgotten me.” The original שָׁכַחַתְּ is second person feminine: you, this city, forgot me.</p><p>This is the bottom of the whole list. The horizontal acts have a vertical root: forgetting God. People remember money, remember face, remember who owes whom, and just do not remember the God who watches all of it.</p><p>Landing: forgetting is usually not one decision but thinking of him a little less each day. There is a very small practice: before every decision, pause for one second and ask “is he watching?” Not out of fear, but in order to remember.</p>
