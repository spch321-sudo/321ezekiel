@@5028@@
My heart is very hard. Has God not yet changed me? How should I pray about this?
@@5029@@
The subject of verse 19 is God: I will give, I will put, I will take out, I will give. So you do not have to soften your own heart first before you come to him. Numbness, or not crying easily, is sometimes the protection that pain has left behind. It is not the same as stubbornness, and it is not a verdict on your character. You can pray in the shortest words: Lord, my heart is hard; please give. Then keep reading Scripture and meeting with others as usual, and notice the day when you suddenly feel something about a small matter. That is the heart of flesh beginning to move. And if for a long time you feel empty inside and cannot feel anything at all, please also find a pastor or counselor to walk with you.
@@5030@@
4. Light from the Original Language: Cauldron, Heart of Flesh, Stood (11:3, 19, 23)
@@5031@@
This chapter has three Hebrew words worth pausing over. The first is the cauldron, the Hebrew סִיר (sir). In verse 3 they say, this city is the cauldron. It is a big pot for cooking, with a fire under it. A cauldron was never for protecting meat; it is for cooking meat. They took something made for cooking and treated it as something to shield them. In daily life: that thing you are leaning on, what was it originally designed for? A job is designed so that you can serve people; a title is designed for service, not for hiding behind. Put it back in its designed place, and it will no longer be your cauldron.
@@5032@@
The second is the heart, the Hebrew לֵב (lev). The stony heart and the heart of flesh in verse 19 are both לֵב, one paired with אֶבֶן (stone), the other with בָּשָׂר (flesh). In Hebrew the heart is not only feelings; it is also the center of thought and will. The interesting thing is that בָּשָׂר is the very “meat” of verse 3: they said, we are the meat in the cauldron; God said, what I will give you is a heart of flesh. The same word, two outcomes: the meat that thought the cauldron would protect it will be cooked; the heart of flesh that God gives will live. In daily life: a stone does not hurt and does not respond, but a heart of flesh does, and that is why he will give it. If your heart aches over something today, that is good, not weakness.
@@5033@@
The third is stood, the Hebrew וַיַּעֲמֹד (vayya‘amod). Verse 23 says the glory stood on the mountain east of the city. The root of this word, עָמַד, means to stand, to take a stand. The glory did not flee; it stood. He first went up, and then stood on the mountain, facing the city. This verb does not say he turned his back. In daily life: when you feel that he has left, remember that the God who has departed is still standing, still watching.
@@5034@@
Put the three words together, and you have the journey of this chapter: the cauldron (סִיר) is what people use to protect themselves, the heart of flesh (לֵב בָּשָׂר) is what God gives with his own hand, and standing (עָמַד) is the posture of a God who, even as he leaves, still keeps watch. What people make for themselves is a cauldron; what he gives is a heart; and when he leaves, he is also standing, not turning away.
@@5035@@
The “flesh” in a heart of flesh, and the Bible’s saying that those who are in the flesh cannot please God: do they contradict each other?
@@5036@@
They do not contradict each other, because the two “flesh” do not mean the same thing. The heart of flesh in verse 19 is a soft heart that feels and responds, in contrast to a dead heart made of stone. The Hebrew בָּשָׂר is originally a neutral word, referring to a person of flesh and blood, and it does not carry the meaning of sin. The “in the flesh” of the New Testament refers to people who live by the sinful nature, which is another matter. So there is no need to mix the two passages: God is not giving you a heart that belongs to the sinful nature, but a heart that is alive and that he can touch. This is the same word used differently in different contexts, and we simply read it according to its context. If you have questions when you read it elsewhere, you can ask a pastor, and slowly sort it out.
@@5037@@
The first deviation is to teach “this city will not be your cauldron” in 11:7 as the general idea that God will protect you and nothing will happen to you. The cauldron here is people putting their security in the place of the city and the temple; God’s word takes away this false sense of security, and does not promise that you will never meet any disaster. We want the congregation to trust him more, not to be more sure that they have a cauldron shielding them.
@@5038@@
The second deviation is to teach that of the two ways of reading 11:3, only one is right. Some read it as “The time is not near to build houses,” and some read it as a question: “Is the time not near to build houses?” The Hebrew has no verb here, both readings can stand, and those who take different views are companions. Both readings point to the same thing: treating the city as a charm for protection. We want the congregation to learn to read Scripture humbly, not to rank each other over a tone of voice.
@@5039@@
The third deviation is to teach the death of Pelatiah in 11:13 as meaning that people who offend God will drop dead. The text records his death in only one sentence and gives no reason; we do not add details, and we do not use one person’s death as a classroom demonstration. If someone present has lost a loved one, we protect them first, and do not ask them to be strong. We want grieving people to be accompanied, not frightened.
@@5040@@
The fourth deviation is to use the new heart and new spirit of verse 19 to scold the weak: how come you still have not changed your heart? The new heart is his gift; give, put, take out, give: the subject of each is him. Numbness and sorrow are sometimes the protection of pain, not stubbornness. We want people to know more that they need him, not to blame themselves more for not trying hard enough.
@@5041@@
The fifth deviation is to teach those who stayed in the city and those taken into exile as if one group were more spiritual than the other. The inhabitants said, “Go far away from Yahweh,” but God said that he had removed them far off, and that he would be a sanctuary to them there. Do not stick the label “far from God” on any group, and do not take “I am still standing here” as evidence that God is with me. We want the congregation to be more considerate of one another, not to rank higher and lower.
@@5042@@
The first issue: we value position very much. The title on a name card, our rank in the family, our role in the church often become our cauldron. I am so-and-so’s son, I have been at this company twenty years, I have served in the church thirty years. These are not bad words, but when they become the source of our security, and one day they are taken away, a person feels as if the foundation has been pulled out from under him. Verse 3 of chapter 11 gives us a very concrete self-check: take a sheet of paper and write: if I did not have this title, what would I have left? When you have finished, you do not have to show it to anyone. Just say to God: This is my cauldron; please give me a heart of flesh.
@@5043@@
The second issue: in front of grief, we are used to saying be strong, hold back your sorrow, don’t think too much, and then, once the business is done, we never mention it again. The prophet in 11:13 did the opposite: he fell on his face and cried out with a loud voice. Scripture allows this posture. If someone near you is grieving, there is no need to hurry to advise him; just sit beside him for a while and say: I am here. If you yourself are grieving, do not force yourself to listen to any great teaching, and when necessary ask a pastor to walk with you, and you may also seek the help of a professional counselor.
@@5044@@
In our church people often tell the grieving to be strong. Is it wrong to say that?
@@5045@@
It is not necessarily wrong; the mistake is in the timing. The three words “be strong,” said at the very moment a person has just fallen, sound like “please don’t cry here.” When the prophet fell on his face and cried out loudly, God did not tell him to get up and be strong; his word came only after he had fallen. You can accompany someone this way: first sit quietly; ask what he needs most right now, rather than asking whether he can look on the bright side; a few days later, send a short greeting. Only when the other person is willing, read Scripture together. Hope is not for stopping up tears; it grows slowly beside the tears.

7. Living Out 321: Take Peace Out of the Cauldron
@@5047@@
Jesus is my Role Model. As he drew near to Jerusalem and saw the city, he wept over it. He never used “I am still here” to set himself above anyone. The prophet, after hearing the judgment, first fell on his face and cried out; I want to be like him: when I speak the truth, I speak it with tears, not with a cold face.
@@5048@@

@@5049@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. The new spirit of verse 19 is something he puts in; I do not earn it by trying hard. Each day I stop for a moment and ask the Holy Spirit: In what matter today have I again put my security in the cauldron? He does not guide me by condemning me, but by giving me a heart that can feel.
@@5050@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness means laying down the sense of security that I have a cauldron to shelter me, and putting my peace back in his hands. To let Jesus be King is not for me to work harder at protecting myself, but for me to admit: position, seniority and status are not my backing; he is.
@@5051@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The heart of flesh is his gift; I cannot say that I finally changed my own heart. Obedience is something he brings about; I cannot say that I did well. The glory goes to the Lord who said, “I will give,” and I keep none of it for myself.
@@5052@@
To build God’s system. Verse 20 says: they will be my people, and I will be their God. The root of the system is not walls and a temple, but a united heart and a new spirit. First life, then relationships, then ministry: first a new heart, then obedience, then a group of people who accompany one another.
@@5053@@
What does letting Jesus be King have to do with taking peace out of the cauldron?
@@5054@@
The connection is very direct. The cauldron is the king I have arranged for myself: it protects me, supplies me, and backs me up. To let Jesus be King is to admit that this seat is not mine, and not any thing’s. This does not mean you must throw away your job, your connections and your seniority right away, but that you no longer rely on them to prove that you are safe. You can do one very small practice each day: in the morning tell him, what I most want to hold on to today is this; in the evening tell him, what I most feared losing today was this. After a while you will find that your hand loosens a little, and the peace he gives comes in a little more.

<p><strong>A phenomenon worth noting: having, but not using.</strong></p><p>עֵינַיִם לָהֶם לִרְאוֹת וְלֹא רָאוּ: “Eyes belong to them, for seeing, yet they have not seen.” The first half says they have the ability to see; the second half says that ability is not being used. Jer 5:21 has an almost identical accusation, and so does Isa 6:9–10.</p>
@@5136@@
<p><strong>It is not that they cannot see, but that they will not see.</strong></p><p>The prophet has just come down from the visions of chapters 8 to 11 and returned to the crowd of exiles. The first diagnosis God gives him is not “they lack information,” but “they have eyes and ears, yet do not use them.” The word is מְרִי, rebellion, an attitude of “talking back.”</p><p>Notice a small preposition: “you dwell in the ‘middle’ of the rebellious house,” בְּתוֹךְ. The prophet is not standing on high and accusing; he lives among them. God did not send an outsider to speak; he put his word in a man who was also in exile.</p><p>For the next thirty-some verses, God will try a different way. If words do not get in, he will make them see.</p><p>In daily life: we often think that what people lack is information, and that if we give a few more lessons and a few more proofs, they will believe. The text says that sometimes what is lacking is willingness, and no amount of talk can wake someone who has decided not to look. What we can do then is first to examine ourselves: Do I too live in the middle of them, with some places where my own eyes refuse to look?</p>

<p><strong>When words do not get in, make them see.</strong></p><p>“In their sight,” לְעֵינֵיהֶם, appears seven times in verses 3 to 7. The prophet’s whole action has only one point: to be seen. By day, he carries the baggage out like someone about to go into exile; at night, he digs a hole in the wall and carries things out through the hole, with his face covered.</p><p>This is a performance. His body is the sermon of the moment.</p><p>A word is hidden in the middle: אוּלַי, “perhaps.” God knows these people are rebellious, yet as he speaks to the prophet he leaves a “perhaps”: perhaps they will see. He has no certainty to fall back on, but he has a hope he cannot let go of.</p><p>There is also a cost. What the prophet is to do looks ugly in people’s eyes: covering his face, tunneling through a wall, carrying a bundle, like a madman. The word “sign” means that he himself becomes a mark, not only a mouth that speaks.</p><p>In daily life: is the way you live the part that others can see? Some people cannot take in your words, but they will keep watching you. Your days are the sermon you preach.</p>

<p><strong>A phenomenon worth noting: the report is almost a copy of the command, but with one extra detail.</strong></p><p>After וָאַעַשׂ כֵּן, verse 7 restates the commands of verses 3–6 item by item, in the same order. What is added is בְּיָד, “with the hand”: the command says only “dig through,” and the action adds “with the hand.” Also, the “cover your face” of the command is not restated in verse 7.</p>
@@5142@@
<p><strong>The prophet did not discuss, did not protest; he took up his hand and dug through the wall.</strong></p><p>This verse has only one thing: he did it. By day he carried the baggage out; at night he dug a hole; in the dark, in their sight, he put the things on his shoulder. Every verb answers one of the earlier commands.</p><p>Look at one detail: “dug through the wall with my hand.” The command says only dig; in his report there is the extra “with my hand.” You can imagine it: a man in the dark, with his own hands, tearing down his own wall. It is not a comfortable picture, but it is what real obedience looks like.</p><p>There is also an order to notice. At this moment, no one has told him what any of this means. The explanation does not come until “the morning” (verse 8). He did first, and understood later.</p><p>Obedience does not always have to wait until we understand. He often gives an action first, and after a night, gives a sentence of explanation.</p><p>In daily life: has God asked you to do something you still cannot give a reason for? Not every such thing should be done at once; it must be tested against his word. But if it is his word, the one who does it first is often the first to receive the explanation.</p>

The second layer: “I am your sign: as I have done, so will it be done to them.” The prophet is a mirror, and his body is the letters of this sentence.</p><p>Look at the words in verse 10. “Prince” is נָשִׂיא, “one who is lifted up”; “burden” is מַשָּׂא, “load,” from the same root נשׂא, “to lift, to carry.” The one who was lifted high will have to carry the load himself. They were hoping Jerusalem would still be standing, but this sign says that even the people of Jerusalem will be taken captive.</p><p>In daily life: when someone asks you, “Why do you live this way?” your door has opened. Often a person does not first need an answer; he first needs something that makes him want to ask.</p>

<p><strong>“Brought to Babylon, yet he will not see the land”: how is that possible?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Agreeing with history:</strong> 2 Kgs 25:7 records that the king’s eyes were put out, and then he was brought to Babylon. <p><strong>He thought he was escaping, but he was walking into the net that God had long since spread.</strong></p><p>Whatever the prophet did, the prince does the same: carrying things in the dark, digging through the wall to go out, covering his face. Even the details are the same; only the main character is different.</p><p>Then God adds the most important sentence: “I will also spread my net on him.” It is his net, not the enemy’s net. The prince planned a small road in the night; God had already spread the net on the road.</p><p>The historical record agrees with this: 2 Kgs 25:4–7 says that on the night the city was breached, the king went out with his men by the gate near the garden, was overtaken in the plains of Jericho, and had his eyes put out at Riblah. The text does not name him, but the reader can tell.</p><p>“Not seeing” is one line from beginning to end: those who have eyes and will not see finally really see nothing at all.</p><p>In daily life: we also have our own “digging through the wall,” going around something we should face, looking in the night for a small road we think no one knows about. Ask yourself: Whom am I hiding from? In his net, hiding is useless; but the end of this net is not destruction, it is the sentence in verse 15, “they will know that I am Yahweh.”</p>

On the day you tell it, those days will not have been in vain.</p>

<p><strong>Even eating became testimony.</strong></p><p>After the prophet finished the first sign, God gave the second: eat with quaking, drink with trembling. The quietest, most ordinary action of a person’s day became the loudest announcement.</p><p>The words are heavy. “Quaking” is shaking, “trembling” is shivering, “fearfulness” is anxiety, “dismay” is desolation. The last two also appeared as a pair in 4:16; that was how they ate their bread and drank their water. Eating and drinking are the most direct signs of a person’s peace; when even eating and drinking cannot be peaceful, the city is truly unsettled.</p><p>God points out the cause: the violence of the people who live in the land. The land is “emptied,” emptied of what fills it.</p><p>But please be careful: this passage does not say that everyone today who cannot eat or sleep is under judgment. What the text speaks of is the consequence for a city corrupted by violence.</p><p>In daily life: if you are eating today in trembling, this passage is not rebuking you. Before you, the prophet already sat at this table on behalf of people, and tasted this taste. Your anxiety is no stranger to him.</p>

<p><strong>A phenomenon worth noting: דָּבָר is “word,” and also “thing.”</strong></p><p>Verse 23: וּדְבַר כָּל חָזוֹן, “the word of every vision,” which is also “the thing of every vision”; verse 25: אֲדַבֵּר דָּבָר וְיֵעָשֶׂה, “I will speak a word, and it will be done.” The same word shows that with him, what is spoken is what is done.</p><p>Also, the people in verse 22 said the vision “fails,” but his answer in verse 23 is not “it has not failed,” but “its word (its thing) is at hand.”</p>
@@5157@@
<p><strong>They had a saying that came out smoothly: the days are prolonged, and every vision fails.</strong></p><p>It was a proverb, מָשָׁל, the kind of saying that comes out without thinking. Its logic is simple: what he said has not come; if it has not come, it will not come. The length of time was taken as evidence.</p><p>God’s answer is not an argument; it is a declaration. He says: I will make this proverb cease. Then he replaces the sentence: the days are at hand, and the “word” of every vision will be fulfilled. The “word” דְּבַר is exactly the “thing.” With him, what is spoken is already being done.</p><p>Verse 25 has דָּבָר three times, like a chain: I speak a word; the word is done; no more delay.</p><p>Hab 2:3 also says: “Though it takes time, wait for it; because it will surely come. It won’t delay.” Delay does not mean failure.</p><p>In daily life: to turn “not yet fulfilled” into “will not be fulfilled” is the laziest theology of the human heart. What he has not yet done is not forgotten; his time is not our time. Waiting is not passive; it is refusing to let doubt be made into a catchy saying.</p>

<p><strong>A phenomenon worth noting: they speak of the prophet in the third person.</strong></p><p>הוּא חֹזֶה, הוּא נִבָּא: “He sees,” “he prophesies.” The house of Israel is not speaking to the prophet; they are discussing him among themselves, as if he were not there. To turn a person into “he” is to hold his words at a distance.</p><p>Also, the “כָּל” in לֹא תִמָּשֵׁךְ עוֹד כָּל דְּבָרַי together with the negative means “not a single one,” which the CUV renders “none.”</p>
@@5160@@
<p><strong>“That is for later,” this sentence is the safest.</strong></p><p>The people in verse 22 said: the vision has failed. The people in this verse have changed their line: the vision is true, but it is for a long time from now and has nothing to do with us today. The former is denial, the latter is putting off. Putting off is harder to notice than denial.</p><p>When they talk about the prophet they use “he”: what he sees, what he says. It is like talking behind the back of someone who is not there; the word is put far away, and the person is put far away too.</p><p>Yahweh’s answer says it once more: none of my words will be deferred any more. The same word מָשַׁךְ, “to draw out.” His word leaves no room for any single word to go off to “far away.”</p><p>This verse is not giving us a timetable, but a mirror: how we push the word we should hear now off to later.</p><p>In daily life: which word has he spoken to you today that you are saying “that is for later”? Forgiving someone, apologizing to someone, letting go of something. It is not for the future; it is for today.</p>
