@@409@@
IV. Wheels Within Wheels, the Throne, and Falling Down (1:15–28)
@@410@@
From verse 15 on, wheels appear beside the living creatures. The wheels looked like beryl in shape and color, a wheel within a wheel, able to go straight in all four directions without turning. Have you ever taken a taxi in a strange city? The driver says he could find the way with his eyes closed, and you still feel nervous. But these wheels are different: verse 18 says the rims of all four were full of eyes all around. This is a vehicle with no blind spot. For a people who fear most of all being forgotten, this is very real news: there is no corner that he has not seen.
@@411@@
Then comes verse 20, the key to this chapter: “Wherever the spirit was to go, they went. The spirit was to go there. The wheels were lifted up beside them; for the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels.” Why can the wheels move? Because the Spirit is in them. Verse 21 goes on: “When those went, these went. When those stood, these stood.” This is not a machine; a machine has no faces and no eyes. This is something alive, joined together, going and stopping as one, each facing the same direction.
@@412@@
The prophet’s eyes move upward, layer by layer. Above the heads of the living creatures is the likeness of an expanse, awesome to look at like crystal. The noise of the wings is like the noise of great waters, like the voice of the Almighty. Have you ever heard a waterfall? Standing in front of one, you do not think of measuring the water. You simply stop. Verse 25 has a lovely detail: when they stood, they let down their wings. Even the living creatures, in his presence, let down their wings.
@@413@@
Finally he sees the highest place. Verse 26: “Above the expanse that was over their heads was the likeness of a throne, as the appearance of a sapphire stone. On the likeness of the throne was a likeness as the appearance of a man on it above.” The exiles’ eyes were usually fixed on city walls, on the guards, on tomorrow’s labor. But what he saw was this: the throne is still there. Jerusalem had already been broken into, the king had been carried away, hearts had collapsed, and everyone was asking: is God’s kingship over? This vision does not answer with words. It simply shows them: above everything, there is a throne.
@@414@@
Please notice how the text speaks: like a sapphire stone, like the appearance of a man, like glowing gold. He does not say “I saw God.” He says “like,” and he is very careful. As for who the “likeness of a man” is, there are different readings. Some say this is Yahweh’s glory showing itself, and the text itself says, “This was the appearance of the likeness of Yahweh’s glory,” without defining it further. Some look back from the New Testament and think that the one on the throne with a human form later became a man and lived among us. Others hold that we should stay with what the text says and not go one step beyond. The judgment of these notes is this: first stay with what the text says; if one humbly looks back through the New Testament, one may give a testimony, but we do not present that looking back as the only possible reading.
@@415@@
Verse 28 is the high point of the whole chapter, and also its order: “This was the appearance of the likeness of Yahweh’s glory. When I saw it, I fell on my face, and I heard a voice of one that spoke.” See the order: first he sees, then he falls down, and only then does he hear. Before he fell down, what he heard was the noise of wings, like great waters, like an army. After he fell down, he heard one speaking to him. Glory is not only to make people afraid. Glory makes people grow quiet, and then they listen to him speak.
@@416@@
There is a kind of person who holds on for a very long time. Holding up a family, holding up a job, holding up a ministry, not daring to fall down, for fear that once down they will not get up again. But Ezekiel fell down. Who made him stand up again, we will say in the next chapter. Today, learn only this one thing: let glory be glory first, let God be God first, and let me be first of all a person lying on the ground.
@@417@@
Can we say that the “likeness of a man” on the throne is Jesus?
@@418@@
This is a place where different traditions read differently. Some say the text only calls it “the likeness of Yahweh’s glory,” and they stop there. Others look back through the New Testament and see that the one on the throne with a human form later became a man and lived among us. The position of these notes is this: first stay with what the text says; if someone humbly testifies that in Jesus we recognize this one, that is also acceptable, as long as we do not present it as the only way to read it. What matters more is the prophet’s response: as soon as he saw it, he fell on his face. If you cannot work it out for the moment, please fall down first anyway. God will not stop listening to you because your theology is not enough.
@@419@@
V. Several Mistakes We Must Recognize
@@420@@
The first mistake is to turn the four living creatures and the wheels into a diagram that can be drawn and measured. The text itself keeps saying “like” and “as it were,” using human words to speak of what human words cannot reach. When the congregation leaves, we want them to remember glory and falling down, not a drawing.
@@421@@
The second mistake is to force the four faces to match four particular things and call that the only correct answer. Each tradition has its own view, and the text does not explain. Lay out the different views, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. We want the congregation to read more humbly, not to argue more.
@@422@@
The third mistake is to argue over details such as “thirty years” or “the likeness of a man,” until they cover what this chapter is saying. What this chapter says is: the heavens were opened, the throne is still there, and a man fell down. The time spent on controversy should not exceed the time spent on the words “the heavens were opened.”
@@423@@
The fourth mistake is to say to someone who is suffering: “See, God is in the land of exile, so you should be thankful.” Exile is not a good thing, and suffering is not a good thing. The text only says that he comes, and nothing can stop him; it does not say this situation is something to be glad about. We want those who suffer to feel seen, not to be told to be happy.
@@424@@
The fifth mistake is to teach “falling down” as putting on a show of piety, as if weeping on the floor were what makes one spiritual. Ezekiel’s falling down was the natural response after he saw the glory; it is not a technique that can be trained. One minute of quietly bowing the head is enough. We want the congregation to love the Lord more, not to perform better.
@@425@@
The speaker says “one minute of quietly bowing the head is enough.” Is that too light?
@@426@@
It is not too light. Ezekiel’s falling down was a natural response after seeing; it was not something he produced. We cannot push ourselves to that point by performing. So the point of a one-minute bow is not its length but its honesty: I admit that I am not the one who holds everything up. If one day you truly weep or truly fall down because you have seen God, that is not a shape you were asked to learn. You can begin in the smallest way: once a day, in a fixed place, bow your head and say one sentence. Over time, this small action becomes a habit and slowly softens your heart.
@@427@@
VI. Two Real Questions in a Chinese Context
@@428@@
The first question: we are used to thinking, “I must be in the right place to draw near to God properly.” Many people have left their familiar church and their old pastor for work, study, or caring for family, and in their hearts there is often one sentence: when I am settled, I will draw near to God properly. But the bank of the Chebar was the least settled place there could be, and the heavens were opened right there. You can begin like this: this evening, right where you are sitting, bow your head for one minute, say the name of the place where you are, and then say to God: Lord, I am here.
@@429@@
The second question: we are used to thinking, “I must hold on; I must not fall.” The eldest son in the family, the backbone of the workplace, the co-workers in the church have all learned one thing: weakness must be hidden, and falling down means losing. So we stand very straight, and we stand very tired. Ezekiel’s falling down is not failure; it is what is real after seeing the glory. This week, find a place where no one can see you, put your burden down for one minute, and say to God: I cannot hold on any more; you come. If you have held on until you cannot sleep or eat, please also talk with a pastor or a trusted companion, and seek professional help if needed.
@@430@@
I have always been the pillar of my family. Can I really put it down and stop holding on?
@@431@@
The “putting down” here is not irresponsibility; it is admitting: I am not the one who holds everything up. Ezekiel lay on the ground, but that did not mean he did nothing from then on; later he stood up and was sent out as a prophet. You can tell two things apart: what is mine to do, and what I actually cannot do but am forcing myself to hold up. Say the second kind out loud, and hand it to God and to one trustworthy person. If you worry, “once I put it down, I will collapse,” you can start with something very small, for example letting a family member share the cooking for one meal, or saying in your small group: I have been very tired lately. If the tiredness is affecting your sleep, your emotions, or your health, please seek professional help.
@@432@@
VII. Living Out 321: See the Glory, Fall Down, and Then Walk Together
@@433@@
Jesus is my Role Model. On the throne there is the likeness of a man, and looking back through the New Testament, we see the Lord who became a man. On earth too, wherever the Spirit went, he went; even on the most painful road he went straight ahead and did not turn back. What we learn is his pattern: not to look for another road around the place that hurts.
@@434@@
The Bible is my Standard. Ezekiel first fell down, and only then heard the voice of one who spoke (1:28). Our reading of Scripture has the same order: not first taking a verse to prove ourselves, but first bowing our head, and then listening. This week, read Ezekiel chapter 1; do not look for feeling, do not look to understand everything, only look to hear the one sentence that is spoken to you.
@@435@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. “Wherever the spirit was to go, they went” (1:12); the wheels could move because the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels (1:20). The direction of our life is not arranged by our own cleverness; it comes from the Spirit’s leading. Every morning ask one question: Lord, which thing today is the one where the Spirit wants me not to turn back?
@@436@@
Let Jesus be King. The throne is above the expanse and has never been overthrown (1:26). Letting Jesus be King does not begin with a great resolution; it begins by falling down: admitting that I am not the one who holds everything up, and that he is. Selflessness is not nothingness; it is that, after seeing the glory of that one, I naturally grow soft and enter the limitless.
@@437@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. As soon as the prophet saw “the appearance of the likeness of Yahweh’s glory,” he fell to the ground, and he did not pick up pen and paper to record credit for himself (1:28). If our service bears fruit, if we have stood up from the muddy bank, please do not say it was our ability; give the glory to the Lord who opened the heavens by the river.
@@438@@
To build God’s system. The spirit was in the wheels, and the living creatures and the wheels went and stood together, each facing the same direction (1:20–21). God’s system is not people doing things in a very orderly way; it is the Spirit being in it, and all the people and tasks following the Spirit. This is a community centered on the throne, not an organization centered on human ability; life first, then relationships, then ministry.
@@439@@
Why must “let Jesus be King” begin with falling down?
@@440@@
Because there is only one seat for the King. We often say with our mouths that Jesus is King, but in trouble our first reaction is to stand up ourselves and hold on. The order in chapter 1 is very clear: first see the throne, then fall down, then hear the voice speak, and only then be sent. If this order is reversed, we become people who manage the world on God’s behalf. You can practice this every morning: first say to Jesus, “What I most want to control today is this”; then say, “I put it back in your hands”; finally, wait quietly for a moment and see whether a word comes to you. This is reigning that begins with falling down.
@@441@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Heart, One Invitation
@@442@@
The proud person says: “I made it through my situation by myself; I do not need to fall down.” The text says to him: the throne is above the expanse, not on your shoulders. Pride is leaving man’s position, grasping God’s power, and wanting to take God’s place. You do not need to put everything down at once; today, first say one thing you are “holding up,” and hand it to him.
@@443@@
The fearful person says: “Such great glory; if I see it, will I be killed?” The prophet also fell to the ground, but after he fell, what he heard was a voice speaking to him, not destruction. This God of glory is surrounded by light and a rainbow, and the rainbow is the sign of the covenant. The fearful person can begin with one minute of bowing the head, and does not have to come near all at once.
@@444@@
The ashamed person says: “My situation is such a mess; how could God be here?” The bank of the Chebar was exactly a place like that, a mess, and the heavens were opened right there. Shame says “you do not deserve it”; the text says “the heavens were opened from above.” The ashamed person does not need to tidy himself up first; today he only needs to sit there and say one sentence: Lord, I am here.
@@445@@
The wounded person says: “I have lost my home, the temple, the gatherings. I do not want to believe in glory any more.” God does not rebuke this kind of wound. Ezekiel’s exile was not his choice; your loss is also not your fault alone. The wounded can slowly look for someone willing to sit with you for a while; the one who accompanies should not hurry to say “you must have faith,” but only say: I am here, I am with you.

X. Conclusion
@@459@@
Chapter 1 begins beside a river and ends with a man lying on the ground. At the beginning is an exiled priest, with no temple and no altar; in the middle are fire, wheels, eyes, and the Spirit; at the end is the throne above the expanse, and a man who fell down and heard one speaking. From the muddy bank to the throne, from exile to falling down, this is a journey of the heavens being opened.
@@460@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is not bound inside the temple, and that he comes among the exiles. God wants us to see that the throne is still there, and that wherever his Spirit goes, everything goes together. God also wants us to learn to fall down first in his presence. The Lord on the throne, with the likeness of a man, we know in Jesus Christ; he came among us, and he also sits with us on the muddy bank.
@@461@@
So please remember the three main points of today’s lesson: the heavens opened, the Spirit leads the way, and a man fell down. The focus of this lesson is not how far away your situation is, not whether you can understand every wheel, and not whether you have the strength to stand, but Jesus, who sits on the throne and appears to those in exile.
@@462@@
Lord, thank you; you opened the heavens by our River Chebar. Forgive us for thinking you stayed far away, and forgive us for standing with all our strength, holding everything up. May your Spirit lead us, so that we do not turn back but go straight ahead; let us see your glory, fall down, and also hear your voice. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ. Amen.

@@507@@
Glory makes a person fall down, and then what? Many people, after seeing God deeply, find they know even less how to get up: the legs will not obey, the strength is spent, and they are still to be sent to face a people who will not listen. Ezekiel chapter 2 gives a very concrete answer: God first says to the man lying on the ground, “Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you,” and then the Spirit enters him and sets him on his feet. The place he sends him to has briers, thorns, and scorpions, and the result is not guaranteed either; he only says, “whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse.” But at the same time he says, do not be afraid, and he puts a scroll into the prophet’s mouth. Standing up, being sent, eating first: all of it is his doing.
@@508@@
Standing up is his word together with his Spirit; being sent does not depend on people’s response; before speaking, one eats first, so that God’s word becomes one’s own food.
@@509@@
Ezekiel lay on the ground and heard God call him “son of man” and tell him to stand on his feet, and the Spirit entered him and set him on his feet. God tells a person to stand, and also gives the Spirit to set a person on his feet; one who is sent must first listen and first eat, and only then speak.
@@511@@
Standing up is first God’s word and then God’s Spirit (his presence in the present tense); we do not need to pull ourselves up from the ground by our own strength.
@@512@@
Selflessness is not even owning the act of “standing up”: the command is his, the strength is his gift, and even the words to be spoken are received from his hand.
