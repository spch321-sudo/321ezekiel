<p><strong>How is “making the righteous sad” done?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>False judgment and curses:</strong> they speak of the righteous as if they were doomed to be punished, echoing verse 19, “to kill the souls who should not die.”</li><li><strong>False peace:</strong> they say “Peace” to the wicked, so that the righteous cannot see justice and lose heart.</li></ul><p>הַכְאוֹת (to discourage) and הִכְאַבְתִּיו (to cause pain) sound alike. One is done by “you,” the other is what “I have not” done. “Strengthening the hands of the wicked” is the same charge as in Jer 23:14.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best way to understand it:</strong> the two roads lead to the same result: the righteous are pressed down, and the wicked are lifted up. This reversal is the close of the whole chapter, and also its measuring line.</p></blockquote>
@@5526@@
<p><strong>Lies have two victims: one is pressed down, one is lifted up.</strong></p><p>This is the summary of the whole chapter. The first victim is the righteous person. His “heart,” לֵב, is crushed by the lies. It is the same word לֵב, and in 11:19 this is the very heart that God promised to replace. The second is the wicked person. His hand is strengthened, so he does not have to turn back, and is “saved alive.”</p><p>This is the “saving alive” that the false prophets speak of: living without having to turn back. “Saved alive” in the original means that he is kept alive, but is not asked to turn from his evil way. But God says that he takes pleasure in people turning and living (18:23 and 33:11, whose full text is elsewhere). True “saving alive” always passes through turning back.</p><p>In the middle of all this there is one very tender sentence: “I have not made the righteous sad.” He is the one who guards the heart of the righteous. He says it in the negative, as if to say: this was not my doing, this is not how I treat my people.</p><p>And last comes verse 23: he will deliver. He does not only pull down the wall of lies; he rescues people out of those hands.</p><p>Landing it: check your own words. Do they make the one walking in the light stronger, or do they make the one going his own way more at ease? The authority to save alive and to make the righteous sad was never in human hands. When we let God be God, man has room to be man.</p>

I. The Prophet Is to Prophesy Against Prophets (13:1–7)
@@5769@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. On your phone, is there a family group chat where, when you wake up in the morning, a forwarded message is lying there? “God told me: whoever forwards this to ten people today will be greatly blessed; whoever doesn’t forward it should be careful this month.” And behind it, a picture of folded hands. I know a sister who read this message and did not sleep all night. It was not that she did not believe; she was afraid: what if it is true? I asked her: who wrote this message? She said she did not know, it had already been forwarded through many hands. I said: then what you are afraid of, is it God, or a person with no name? She was stunned for a moment and did not answer. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 13, and it asks exactly this question: that “God says” you heard, who said it?
@@5770@@
Verse 1 is very short: Yahweh’s word came to me, saying. Please notice that this chapter begins with “word,” not a vision, and not a feeling. In verse 2 God told Ezekiel to prophesy against the prophets of Israel who prophesy. A prophet is to speak heavy words to prophets. What is even more interesting is that the first command God gave them was not “Be quiet,” but “Hear Yahweh’s word.” What they lacked was not eloquence, nor zeal, but listening.
@@5771@@
Verse 3 says: Woe to the foolish prophets, who follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing. “Woe” is the wail at a funeral. God does not begin with anger, but with weeping, as if watching a man walk along the edge of a cliff while loudly telling others that it is safe ahead. The root of their sickness is “following their own spirit”: the words grow out of their own heart, and then one more sentence is added, “Yahweh says,” and they become the word of God. We must say honestly that this is often not deliberate deceit. Most of the time they want something to happen too much, or fear the tears of the person in front of them too much, and so they dress their own wish in God’s clothing.
@@5772@@
God says, “I have not sent them.” So how do I know whether the sentence I just said was sent by God?
@@5773@@
The text does not give us a test, but it gives several directions. First, look at the source: did this sentence come from hearing, or from my own heart (verses 2 and 3)? Second, look at the use: is this sentence to protect people, or to make myself comfortable (verse 5 says a true prophet stands in the breach)? Third, look at the effect: do the hearers come closer to God, or depend more on me (Ezekiel 13:22)? Before you say “the Lord says,” you can pause for three seconds and ask yourself: can I point to which verse of Scripture this sentence comes from? If you cannot, honestly say: this is my own view, please take it to prayer and measure it by Scripture. This is not shameful, this is reverence.
@@5774@@
Please also look at the four words “have seen nothing.” They said “I saw it,” and said it the loudest, but God said: you have seen nothing at all. Those who truly see are those who are taken to see. Ezekiel was gripped by God’s hand and lifted by the Spirit; he did not go looking for visions, the visions found him. So the foolish prophets turn the order upside down: it is not that God took them to see, but that they took their own ideas and went to speak for God. Today we too can easily do this: we first reach a conclusion, and then go back to find a verse to put a stamp on it.
@@5775@@
In verses 4 and 5, God drew a fox. A fox in the waste places is most at home in the ruins: when the wall falls, it is not sad, because the breach is just its door. What a true prophet should do is go up into the gaps and rebuild the wall for the people, so that they can stand in the day of battle. Standing in the breach costs something, because the breach is where the sword arrives first, and the one who stands there often says what people do not love to hear. But these people did not stand in the breach; they stood in the applause. Verses 6 and 7 put it most plainly: they say Yahweh says, but Yahweh has not sent them. Please turn the mirror around and look at yourself first: have I ever said “God told me” when it was really something I wanted badly? A brother told me that when he was young he advised a couple: “I feel that God says this is the one.” Two years later the two ended in pain, and he went to their door to apologize, saying: I was not trying to deceive you then, I just liked you too much. From then on he learned one sentence: this is my own view, please pray about it again.

II. The Whitewash on the Wall (13:8–16)
@@5777@@
When I was young I worked on a building site and knew an old plasterer. He said that the plaster for a wall has to be mixed through with water and left overnight, and only the next day can it be used. Those in a hurry think that is too slow and just stir it a bit and put it on. He said: when it first goes on, it looks exactly the same, white and smooth; but after one rain, it spots, and falls off piece by piece. At the time I thought it was only a craftsman’s rule. Reading this chapter, I understood that it is a very heavy picture.
@@5778@@
In verse 8, the way God speaks changes: I am against you. In the original it is “Behold, I.” He steps out personally and stands across from them. Why is it so heavy? Verse 10 says, because they seduced my people, saying: Peace! and there is no peace. The people built a wall, and the prophets did not reinforce it, but only plastered it over with whitewash that had not been mixed through. “Peace” is one of the most beautiful words in the Old Testament, meaning complete, lacking nothing. But here, this word is used as paint.
@@5779@@
In verses 11 to 14, rainstorm, hailstones, and stormy wind, layer by layer, until the foundation is laid bare. Verse 12 has a very quiet question: when the wall has fallen, won’t it be said to you, “Where is the plaster with which you have plastered it?” This is not a roar; it is someone under the wall lifting up his head to ask one thing: what did you say to me back then? Verse 15 says the wall is no more, neither those who plastered it. Please notice that those who plaster the wall are not standing outside the wall watching; they are with the wall. The one who covers others with lies ends up covering himself too.
@@5780@@
There was a sister whose husband died, and on the third day the people from the church came to visit one after another, each with one sentence: “He is better off in heaven,” “This is God’s good will,” “You must be strong.” Later she told me that in those three days she heard dozens of sentences, and not one stayed with her. Only one old sister said nothing, came in, sat down beside her, and spent an afternoon shelling beans with her. She said: that was the first time I felt I could breathe. You see, truly holding someone up sometimes needs no whitewash, only a pair of hands willing to stay.
@@5781@@
Verse 16 says that those who plaster the wall are the prophets of Israel. Some, reading this far, become afraid of all comfort. That is a misunderstanding. God himself is the God of comfort. So where is the difference? True comfort is willing to look at the crack; it says: I see it, this crack is deep, I am with you. False comfort is in a hurry to paint; it says: it’s fine, don’t think about it. True comfort stays and waits together; false comfort says its piece and leaves. However great God’s anger, it is for the sake of the people leaning against the wall. His anger is for people.
@@5782@@
I often comfort others by saying “It will be all right” or “God has his good purpose.” Is that also a coat of white paint?
@@5783@@
Not necessarily. These two sentences are not wrong in themselves; it depends on how and when you say them. You can tell by three things. First, did you first see his wound, or were you in a hurry to end the conversation? Second, does what you say have a root in Scripture, or is it only “I hope so”? Third, after you have spoken, do you stay, or leave? If he is in pain, the most honest sentence is often only: “I don’t know why. I’m here.” That is not paint, it is a shoulder. When he is ready to listen, slowly read Scripture to him. You do not need to be afraid to comfort people because of this chapter; you only need to build your comfort on God’s word.
@@5784@@
III. The Women Who Sewed Pillows (13:17–23)
@@5785@@
In verse 17, the camera turns to a group of women. Please see clearly: the charge is not that they are women; the charge is exactly the same as before: prophesying out of their own heart. God’s accusation against them is “to hunt souls.” They sewed pillows on all elbows and made kerchiefs for the heads of people of every stature, things that in that time carried the purpose of charms. “Hunt” is a hunter’s word, like spreading a net to catch birds or setting a snare for rabbits. Their method was terror and soothing: who would have disaster and who could have peace, they claimed to decide.
@@5786@@
Verse 19 says that all this was only for handfuls of barley and pieces of bread. Turning other people’s fear into your own business is not strange in any age. Whoever keeps you by manufacturing your fear, please ask one more question: why does he want me to be afraid? I know a preacher who, when he first believed, was frightened by a man who was “very effective.” The man said someone in his home would have an accident, and that he had to give regularly to keep it away. He said that in those years, every time the phone rang, his heart raced. Later it was an older believer who read Scripture with him verse by verse, and he slowly understood: God does not keep people by frightening them.
@@5787@@
Verse 22 is the most tender sentence in this chapter: with lies you have grieved the heart of the righteous, whom I have not made sad; and strengthened the hands of the wicked, that he should not return from his wicked way, and be saved alive. Look, lies have two victims. One is pressed down: the weary righteous, whose heart breaks when the lies come. The other is lifted up: the wicked, because someone told him: you don’t have to turn back. False words wound at both ends: they wound the one who should be held up, and comfort the one who should turn back.
@@5788@@
Please think about who that “weary righteous” person is. He may be a brother who has led a small group for ten years and no one has ever asked if he is tired; he may be a sister caring for a long-ill family member who has long had no strength left. They are already trying very hard, and if someone says to them one more time, “Your faith isn’t enough,” that sentence is an arrow. God says: I have not made the righteous sad. He sees them, and he stands on their side.
@@5789@@
In verses 20 and 21, God says: I am against your pillows, and I will tear them from your arms and let the souls go, those whom you hunt to make them fly. I will also tear your kerchiefs and deliver my people out of your hand. Those who are bound do not need to untie themselves first; God says: I will tear, I will set free. The same God who says “I am against you” to the lie, says to the deceived, “I will deliver my people.” What he opposes is the coat of paint; what he loves is the person behind the paint.
@@5790@@
If I was once frightened in the name of God, or bound by someone’s prophecy, can I still trust God now?
@@5791@@
Yes, and this passage was written for you. In verses 20 and 21, what God says is “I will tear,” “I will tear them,” “I will deliver my people”: he stands on the side of those who were hunted, and acts himself; he does not wait for you to break free first. You can take it slowly. First step: you do not need to say all the details now; just say one sentence to God: “Lord, I was frightened; please come and rescue me yourself.” Second step: find a mature person you trust, and together measure that sentence under Scripture. Third step: if the fear affects your sleep and your life, please also seek the help of a pastor or a professional. Those words never had authority to bind you. You are God’s people.
@@5792@@
IV. Light from the Original: Falsehood, Whitewash, Behold I (13:6, 10, 8)
@@5793@@
There are three words in this chapter worth slowing down for. The first is “falsehood,” Hebrew שָׁוְא (shav’), which appears again and again in verses 6, 7, 8 and 9, and comes back in verse 23, so that the whole chapter is like an echo. The word means empty, without weight. It also appears in the Ten Commandments: do not misuse the name of Yahweh your God (Exodus 20:7), and that “misuse” uses this very word. So to present your own words as “Yahweh says” is, before God, to take his name and speak empty words with it. Thirty seconds into life: next time before you say “God says,” ask yourself one thing: can I say where this sentence’s weight is?
@@5794@@
The second is “whitewash that has not been mixed through,” Hebrew תָּפֵל (tafel), in verses 10, 11, 14 and 15. Scholars understand this word in different ways: some say it is plaster that was not mixed well, some say it is a white lime used for decoration. Either way, the word also carries the meaning of “tasteless, without salt,” as Job 6:6 says: “Can that which has no flavor be eaten without salt?” The meaning is clear: it looks like a wall, but tastes like nothing. Thirty seconds into life: does the comfort I speak today have salt? That is, does it have real weight, or is it only pleasant?
@@5795@@
The third is “Behold, I,” Hebrew הִנְנִי (hinneni), in verse 8: “Behold, I am against you.” Elsewhere, this is the word people answer God with. When Isaiah heard God ask, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” he said: “Here I am. Send me!” (Isaiah 6:8), and that “Here I am” uses this very word. The same word, here, is spoken by God himself. God steps out, not because he is angry from far away, but because he is here, and he cares. Thirty seconds into life: to the lie God says “I am here, and I am against it,” and to the deceived he says “I am here, and I will rescue.” Today, you can say the same sentence to him: Lord, I am here.
@@5796@@
Could the meaning of the original words become just scholarship? How do I bring it into daily life?
@@5797@@
You can use it like this: keep only one sentence in hand for each original word. “Falsehood” (שָׁוְא) reminds me: the two words “God says” carry weight and cannot be used emptily. “Whitewash” (תָּפֵל) reminds me: does my comfort have salt, that is, does it have truth? “Behold, I” (הִנְנִי) reminds me: God is the God who is here; he is not angry from far away. This week you can choose one word, write it on a small card, and put it in your pocket or on the back of your phone. Every time before you say “God says” or “It’s fine,” touch the card. The original words are not meant to make us look learned; they are meant to give the text a handle that we can grasp.
@@5798@@
The first deviation is to preach this chapter as a witch-hunt for “finding who the false prophets are.” This is God’s warning to those who speak for him, and a Scripture that holds up a mirror. We should let the congregation first examine their own mouths, and not begin judging some particular person, or we become another kind of foolish prophet.
@@5799@@
The second deviation is to preach the “peace” of verse 10 as “all comfort and encouragement is false.” What God rebukes is a peace that has no basis in his word and covers over the crack, not comfort itself; God himself is the God of comfort. We should make the congregation more willing to reach out to others, not more afraid to open their mouths.
@@5800@@
The third deviation is to say to someone who is suffering, “You need to see your own wall clearly.” The one who plasters is the one who speaks, not the one who suffers. The sufferer does not need the extra weight of “is something wrong with me?”; what he needs is someone to sit down with him and not rush to fix him.
@@5801@@
The fourth deviation is to preach the wrath of verses 13 and 14 as God’s temper. This anger is aimed at the lie and the hunting of people; it moves for the sake of the people behind the wall; and the same chapter also says “deliver my people.” We should let the congregation see that God is the one who defends his own people, not a God who is hard to approach.
@@5802@@
The fifth deviation is to use verses 17 to 23 to belittle the ministry of women. The charge is “prophesying out of their own heart” and “hunting souls,” and has nothing to do with gender; the same words were already said to the male prophets in verse 2. Do not use the word “charms” to attach a label to any present-day custom or any group of people either; the point is the use, not the object.
@@5803@@
This chapter speaks of false prophets, so how do we tell whether a person or a word is from God?
@@5804@@
The Bible does not give a single standard, but several aspects that are to be looked at together. First, the source: does it come out of one’s own heart, and has God not sent him (verses 2, 3 and 6)? Second, the motive: is it protecting people, or hunting them (verse 18)? Third, the effect: does it make the righteous sad, strengthen the hands of the wicked, and keep people from turning back (verse 22)? Fourth, is it consistent with Scripture? Some ask: if a word does not come true, is it necessarily false? Fulfillment is one aspect, but not the only standard, because some words are warnings, and they may not come about when people repent. Most important: these standards are first used to examine ourselves, and then to examine one another within the body, and are not used to condemn people.
@@5805@@
The first question: we are very used to well-meant white lies “for your own good.” When a loved one has a serious illness, we say “It’s fine”; when a friend loses a job, we say “It will pass soon”; to keep a scene from being awkward, we make our words sweeter than the facts. Behind this there is often love, and also face and harmony. But chapter 13 reminds us: love is not painting. You can begin like this: next time someone pours out their troubles to you, do not say “It’s fine” first, only say: “I hear you, I’m here.” If you need to tell the truth, pray first, then say it slowly, and stay after you have said it.
@@5806@@
The second question: we are very used to respecting spiritual authority, and are embarrassed to ask “Where is it in Scripture?” “What the pastor says is what God says,” in a culture that honors teachers, can easily become a sentence that no one dares to check. But the God of this chapter first says to those who speak for him: “Hear Yahweh’s word.” A good pastor welcomes your checking. You can begin like this: next time you hear “God says,” gently ask: which passage of Scripture is this? A good leader will be glad that you asked.
@@5807@@
In a Chinese church, how do I check a pastor’s or elder’s words without seeming disrespectful?
@@5808@@
Checking and disrespect are not the same thing. Acts 17:11 says the Bereans were examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so, and they are called more noble than others. You can ask in the tone of seeking advice: “Where is this sentence in Scripture? I’d like to go home and read it again.” This is learning, not challenging. You can also build a habit in the small group: before anyone says “God told me,” they first say where it is from, or first say, “This is my own impression, please help me look at it.” Mature pastors will breathe a sigh of relief, because they do not want to carry the two words “God says” alone either. If you meet a teaching that allows no checking at all, please do not bear it alone; you can find another mature brother or sister to walk with you.
@@5809@@
VII. Living Out 321: Listen First, Then Speak
@@5810@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He said: the words I speak, I do not speak from myself. At Lazarus’s tomb he did not hurry to say “Don’t cry,” but wept himself first. His comfort is comfort after seeing the wound; his words are words heard from the Father.
@@5811@@
The Bible is my Standard. In Ezekiel 13:2, the first command God gave the false prophets was: Hear Yahweh’s word. The voice in my heart, however sincere and however much like God, must be measured under Scripture. Scripture is above my feelings, and above my good intention to comfort people.
@@5812@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. Ezekiel was lifted up by God’s Spirit and taken to see; those foolish prophets followed their own spirit. It is not that I lead him, but that he leads me. Each time before I speak, I will be quiet for a moment and ask: Lord, is this sentence yours?
@@5813@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness does not mean I have no thoughts; it means I lay my thoughts at his feet and let him be King. Even that “little prophet” in my heart who speaks well and sounds so much like God must listen to him. When I say, “This is my own view, not the Lord’s word,” that is what it looks like for the Lord to be King.
@@5814@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If someone is comforted and stands up because of my words, it is not my eloquence, it is the Lord’s word that held him up. I do not need to be taken for “the very spiritual one,” because all the glory belongs to the Lord who truly has a word to speak.
@@5815@@
To build God’s system. In God’s system, words have a source: they come from Scripture, are confirmed by the Holy Spirit, and are examined within the body, not decided by one person’s feelings. In such a community the righteous are not crushed by lies, and those who have strayed are not easily let off. This is, on earth, a small piece of God’s system.
@@5816@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “listen first, then speak”?
@@5817@@
The relationship is direct. The king’s word comes first, and the servant’s word comes after. If Jesus is King, I do not need to speak for him, and I do not need to prove I am spiritual; I only need to bring what he said to people just as it is, and mark my own ideas as my own ideas. Many people think this will make words lose their power. In fact it is the opposite, because when the hearers know which sentence is Scripture and which is yours, they dare to trust you more. You can practice like this: every night before bed, ask yourself two questions. Which sentence of “God says” did I say today? What is its source? Which sentence of comfort did I say today, and did I first see the other person’s crack? There is no need for self-blame, only honesty.
@@5818@@
The proud type says: “My impressions are very accurate, and I don’t need others to check them.” Scripture says to him: foolish prophets, who follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing. Pride is leaving man’s position and dressing your own words in God’s clothing. Returning to one’s position is saying: this is my own view, please help me look at it. You do not need to change everything at once; start with pausing for three seconds before you open your mouth next time.
@@5819@@
The fearful type says: “What if I heard wrong? What if that message is true? What then?” God says: I have not made the righteous sad. He does not keep people by frightening them. The fearful type needs to learn to change fearing “that sentence” into knowing “that God”: his word is for rescuing, not for hunting. You can begin with the smallest thing: put the saying that frightens you under Scripture, and ask a mature person to read it with you.
@@5820@@
The ashamed type says: “I have said ‘the Lord says’ quite a few times, when it was really my own, and I no longer have the right to open my mouth.” God’s opposition is aimed at that coat of paint over the lie, not at the person behind the paint. Admitting “that was my own idea” is not the end; it is the beginning of reverence. The ashamed type does not have to fix himself first; today, just say one true word to God and to one person you trust.
@@5821@@
The wounded type says: “Someone spoke in God’s name and hurt me deeply, and I don’t want to hear any more ‘God says.’” God does not blame you for your wound. He says: I have not made the righteous sad, and I will deliver my people. The wounded can slowly find someone trustworthy and tell your story; those who accompany should not rush to say “You need to forgive” or “You need to have faith,” but only say: “I’m here. I’m with you.”
@@5822@@
First, each day after reading a short passage of Scripture, be quiet for one minute, only listening, not asking, and not speaking for God; write down the date.
@@5823@@
Second, find one thing you badly want to happen, write down “This is my own thought,” and tell apart which parts come from Scripture and which come from your own wish; say which brother or sister you will ask to help you look at it.
@@5824@@
Third, the next time someone pours out their troubles to you, do not give advice and do not quote Scripture, only say: “I’m here, you can say as much as you like.” After you have said it, stay, for at least ten minutes.
@@5825@@
Fourth, pick one comforting phrase you often say (“It’s fine,” “It will all pass”), rewrite it into a more honest sentence, and use it once with one person this week; write down which day.
@@5826@@
Fifth, if you have a position of teaching or leading, this week ask a mature brother or sister to check with Scripture a sentence you are about to say; say the name and the time.

I declare: I will listen to God’s word first, and then open my mouth; I will not dress my wishes in God’s clothing.
@@5828@@
I declare: I will not use lies to make the righteous sad, nor use pleasant words to make those who have strayed more at ease.
@@5829@@

@@5831@@
What is one thing I can do right away this week, so that it does not stop at listening to the sermon?
@@5832@@
The simplest is to pick someone who is suffering today and send a very short message: “I thought of you. I don’t know what to say. I’m here.” Do not add Scripture, do not add advice, and do not add “You need to have faith.” Then write on paper the one “God says” that you have most wanted to say recently, and write the source after it; if you cannot write one, write after it: “This is my own thought.” At the next small group meeting, just say: this week I did one thing, and I do not need to say what it was. This action is the answer of chapter 13: not learning to speak more beautifully, but learning to speak words with roots.
@@5833@@
Chapter 13 begins with “word” and ends with “deliver.” It begins with Yahweh’s word coming to me; in the middle is a wall plastered with whitewash that was not mixed through, and a group of hunting pillows; and at the end God says: I will deliver my people out of your hand. From the foolish prophets, to the whitewashed wall, to not making the righteous sad, the whole chapter asks one thing: whose word are you listening to?
@@5834@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that what he opposes is the coat of paint, and what he loves is the person behind the paint. He wants us to listen before we speak, to see the crack before we comfort, and also, when we are deceived, to remember that he will himself come and tear open the knots. That God, in Jesus, came among us, and what he holds in his hands is not a whip, but the nail marks.
@@5835@@
So please remember the three key points of today’s lesson: emptiness, wall, charm. Emptiness is presenting your own words as God’s word; the wall is covering the crack with pleasant words; the charm is hunting people with fear. The focus of this lesson is not who is false, and not whether you have ever said something wrong, but Jesus, who truly has a word to speak, and who is willing to look at the crack and stay.

@@5864@@
All of us have times when we come before God to “inquire.” But Ezekiel chapter 14 makes us go quiet: several elders sat properly before the prophet, yet God said they had taken false gods into their hearts and set the stumbling block before their faces. Idols are not necessarily in the temple; more often they are in the heart. This chapter first shatters one hope: thinking that having a righteous person in the city, or an ancestor’s good name, can excuse us from turning back ourselves. Then it gives a greater voice: “Return!” God does not want to shame people; he wants to answer, himself, those who turn back.
@@5865@@
Idols in the heart are the beginning of estrangement between people and God; righteousness cannot be transferred, yet there is a God who is willing to answer, himself, everyone who turns back.
@@5866@@
The elders came to inquire, but God said they had taken false gods into their hearts, which estranged them from him. He called: “Return!” and promised to answer, himself, those who turn back. Then he pointed out that even if Noah, Daniel, and Job were among them, they could save only themselves, for righteousness cannot be transferred. Finally, for Jerusalem God leaves “a remnant,” so that people may see that what he has done is not without cause.
@@5867@@
The idol’s place is in the לֵב (heart): not in the temple, but on the throne; God’s response is not first to interrogate, but first to call “שׁוּבוּ” (Return).
@@5868@@
The gospel here is two verbs: we “return” (an imperative, which can be done now), and he “answers himself” (a promise, with no one in between). Those who return do not have to clean themselves up first.
@@5869@@
Selflessness is handing over the throne in the heart: not trying hard to tear down idols, but letting Jesus sit on it, so that everything else goes back to its place, becoming a gift and not a god.
@@5870@@
“Return. I will answer you myself. You are my people, and I am your God.”

God’s system requires life first, then relationships, then ministry: first deal with the throne in the heart, and only then talk about doing things for God; every person must stand personally before God, not relying on someone else’s righteousness.
@@5877@@
[1. Icebreaker] Please share one thing you care about a lot right now and badly want to accomplish. You do not need to go deep; just say how important it is to you.
@@5878@@
[2. Heart-piercing] In 14:3 God says “taken their idols into their heart.” Do you think that now, on the throne in your heart, there is something that, “if I lost it, I would feel the sky was falling”? You do not need to say its name; you can say only what kind of thing it is.
@@5879@@
[3. Gospel] In 14:6 God says: “Return!” and in 14:7 he says again: “I … will answer him by myself.” What do you feel about “he answers himself”? How is it different from what you used to think of as “God’s response”?
@@5880@@
[4. Action] 14:14 says the righteous can only save themselves. Have you ever relied on someone else’s righteousness, for example a parent’s faith, a pastor’s spirituality, or the church’s reputation? This week, what small step can you take to stand personally before God?
@@5881@@
[5. Intercession] Please pray for each person in the group: “Lord, please answer him yourself.” And for anyone present who is grieving or waiting over a child or family member, simply keep them company quietly, without pressing.

This week, each day say honestly before the Lord: “Right now, what I cannot let go of in my heart is ____.”
@@5883@@
Toward God: this week, before praying, be quiet for one minute and ask: “Today, have I come to inquire, or to ask you to stamp my plan?”
@@5884@@
Toward yourself: write down three things “without which I would feel I cannot go on living,” and write beside them: “This is a gift, not God.”
@@5885@@
Toward others: do not say to someone who is losing something, “Is it because there is an idol in your heart?”; only say: “I’m here, you can say as much as you like.”
@@5886@@
Toward daily life: pick one thing you rely on too much (your phone, work, other people’s approval), and this week deliberately leave a small blank space, put it down, and replace it with a stretch of quiet.
@@5887@@
Toward mission: if you serve, this week ask: is my motive for serving from the Lord, or from “needing to be needed”? Hand the answer to a trustworthy companion.
@@5888@@
① Preaching “idols in the heart” as an accusation against every troubled person
14:3 was said to a group of elders who came to inquire with idols; it was not said to every person who cries out in pain.
Test: after hearing it, does the one in pain carry one more burden of “is there an idol in me?” or one more invitation of “God is waiting for me”?
@@5889@@
② Using 14:14–20 to say “other people’s prayers are of no use to you”
This passage says that righteousness cannot be transferred as a person’s ground for saving himself; it is not denying intercession.
Test: do the hearers become less willing to pray for others? If so, it has been preached off course.
@@5890@@
③ Quoting “neither son nor daughter” to those who have lost a child or family member
This line must never be used to suggest that the loss is anyone’s fault. The preacher must say plainly: this verse is not saying that the loss of a child is the parents’ responsibility.
Test: if someone present has lost a loved one, will they walk away with an added layer of guilt after hearing it?
@@5891@@
④ Treating the four judgments (sword, famine, evil animals, pestilence) as matching any present-day disaster
The text is a judgment on Jerusalem at that time; it does not pass a verdict on any present event, nor is it for working out a timetable.
Test: after hearing it, does the congregation love the Lord more and become more patient, or become more alarmed and eager to match things up?
