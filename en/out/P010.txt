@@3253@@
⑤ Preaching “the sun” as “everything good is an idol”
Work, family, and service are good in themselves; they become idols when they stand between God and a person.
Test: does the congregation end up afraid to enjoy God’s gifts?
@@3254@@
⑥ Using “Yahweh doesn’t see” to mock other people’s weakness
8:12 is the elders making excuses for their own choices; it is not the honest doubt of a weak person.
Test: can someone who is truly struggling still dare to ask God, “Where are you?”
@@3255@@
⑦ Preaching “my eye won’t spare, and I will not hear” (8:18) as if it were said to everyone who prays
This was said to a generation that had turned its back on him and still said he could not see; elsewhere Scripture says that he will hear everyone who calls on him.
Test: after hearing it, does someone praying in tears at night still dare to pray?
@@3256@@
⑧ Preaching only judgment and not “the glory was still there” (8:4)
The order of this chapter is glory first, abominations afterward; skip the glory and it becomes a threat.
Test: when the congregation leaves, are they more sure that he is still there, or more afraid of being thrown away by him?
@@3257@@
⑨ Using the failure of the “elders” and “leaders” to encourage the congregation to distrust their pastors
The seventy elders standing in the dark room are there so that everyone, leaders above all, will first look at themselves; it is not for the congregation to sit in judgment on their leaders.
Test: does the sermon first invite the preacher and the leaders to stand in the light?
@@3258@@
⑩ Saying “the church of this generation is the Jerusalem of chapter 8”
To equate the ancient temple directly with some church or group today is to stick a label on people; the text does not do that.
Test: does the sentence pattern “They are …” appear anywhere in the sermon?
@@3259@@
⑪ Preaching “they put the branch to their nose” (8:17) as some definite ritual
Interpreters differ; the text only makes clear that God counted it an abomination. Do not invent details.
Test: has this detail been taught with more certainty than the text itself states?
@@3260@@
⑫ Telling someone who has been hurt, “Your dark room is your own problem”
If the dark room holds harm that others did to you, no one should demand that you see it at once, forgive at once, or confess at once; first protect, then accompany, and do not press for details.
Test: do those who have been hurt feel that this gathering is safe?
@@3261@@
⑬ Preaching God’s question “Have you seen this?” as the prophet being cross-examined
The question is asked four times; it is an invitation, because he does not want to bear this alone; it is not an exam.
Test: do the hearers feel invited, or cross-examined?
@@3262@@
⑭ Using panic to lead people to “open the dark room”
The fruit should be patience and a deeper love for the Lord, not lying awake at night and turning back to blame oneself again and again.
Test: when the congregation goes out, do they love the Lord more, or fear themselves more?

I. A Hand and a Lock of Hair (8:1–4)
@@3652@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever had a renovation man come to your house? Once, a workman broke open a wall and said, “Sir, the pipe inside here has been leaking for a long time.” We looked in together. The wallpaper on the outside was still fine, clean and white, without a single water mark. But on the inside the wood had turned black and a layer of mold had grown. I asked, “How can this be?” He said, “The water kept flowing inward and never came out, so you could not see it.”
@@3653@@
Today we read Ezekiel chapter 8, and what God took the prophet to see was the inside of a wall. On the outside of the wall, the temple was splendid, the festivals went on as usual, and the elders sat before the prophet as usual. On the inside of the wall, it was another matter. This chapter has four scenes, each one further in than the last, and each one heavier. But please remember one thing first: before God took the prophet into the dark room, there was a sentence: “the glory of the God of Israel was there.” The glory was there first, and only then were the abominations seen.
@@3654@@
Verse 1 says: “In the sixth year, in the sixth month, in the fifth day of the month, as I sat in my house, and the elders of Judah sat before me.” A group of elders came to the prophet’s house, sat down, and waited to hear what word God had. A very ordinary afternoon. Then, at the end of verse 1: “the Lord Yahweh’s hand fell on me there.” (The CUV has “Spirit” here, but the Hebrew word is “hand.”) God’s hand suddenly fell on a man sitting at home. You never know on which ordinary afternoon God’s hand will fall.
@@3655@@
In verse 2, the prophet saw a likeness, as the appearance of fire, with brightness above the waist, as it were glowing metal. This is the scene he knew well from chapter 1. We will not draw a picture, and we will not look for a match for every detail. We only notice what he did after he saw it. Verse 3 says: “He stretched out the form of a hand, and took me by a lock of my head.” Notice “the form of.” The prophet did not dare to say it was a hand; he said only the form of a hand. But that grip was real. To take hold of the hair is not to pat the shoulder gently; it is not allowing you to refuse to follow him. Sometimes God has us look at things we would rather not look at. He is not pushing us; he is taking hold of us, because he knows we would turn our heads away.
@@3656@@
Verse 3 goes on: “the Spirit lifted me up between earth and the sky, and brought me in the visions of God to Jerusalem, to the door of the gate of the inner court that looks toward the north; where there was the seat of the image of jealousy, which provokes to jealousy.” Then verse 4 has a line that is easiest to miss in the whole chapter: “Behold, the glory of the God of Israel was there, according to the appearance that I saw in the plain.” The glory was still there. Right beside the door where the idol was set. God did not wait for people to sweep the temple clean before he came; he was still there beside the defilement. This is the undertone of the whole chapter: he is still there, and that is why he let the prophet see.
@@3657@@
Was the prophet’s body really taken to Jerusalem, or was it in a vision? Does the difference matter?
@@3658@@
Interpreters read it in two ways. One holds that the prophet’s body was really lifted to Jerusalem; the other holds that the text says “in the visions of God,” and that verse 1 says he was still sitting in his house, so this was an experience in the spirit, and the body did not leave. These notes follow the text’s own words: in the visions of God. But please notice that this difference does not affect the weight of the whole chapter. A vision is not imagination; it is God letting the prophet see a real scene: a real temple, real people, real dark rooms. After the prophet had seen it, chapter 11 will return to the land of exile and tell those sitting before him what he saw. So for us, the point is not where the prophet’s body was, but what his eyes saw, and whether we are willing to look together with him.
@@3659@@
II. Light from the Original Language: קִנְאָה, תּוֹעֵבָה, רָחֳקָה (8:5–6)
@@3660@@
In verse 5, God said to the prophet: “Son of man, lift up your eyes now the way toward the north.” North of the gate of the altar, in the entry, was “this image of jealousy.” In verses 5 and 6 there are three Hebrew words worth stopping to look at.
@@3661@@
The first is “jealousy,” in Hebrew קִנְאָה. The place where this word is most familiar is marriage: a husband’s heart for his wife, because of love, cannot tolerate a third party. God’s “jealousy” is not pettiness and not a wish to control; it is a husband who loves very deeply. The idol is not merely a carved statue in the temple; it is the third party who has broken into the marriage. What you can turn into life in thirty seconds is this: God is not checking whether you have broken a rule; he is asking, who has sat in my place?
@@3662@@
The second is “abominations,” in Hebrew תּוֹעֵבָה, plural תּוֹעֵבוֹת. These are things God hates and cannot bear. Verses 5 and 6 say “great abominations” over and over, and verse 6 says: “you will again see yet other great abominations.” Notice that God uses “again.” This chapter is not a list of every abomination laid out in a row; it goes in, layer by layer. Abominations never grow in a day; they begin small.
@@3663@@
The third is “far off,” in Hebrew לְרָחֳקָה, whose root רָחַק means to be far. Verse 6 says: “that I should go far off from my sanctuary.” The order of this line matters: it is not that God was in a bad mood and left; it is that people doing these things in the temple “pushed him far.” The distance is opened up a little at a time. Interpreters differ here: some say that this is people’s conduct driving God away; some say that it is God himself actively departing, as judgment. These notes hold that both are true: people turned their backs again and again, and God departed step by step; in later chapters the glory really leaves the temple. But in chapter 8, the glory was still there.
@@3664@@
Put the three words together: jealousy (because of love), abominations (being seen), going far off (the result). The starting point is not anger but love. Because he loves deeply, he is jealous; because he is jealous, he cannot leave it alone; because people will not turn back, the distance is opened step by step. If in this chapter you hear only anger, please read it once more: God says again and again, “Have you seen this?” so that the prophet, and we too, will turn back while the distance has not yet grown long.
@@3665@@
God says he is “jealous.” Does that mean God is very petty and hard to get along with?
@@3666@@
No. We often hear “jealous” as small-minded, but when Scripture uses it, it is used for the closest relationship. When a husband sees his wife’s heart taken by someone else, he is not calculating; he is in pain. God’s jealousy is like this too: he is not afraid that we will share his glory; he cannot bear to see us give ourselves to things that cannot save us. Idols never give a person true peace; they only take. So when you read “the image of jealousy,” please do not first think, God is terrifying; first think, God cares about me very much. You can practice it like this: quietly ask yourself, what most easily takes over my heart today? There is no need to rush to throw it away; just tell him honestly: Lord, this place was yours all along.
@@3667@@
III. The Hole in the Wall and the Dark Rooms (8:7–13)
@@3668@@
Verse 7: “He brought me to the door of the court; and when I looked, behold, a hole in the wall.” Verse 8, God said to the prophet: “Son of man, dig now in the wall.” When he dug, he saw a door. Think about it: God is all-knowing, so why did he have the prophet dig? Because it is an invitation: the truth is not thrown down from above; a person has to dig it open with his own hands. Notice that the hole was already in the wall. Someone had seen it long ago; only no one had gone to dig.
@@3669@@
Verse 10 says that when he went in, he saw every form of creeping things, abominable animals, and all the idols of the house of Israel, portrayed around on the wall. Four walls, full. This was not painted in one day, but wall by wall, year by year. The one painting probably said to himself as he painted: it’s only a drawing.
@@3670@@
Verse 11: “Seventy men of the elders of the house of Israel stood before them,” “every man with his censer in his hand; and the smell of the cloud of incense went up.” The censer was a vessel meant for offering to Yahweh in the temple; now it was carried into the dark room, toward pictures on a wall. Some interpreters think the number seventy recalls the elders of Moses’ day, who shared the burden; the text does not say so. But whatever the case, they were the men everyone saw most clearly, the ones who led. They were also the first to walk into the dark room.
@@3671@@
Verse 11 has another name: “Jaazaniah the son of Shaphan stood in the middle of them.” Interpreters differ: some recall the scribe Shaphan, who in Josiah’s time read the book of the law to the king; if it is the same family, it is even more startling, a family that once guarded the book of the law, with a descendant standing in a dark room. Others say that Shaphan was a common name, that the text does not explain, and that it cannot be certain. These notes reach no conclusion; we take it only as a reminder: a good family tree does not guarantee today’s heart.
@@3672@@
Verse 12: “Son of man, have you seen what the elders of the house of Israel do in the dark?” Notice “every man in his rooms of imagery.” The dark rooms were not public but private. Seventy men, seventy rooms. Then comes that line: “For they say, ‘Yahweh doesn’t see us. Yahweh has forsaken the land.’ ” This line is worth looking at slowly. If he does not see, it does not matter what I do; if he has forsaken us, I need not give any account of what I do. It is a reason found for one’s own choices.
@@3673@@
But the order of the text overturns this reason. He not only sees; he brought the prophet there and had the prophet see too; in verse 4 the glory was still there. Who left whom first? Interpreters give three accounts: some say the disaster and exile prove that God left first; some say people first worshiped idols and then needed a saying that “God is absent”; and some say the glory was still there, and God’s departure happened step by step after people turned their backs again and again. These notes take the third: 8:4 says the glory was still there, and God’s departure was not the temper of a single night.
@@3674@@
Reading this far, I must first say a word of protection. Some of what is in a dark room, you did not put there yourself. If you are someone who has been hurt, there is no need today to hurry to open that room, and no one is asking you to see it at once, to forgive at once, or to speak it out at once.
@@3675@@
The elders said, “Yahweh doesn’t see us.” Would we really think that today?
@@3676@@
Few people say these words out loud, but many say them by what they do. When in a place where no one sees we do something we ourselves would not dare let anyone know; when, alone, what we think, look at, and plan is completely different from what we are in the gathering, we are saying: he can’t see me. Verse 12 reminds us that this kind of thinking is meant to set our own minds at ease; it is not because there is any real reason for it. Because God does not only see; he is still there. The good news is: being seen is not necessarily a bad thing. You can practice it like this: tonight, when you are alone, say to God: Lord, you see me, and I won’t hide. Then tell him one thing that you have never dared bring into the light. He has known it all along; he is only waiting for you to speak.
@@3677@@
IV. Tears Outside the Temple and Backs at the Temple Door (8:14–18)
@@3678@@
Verse 14: God brought him to the door of the north gate of the outer court of the temple, where there were women sitting, “weeping for Tammuz.” Who was Tammuz? Interpreters give different accounts: some say he was a god of the ancient Near East associated with harvest and with death and rising again, and there was a yearly ritual of mourning; some say the weeping symbolized the withering of plants in summer and hope for their revival the next year. These notes do not go into the details of the myth; the point is that the women brought their tears to a place that was not Yahweh’s.
@@3679@@
Please notice that God did not rebuke the tears, but where the tears went. There is nothing wrong with weeping. The bereaved weep, the wounded weep, and Jesus himself wept at Lazarus’ tomb. If you are weeping now, please do not take this verse and say to yourself: my weeping is a sin. God never said that. What God asks is: where have your tears been taken?
@@3680@@
Verse 15: “Have you seen this, son of man? You will again see yet greater abominations than these.” In this chapter God asked the prophet four times, “Have you seen this?” in verses 6, 12, 15, and 17. Like a father taking his child’s hand again and again. This is not an exam; it is an invitation: he does not want to bear this seeing alone.
@@3681@@
Verse 16 is the last scene, and the clearest. God brought him into the inner court of the temple, to the door of the temple, between the porch and the altar, where there were about twenty-five men, with their backs toward Yahweh’s temple and their faces toward the east, worshiping the sun. Joel 2:17 says that the priests weep between the porch and the altar, so interpreters often note that this is where those who serve stand. The temple door faces east, so to face east is to turn one’s back on the temple. The position was nearest to God; the direction was farthest from God.
@@3682@@
Verse 17: “Is it a light thing to the house of Judah that they commit the abominations which they commit here? For they have filled the land with violence, and have turned again to provoke me to anger. Behold, they put the branch to their nose.” What was in the dark room finally flowed out into the street and became violence. The dark room and the street are not two worlds. As for what the gesture “they put the branch to their nose” was, interpreters differ: some say it was a ritual connected with sun worship; some say an ancient tradition reads it as a gesture of contempt toward God; some say the text is hard and cannot be certain. These notes do not add details to the text; we are sure of only one thing: this gesture was counted by God as an abomination.
@@3683@@
Verse 18: “Therefore I will also deal in wrath. My eye won’t spare, neither will I have pity. Though they cry in my ears with a loud voice, yet I will not hear them.” Does God really not hear prayer? Some say that once judgment is announced, prayer is of no more use; some say it is a declaration, at the moment judgment is fixed, to a generation that turned its back on him and still said he could not see; and some say we must read the whole Bible together. These notes take the latter two: Ezekiel itself also says that God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but rather that he should turn from his way and live. So this line was said to a generation that kept turning its back; it was not said to everyone who prays in the night.
@@3684@@
Chapter 8, verse 18 says, “I will not hear them.” Does God really not hear people’s prayers?
@@3685@@
This line is very heavy, but please notice whom it was said to, and when. It was said to a generation that turned its back on him and said he could not see, after judgment had already been fixed. It is not a rule: “God does not hear people’s prayers.” Elsewhere the Bible says again and again that whoever calls on his name, he will hear; Ezekiel itself also says that God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but rather that he should turn from his way and live. So if you are someone who prays in tears at night, please do not put this line on yourself. But this verse also reminds us: prayer cannot take the place of turning around. To turn your back on him and at the same time ask him to hear makes no sense. Turn around, and he is right here. You can begin like this: stop first, and ask yourself one question: am I facing him now, or have I turned my back on him?
@@3686@@
The first deviation is to preach this chapter as “idols are everywhere in the church; find them,” using it to point out the dark room of some brother or sister or some ministry team, or saying “the church of this generation is the Jerusalem of chapter 8.” The text is four scenes in a vision; it is not a checklist and not a label. We want the congregation first to open their own doors, not to go looking for other people’s dark rooms.
@@3687@@
The second deviation is to draw the four scenes as a precise map or a ranking, or to preach “they put the branch to their nose” as some definite ritual. The point of the text is “Have you seen this?”, and interpreters do not agree on the details; do not invent details the text does not give. We want the congregation to know God’s heart better, not to admire the preacher’s scholarship more.
@@3688@@
The third deviation is to preach the women weeping for Tammuz as “weeping is a sin,” implying that the bereaved and the wounded do not have enough faith, or to say to someone who has been hurt, “Your dark room is your own problem.” Verse 14 of chapter 8 rebukes where the tears went, not the tears. We want those who grieve still to feel that they may weep before God.
