<p><strong>“Perpetual hostility”: once hatred is not let go, it becomes a person’s dwelling place.</strong></p><p>Israel and Edom were brothers (Gen 25—27). On the day Jerusalem fell, Edom did not come to the rescue, but instead “gave them over to the sword.” The phrase “the time of the iniquity of the end” also appears in Chapter 21, verses 25 and 29, and points to the same threshold. Edom may not have drawn the sword himself. A person may not lift a hand, and yet stand by and allow it.</p><p>Hatred is seldom one great act. More often it is the daily accumulation of refusing to let go. What is hardest to let go is often not the old debt, but our own reaction on the day another fell. Handing that day over to God is lighter than holding on to it.</p>

I. God Turns and Faces a Mountain (35:1–4)
@@15755@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Let me begin with a scene in a tea room. At a company there was a colleague who had worked hard for a whole year, and on the day the promotion list was posted, his name was not on it. At noon that day, in the tea room, I heard someone say in a low voice: I knew it would turn out this way. I said nothing then, but I noticed that there was a very small voice inside me that quietly said one word: good. That word lasted only half a second, and no one heard it. I frightened myself. Today we read Ezekiel 35, which speaks through the whole chapter to only one mountain, and what it speaks of is that half second.
@@15756@@
Verse 1 says: “Moreover Yahweh’s word came to me, saying.” Verse 2 says: “Son of man, set your face against Mount Seir, and prophesy against it.” Please notice that in Chapter 34 God had just finished saying “I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep,” in a voice so gentle; turn one page, and God’s face turns in another direction. He is not speaking to Israel, but to a mountain. Mount Seir is the hill country of Edom, and Edom is the descendants of Esau, Israel’s own brother. The main character of this chapter is not an enemy far away, but the one nearest of all.
@@15757@@
If you remember Genesis, you know that the story of these two brothers is long. Twins, struggling with each other in the same womb; a bowl of red stew and a blessing, and from then on the two were parted. Later the descendants of Esau lived in the hill country of Seir, and the descendants of Jacob became Israel. They were separated by mountains and by rivers, yet the blood tie was never truly cut. Sometimes blood is a blessing, and sometimes it is like a rope pulled too tight, which, when you let go, snaps back and strikes you in the face. So it is no accident that God chose to begin with this mountain.
@@15758@@
The first sentence of verse 3 is heavy: “Behold, I am against you, Mount Seir.” This is a declaration of war, and in Ezekiel it is spoken to other places too. God does not say this to only one nation; he shows partiality to no one. Then come three actions: stretch out the hand, make you desolate, make you an astonishment. Verse 4 says: “I will lay your cities waste, and you will be desolate. Then you will know that I am Yahweh.” Please notice that last half. God says the purpose of all this is not to let off steam, but that people may know him. Behind every word of judgment, there is a knowing hidden.
@@15759@@
We also have to be honest: reading this, some people will feel relief and think: at last someone is standing up for us. One sister told me that when she was little she was bullied by relatives, and reading a passage like this she secretly cheered in her heart. I said to her, there is nothing wrong with your cheering; that is the voice of your wound. But what this chapter wants to give you is more than a cheer, and as we read on, you will see.
@@15760@@
Why does God say “I am against you” to a brother by blood? Is that not too harsh?
@@15761@@
Because the relationship of brothers carries heavier responsibility to begin with. Edom is the descendants of Esau, Israel’s own brother, and to strike when a brother is in disaster is a heavier sin than to do so as a stranger. But please see clearly that what verses 3 to 5 speak of is not that the Edomite people are born to be hated, but that he “had a perpetual hostility” and gave people over to the sword. God’s opposition is aimed at these actions and attitudes, and its purpose is “you will know that I am Yahweh.” So we should not take this sentence and use it on anyone. If you yourself have been hurt by someone closest to you, you may find comfort here: he takes that wound seriously. You do not need to hurry to carry it out for him, and you do not need to be pressed into reconciling.
@@15762@@
II. Perpetual Hostility: On the Day a Brother Fell into Calamity (35:5–9)
@@15763@@
Verse 5 is the verdict of this chapter: “Because you have had a perpetual hostility, and have given over the children of Israel to the power of the sword in the time of their calamity, in the time of the iniquity of the end.” There are three details here; please look at them one at a time. First, “perpetual.” This is not a moment’s angry word; it is an old matter kept in the heart, kept so long that it became a posture. Second, “in the time of their calamity.” Not an ordinary quarrel, but the day people are most vulnerable. Third, “gave over to the sword.” He did not merely watch; he took action and sent people out.
@@15764@@
I knew an old man who, over dividing the family property, had not spoken with his brother for more than thirty years. Later the younger brother had a stroke and lay in the hospital. When the old man heard the news, he said one sentence: now it’s his turn. After saying it, he sat in the living room for a long time without moving. Afterward he told me, Pastor, only when that sentence left my mouth did I discover how many years I had kept it hidden in my heart. The most frightening thing about perpetual hostility is that it makes no noise and no fuss; it just waits quietly, waits for the day a person falls.
@@15765@@
Please think: why does the Bible record this so clearly? Because hatred is very good at disguise. Sometimes it wears the clothes of “justice,” sometimes the clothes of “I’m only telling the truth,” and sometimes it wears no clothes at all, only mentions casually at the dinner table: he used to do this and that. No one listening feels anything is wrong, but that one sentence is a stone carried in piece by piece. This chapter points out the stones one by one so that afterward God can take them away one by one.
@@15766@@
In verse 6 God swears: “as I live,” I will prepare you for blood, and blood will pursue you. The second half gives the reason: “Since you have not hated blood, therefore blood will pursue you.” Please notice the verb “pursue.” Sin is not a stone dropped from heaven, but footsteps following behind a person. If a person feels no distress at bloodshed, wherever he goes, it follows him.
@@15767@@
Verses 7 to 9 are a picture of desolation: the mountain desolate, those who pass through cut off, hills, valleys and watercourses filled with the slain, the cities uninhabited. The picture is heavy, and as we read we do not need to enlarge it, nor to set music to it in our minds. The aim of reading is not to watch a spectacle, but to hear God’s heart. The last line of verse 9 is still the same line: “Then you will know that I am Yahweh.”
@@15768@@
I have been hurt very deeply. Reading that God will repay those who hurt people, I feel satisfied. Is that all right?
@@15769@@
You may be honest, and there is no need for shame. Part of your satisfaction is the comfort of “at last someone sees,” and that is real. But please notice two things at once. First, the weight of this chapter is “God heard,” not hoping for another’s misfortune; if you place your hope on someone else’s bad luck, you will keep waiting, and keep being tied to that person. Second, the seat of justice is to be given back to God, and you only need to return to your own position. If this satisfaction cannot be put down for a long time, or comes mixed with thoughts of harming others or yourself, please let a pastor or a professional counselor walk with you. You can also first say to God: Lord, I am in great pain, you heard it all, and that is enough.
@@15770@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Desolation, Hostility, Heard (35:3, 5, 13)
@@15771@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth a close look. The first is “desolation,” שְׁמָמָה (shemamah). Verse 3 says: make you a desolation and an astonishment. In just fifteen verses this word appears again and again, in verses 7, 9, 14 and 15, like a drum that keeps beating. Its meaning is waste, emptied, with no sign of life. Please notice that what God wants people to see is not the shape of a mountain’s terrain, but a kind of “emptiness”: a person who counts another’s desolation as his own harvest ends up walking into an empty place himself.
@@15772@@
The second is “hostility,” אֵיבָה (eivah). Verse 5 says “perpetual hostility,” and the original is אֵיבַת עוֹלָם, literally “an enduring enmity.” This root first appears in Genesis 3:15, where God says: “I will put hostility between you and the woman.” Enmity is not a small human mood; it has been on the earth since the fall. But please notice that verse 5 uses the word “perpetual” to describe it: not anger, but enmity stored up as a lasting home. Today we can ask ourselves: is there someone in my heart whom I am no longer angry with, but for whom I have kept a place all along?
@@15773@@
The third is “heard,” שָׁמַע (shama). In verses 12 and 13 God says twice: I have heard. This is the gentlest word in the whole chapter, and also the most serious. To the one who was hurt, it is comfort: those things said about you behind your back, the grievances you swallowed in the dark, he heard them all. To the one who spoke, it is a warning: what you thought only you and your friends heard, he heard too. The same word, in the ears of the wounded, is being cared for; in the ears of the one who slandered, it is being seen.
@@15774@@
Put the three words together and you have the journey of this chapter: hostility (eivah) stored long in the heart bears slander and secret gladness, and the end is desolation (shemamah); and in the middle, a God who has been listening all along (shama). Would you like to ask yourself a question right now: the words I spoke today, do I want him to have heard them? This question can be taken home within thirty seconds.
@@15775@@
The word “desolation” keeps appearing. Does God especially like to frighten people with harsh words?
@@15776@@
No. The word is repeated so many times because it is the principle of judgment in this chapter: the way you treat another’s desolation is the way your own desolation will answer you. Verse 15 says: “As you rejoiced over the inheritance of the house of Israel because it was desolate, so I will do to you.” The repetition is so that people cannot pretend they did not hear. You can also read it this way: the more God knocks with the same word, the more it shows he wants people to turn around. Because desolation is not his purpose; the purpose in verses 4 and 9 is “you will know that I am Yahweh.” So when you read a repeated word, do not be afraid first; ask first: what does he want me to know of him?
@@15777@@
IV. “Mine” and the Sentence in the Parenthesis (35:10–15)
@@15778@@
Verse 10 is the most interesting verse of the chapter. God says: “Because you have said, ‘These two nations and these two countries will be mine, and we will possess it.’” These two nations are Israel and Judah, the northern kingdom and the southern. When Edom saw his brothers fall, his first thought was not sympathy but accounting: this land has no owner now, and it can be mine. That is the scheme of “mine”: another’s disaster becomes my opportunity.
@@15779@@
Then the text adds a sentence in a parenthesis: “Yahweh was there.” This is the quietest sentence in the chapter. Edom thought the land was empty and had no owner, but the owner of the deed was still there. Chapter 11 said that God’s glory left the temple, and it looked as if God had left; but this parenthesis tells us: he never gave up that land, and he never gave up those people. At the very end of the book, this city will be called “Yahweh Is There,” and this parenthesis is the foreshadowing.
@@15780@@
Verses 12 and 13 are what God heard: all your insults, the words you spoke against the mountains of Israel, saying: “They have been laid desolate. They have been given us to devour.” “You have magnified yourselves against me with your mouth, and have multiplied your words against me. I have heard it.” Please notice “with your mouth against me.” He thought he was speaking ill of Israel, and God says, you were speaking to me. The words people say behind backs turn out to be said for God to hear.
@@15781@@
Verses 14 and 15 are the last symmetry of this chapter: “When the whole earth rejoices, I will make you desolate.” “As you rejoiced over the inheritance of the house of Israel because it was desolate, so I will do to you.” As you are, so I will be. This is not God’s temper but God’s fairness: with the measure you use to measure out to others, he will measure out to you.
@@15782@@
Please pause once more. Verse 14 says “When the whole earth rejoices.” The whole earth is rejoicing, and Edom is to be desolate. This line has a picture: the bustle of the world and the emptiness in one person’s heart can exist at the same time. If a person takes another’s desolation as his own joy, in the end this joy is borrowed, and when it falls due it must be repaid. The joy that never has to be repaid is the kind that, before God, does not need to step on anyone to stand secure.
@@15783@@
Let us read clearly: this chapter is not teaching us to hope for anyone’s desolation, nor that we may stick the label “Edom” on anyone. It does two things. To the grieving, it says: he heard. To the one with secret gladness in the heart, it says: he saw too. Both are true, and both come from the same heart.
@@15784@@
Does “Yahweh was there” mean that God will certainly step in right away to deal with the wicked?
@@15785@@
No. The point of this sentence is “he was there,” not “he acts on my timetable.” Edom thought the land was empty, and God said he was still there, which means his sovereignty never left. For those who are waiting, this is security, not pressure. Please do not take this sentence and say to the bereaved, the seriously ill or the long-wounded: you need to have faith. For them, this sentence is at most a companionship: you have not been abandoned, he is still in this room. If you yourself are waiting, you can say one sentence to God each day: Lord, I do not know when you will act, but I believe you are still there.
@@15786@@
The first deviation is using Chapter 35 to curse a particular person, a people, or a group in conflict with you. This is God’s word to Edom, not a tool people may pick up and use. If a sermon causes the listeners to feel hostility toward anyone, it should be deleted. We want the congregation to be more sure that God hears, not to hate anyone more.
@@15787@@
The second deviation is directly matching Edom to some modern country or ethnic group, or using this chapter to build a timetable and count days. Interpreters read this chapter in different ways, and these notes do not make any one reading a condition of faith, nor do they name any modern party. We want the congregation to revere God more, not to panic more.
