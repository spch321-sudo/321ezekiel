I. The Baby in the Open Field (16:1–5)
@@7002@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. I have a friend who has served in a children’s home for many years. I asked her: When a new child arrives, what is the first thing you do? She said: I do not ask the name, and I do not ask where he comes from. I first take a clean towel, wrap him up, and hold him for a moment. I asked why, and she said: Because when many children come, what they most lack is the experience of being wrapped up well. She said this very softly. I sat there and did not ask anything more.
@@7003@@
Today we are reading Ezekiel chapter 16. This is one of the longest and hardest chapters in the whole book, with 63 verses. It begins with a child nobody wrapped in a towel. Verse 1 says: Yahweh’s word came to me, saying. In verse 2, God tells the prophet to cause Jerusalem to know her abominations. We may feel a tightening in our hearts and think that what follows is all rebuke. But God does not begin by counting her sins. He begins by speaking of where she came from.
@@7004@@
Verse 3 says: “Your origin and your birth is of the land of the Canaanite. An Amorite was your father, and your mother was a Hittite.” This is not a comment on any ethnic group. It means: your starting point is not your own merit. Before Israel took it, Jerusalem was a city where others lived, and she had no family pedigree to boast of.
@@7005@@
Then in verses 4 and 5, God, in a very calm tone, counts them off one by one: “in the day you were born your navel was not cut. You weren’t washed in water to cleanse you. You weren’t salted at all, nor wrapped in blankets at all.” These are the most basic things at a birth. Then comes the heaviest sentence: “No eye pitied you.” Please notice that in this sentence nobody is named as doing wrong; rather, nobody did anything, and not one pair of eyes stopped. The passage says: “you were cast out in the open field, because you were abhorred in the day that you were born.”
@@7006@@
We need to read this part slowly, and not enlarge the picture. If you yourself have been abandoned or ignored, you may feel very uncomfortable hearing this. Please do not force yourself to be strong. You can listen to just one sentence for now: what is said here is that God remembers where this child came from, and God does not blame the child. The one who was thrown away was never at fault.
@@7007@@
If my origin or childhood was truly ignored, as at the beginning of this chapter, how does God see me?
@@7008@@
At the beginning of chapter 16, God says that whole neglected time out loud. This shows that he sees, and that he did not turn his eyes away. Nor does he put the responsibility on the baby: no one cut the cord, no one washed her, no one showed pity; these are things others did not do, not the child’s fault. So if your childhood had a similar gap, you do not need to persuade yourself that “it was nothing,” and you do not need to hurry to forgive anyone. You can first say to God: This hurts very much. He will not hurry you. If that memory still often affects your sleep, your emotions and your relationships, please find a trusted pastor or counselor to walk with you slowly, so that you do not carry it alone.

II. Though You Are in Your Blood, Live (16:6–14)
@@7010@@
Verse 6 is the turning point of the whole chapter, and it is my favorite verse: “When I passed by you, and saw you wallowing in your blood, I said to you, ‘Though you are in your blood, live!’ Yes, I said to you, ‘Though you are in your blood, live!’” Look at his actions: he passed by, he saw, he spoke. He did not wait for her to wash herself clean, did not wait for her to grow up, did not wait for her to beg. She was still in her blood, and he said: Live. And he said it twice, as if afraid she had not heard it the first time.
@@7011@@
Verse 7 says: “I caused you to multiply as that which grows in the field.” It means that from that day he took care of her, and she grew slowly like the plants of the field, through wind and rain. She grew up and became a beautiful young woman. But please notice that she was still naked and bare, and she still had nothing. Her growing up was also given by him; she did not raise herself.
@@7012@@
Verse 8 is the gentlest picture in the whole chapter. God says: “Now when I passed by you, and looked at you, behold, your time was the time of love; and I spread my skirt over you, and covered your nakedness. Yes, I swore to you, and entered into a covenant with you, and you became mine.” Please notice the order: covering first, then the oath. He did not first make demands of her; he first shielded her from shame. In ancient times, this act was a sign of being willing to take responsibility. He covered her first, and only then said: You are mine.
@@7013@@
I think of an elderly sister. As a child her family was very poor, and she wore old clothes left over from her older brothers and sisters. Later she believed in the Lord, and every time she received new clothes, she would first touch them and say softly: Thank you, Lord. She said: I am not being polite, I am afraid of forgetting. This sister’s “thank you” is practice in remembering: what I have today is not what I deserve, it was put on me.
@@7014@@
In verses 9 to 13, God tells one by one what he gave: he washed away the blood with water and anointed her with oil, gave her embroidered clothes, fine linen and silk, put on her bracelets, a chain, a ring and a beautiful crown, and she ate fine flour, honey and oil. These were all the best things, and even the materials remind us of the tabernacle. She prospered to royal estate.
@@7015@@
You can stop and think: If someone said one sentence to you on your worst day, one that was not a rebuke, how long would you remember it? Perhaps for many years. Because that sentence, when you had no qualification at all, treated you as someone worth treating well. The “live” of verse 6 is such a sentence. It is not a reward; it is a beginning.
@@7016@@
Then in verse 14, God ends the whole section with one sentence: “Your renown went out among the nations for your beauty; for it was perfect, through my majesty which I had put on you,” says the Lord Yahweh. Beauty was put on her by him. This sentence is the key to the whole chapter. Her beauty, her renown and her honor, not one of them was earned by herself. All the tragedy that follows begins from forgetting this sentence.
@@7017@@
God says “live” while she is “in her blood.” Isn’t that different from what we often say, “first get yourself in order, and then come to see God”?
@@7018@@
Very different. The order in verse 6 is that God first passes by, first sees, first speaks, while she is still in her blood. We often think we must first change and first get ourselves in order before we are qualified to come to God, but this chapter says that salvation is his speaking first. This does not mean we do not need to change; later chapters have much to say about turning back. It means that change begins after we have been told “live.” You can practice it like this: today say to God: “Lord, I am not yet in order, but I have come.” Then listen quietly: is the first thing he says rebuke, or “live”? This order is the order of the gospel: grace first, response after.

III. Light from the Original Language: Live, Remember, Covenant (16:6, 22, 60)
@@7020@@
There are three words in this chapter for us to look at. The first is “live,” in Hebrew חֲיִי (khayi). Verse 6 says: “Though you are in your blood, live!” This word is an imperative, and it means “Live!” The moment God spoke it, that “live” gave life at the same time. He was not encouraging her to “try hard to survive,” but speaking in the tone of creation: Live. “In your blood” can be read in two ways: one is “although you are in blood, you may still live”; the other is “in your blood, I say to you: Live.” Neither is wrong, and the meanings are close: God speaks at her worst moment, and gives life in the worst condition. You can use it today like this: when a voice in your heart says “you are finished,” go and listen to the other voice: khayi, live.
@@7021@@
The second is “remember,” in Hebrew זָכַר (zakhar), which appears in two places in this chapter. Verse 22 says: “you have not remembered the days of your youth”; verse 60 says: “Nevertheless I will remember my covenant with you in the days of your youth.” This word means not only “to call to mind,” but “to remember and to act.” She forgot, so she turned to trust in other things; he remembers, so he firmly comes back. This gives us a very practical reminder: weakness in the spiritual life is often not because we are not trying hard enough, but because we forget. We forget that he said “live,” and we forget that he covered me. You can do one small thing every day: write down one thing that “he gave me.” This is practicing zakhar.
@@7022@@
The third is “covenant,” in Hebrew בְּרִית (berit). Verse 8 says: “entered into a covenant with you, and you became mine”; verse 60 says: “I will establish to you an everlasting covenant.” In this chapter this word appears at the beginning and also at the end. She broke the covenant, but God says: “I will establish my covenant with you.” The word “establish” here means that he himself makes it stand. The weight of the covenant is not on her side; it is on his. She is not in the covenant because she kept it well; she is still in the covenant because he established it.
@@7023@@
Put the three words together, and this is the path of the chapter: khayi, he first says “live”; zakhar, she forgets, he remembers; berit, he closes with an everlasting covenant. From a word, to a memory, to a covenant, this chapter wants us to put down “I must prove I deserve it,” and return to “I am one he has said ‘live’ to.”
@@7024@@
Does “remember” mean that if I just keep remembering God’s grace, I will not fall?
@@7025@@
Not quite. Remembering is very important, but the final point of this chapter is not human memory, but God’s remembering. Verse 60 says: “I” will remember. She forgot, and he still remembered. So if you find that you often forget and are often weak, please do not first blame yourself with “I forgot again.” You can do two things: first, write down his grace and put it where you can see it, to help yourself remember; second, also believe that even if you forget, he still remembers you. These two things do not conflict: we try our best to remember, and at the same time put the final weight on his remembering. If you often blame yourself because you cannot remember, please give that self-blame to him too.

IV. Trusting in Beauty and Looking for Other Loves (16:15–34)
@@7027@@
Verse 15 is the turn of the whole chapter: “But you trusted in your beauty, and played the prostitute because of your renown.” The words “But you” are like a door closing behind us. Before this, it was what he gave; here, she takes what he gave and goes off elsewhere. This whole section uses the picture of marriage: God is the husband, Jerusalem is the wife, and she abandoned her husband and went after others. This picture is not an evaluation of any woman, nor of any marriage; it speaks of a city and her relationship with God.
@@7028@@
We do not need to enlarge the picture word by word. The point of this part is what she did with what he gave. In verses 16 to 19, she took the clothes, the gold and silver, the oil and the food he gave, and made other images, and served other objects. He said: I gave these to you. What he gave, she used to offer to others. This is no small thing; it is treating grace as capital and investing it in other gods.
@@7029@@
Verses 20 and 21 are especially heavy. She gave the children she bore for God to others. God said: “you have taken your sons and your daughters, whom you have borne to me, and you have sacrificed these to them to be devoured.” This is the most painful sentence in the whole chapter. It is not an accusation against any parent who has lost a child; it is an accusation against that city. When God says “my children,” it means: those children were loved by him from the start. If you have ever lost a child, please do not read these two verses as an accusation against you. This verse is not about you.
@@7030@@
Next, the passage says that she built high places at the head of every street, and with the Egyptians, the Assyrians and the Chaldeans, one after another, she was still not satisfied. This is ancient political language: trusting great nations, making alliances and asking for protection were all searching for “a patron more reliable than God.” In ancient times, trusting in other powers and worshiping other gods were often the same thing.
@@7031@@
Please notice that from beginning to end this section does not have God saying “you are dirty,” but “you forgot.” The summary in verse 22 is short: you have not remembered your youth. Her problem was not a bad origin, but a forgotten origin. Once a person forgets that he was picked up, he easily thinks he was standing high from the beginning, and it is then very easy to feel that others owe me, and the world should serve me.
@@7032@@
In verses 30 to 34, God says a very deep thing: “How weak is your heart.” Some say this is a sick heart. He also says that she is different from other women, because no one follows her to play the prostitute; she bribes people to come. It means: even this behavior is an empty thing. She paid, and did not get love in return. In the end she is worse than the worst of them, because what she gave was not need, but the abundance he gave her. This picture tells us: after we leave God, we do not find more love; we search and grow emptier.
@@7033@@
This chapter uses “prostitution” to describe God’s people leaving him. Could such a picture make people feel it is aimed at women or at certain people?
@@7034@@
No. This is a picture commonly used by the Old Testament prophets: the relationship between God and his people is like a marriage covenant. The subject of this picture is a city, and the one being rebuked is the city that trusted in others and forgot him; it is not any gender, nor any person who has been wounded. If you have been hurt, these words may feel like a stab, and please do not force yourself to finish the whole chapter. You can stop at verses 6 to 8 and verses 60 to 63. You are not the one being judged. The speaker must also be careful: do not enlarge the picture, do not use this picture to evaluate any person, and never use it to say to anyone who has been hurt, “this is your problem.”
@@7035@@
The first deviation is reading chapter 16 as “women are by nature immoral,” or using it to evaluate any gender or any marriage. This is God’s picture for a city. We want people to know God’s heart better, not to make people’s view of anyone harsher.
@@7036@@
The second deviation is enlarging the pictures of nakedness and prostitution and preaching with shock. A preacher does not enlarge the picture. Someone sitting in the audience who has experienced sexual harm must also be able to listen to the end in peace. We want people to see how God covers, not to wound them again.
@@7037@@
The third deviation is saying “you must forgive” or “you must be strong” to people who have been abandoned or abused, or preaching verses 20 and 21 as an accusation against parents who have lost a child. This chapter is about God remembering people, not about asking people to do something first. We first accompany, without hurrying, without accusing.
@@7038@@
The fourth deviation is narrowing the sin of Sodom to one kind of person, or using it to compare others as worse than ourselves. Verse 49 has already said it: “pride, fullness of bread, and prosperous ease was in her and in her daughters. She also didn’t strengthen the hand of the poor and needy.” This is a mirror, not a finger to point at others.
@@7039@@
The fifth deviation is preaching the everlasting covenant as “you can sin freely, since there is a covenant,” or equating “Jerusalem” directly with any present-day city, nation or people. The forgiveness of verse 63 brings shame and silence, not license; and this chapter was spoken to Jerusalem of that time, names no person of today, and sets out no timetable.
@@7040@@
Verses 35 to 43 speak of judgment very heavily. Is God really that angry? How can it be preached without frightening people?
@@7041@@
This section really is heavy, and we need not pretend it is light. But please notice its direction: the point of the judgment is that a false trust will in the end come to nothing. The lovers she trusted will in the end become the ones who attack her. Verse 42 says: “So I will cause my wrath toward you to rest, and my jealousy will depart from you. I will be quiet, and will not be angry any more.” God’s anger has an endpoint, and its aim is not destruction but that she would no longer walk that road. When you preach it, do not describe the pictures in detail, and do not use them to frighten. You can say it like this: a false patron will in the end fall; only his covenant stands. Those who listen should come closer to God, not be more afraid of being thrown away.
@@7042@@
The first question: we are used to thinking “origin decides everything.” Since childhood we hear: where are you from, what does your family do, what is your education. Some have been told since childhood, “you are not as good as others,” and some take pride in their origin. Ezekiel 16:3 says: “An Amorite was your father, and your mother was a Hittite.” God says this to mean that origin is not your merit, and not your sin either. You can begin like this: find someone you trust, say one thing you have always felt as “I come from there, so I cannot,” and listen together to verse 6: what he says is “live,” not “your origin is bad.”
@@7043@@
The second question: we are used to a culture of “repaying kindness,” but very unused to purely receiving. Everything must be repaid, we fear owing a favor, even to God: how much must I do to deserve his love? Verse 14 says: “it was perfect, through my majesty which I had put on you.” It means that all of this was put on her by him. You can practice like this: this week, accept a kindness someone gives you and say only “thank you,” without hurrying to repay it. Practice receiving in small things, and it will be easier to believe that God’s love is not earned.
@@7044@@
I have served for many years and often feel I am “repaying God’s grace.” Is it wrong to think this way?
@@7045@@
It is not wrong, but be careful. Gratitude is beautiful, but if it becomes “I must pay it all back, or I do not deserve it,” service turns into debt. The order of chapter 16 is that he gives first, he says “live” first, he covers first; our service is a response after receiving, not a way to earn qualification. You can do a small check: is my service today going because I am loved, or to confirm that I am worth loving? If the former, your service will carry joy; if the latter, you will soon be tired. If you find it is the latter, there is no need to blame yourself; you can turn back and say to God: Lord, I have come to receive; please let me serve out of love.
@@7046@@
VII. Living Out 321: One Who Has Been Told “Live”
@@7047@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He too is the one who passes by, stops, sees and speaks. He stopped at the well, stopped by the road, stopped before people who were ignored. He is not pointing the way from on high; he is the one who bends down and covers with the corner of a garment. As we read verse 6 of chapter 16, we ask: Today, have I become one who “passes by and then stops”?
@@7048@@
The Bible is my Standard. We do not measure who we are by our own feelings; we measure by his word. Verse 6 says that he said “live” in the blood; verse 14 says that beauty was put on her by him; verse 60 says that he remembers and establishes an everlasting covenant. The standard of this chapter is not “do I deserve it,” but “what does he say.”
@@7049@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who brings to our mind what he has said, and it is the Holy Spirit who helps us discern: today, am I receiving, or am I trusting in my own beauty, my own talent and my own reputation? Spend a quiet moment each day and ask: Holy Spirit, what is there today that you gave me, but I treated as my own capital?
@@7050@@
Let Jesus be King. Verse 15 says she trusted in her beauty. What this chapter asks me to hand over is my beauty, my reputation, my talent, my service. To let Jesus be King is to put back into his hand what he put on me; what I received, I return to the one who gave it. Selflessness is not having nothing; it is remembering: I am one who has been told “live” by him.
@@7051@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. Verse 14 says that beauty was through the majesty he put on her. So if I have a little that looks good, I do not say “this is mine.” Even in the places where I can stand, can serve, can be praised, the glory goes to the Lord who said “live” to me in my blood.
@@7052@@
To build God’s system. God’s system does not depend on human memory; it depends on his remembering. In such a community, nobody is looked down on for origin, and nobody is lifted up for talent. Even Sodom and Samaria are on the list of those he restores; God’s house is wider than our eyes. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: first be told “live” by him, and then learn to love others.
@@7053@@
What is the connection between “Let Jesus be King” and “remembering that my beauty was put on me by him”?
@@7054@@
The connection is direct. The reason people do not let Jesus be King is often that they forgot: what I have now was given by him. Once I forget, I treat talent, reputation and smooth days as my own capital and begin to trust in them; this is the “trusted” of verse 15. To let Jesus be King is to return these to him every day. You can practice like this: get up in the morning and say three sentences to Jesus: today what I most easily lean on is this; today what I am most afraid of losing is this; today I put them back in your hands. After a while you will find that you are no longer so afraid of losing, because you remember that these were never yours.
@@7055@@
The proud type says: “My achievement today is the result of my own effort.” The passage says to him: “it was perfect, through my majesty which I had put on you.” Pride is leaving a person’s own position, seizing God’s power, and wanting to prove oneself by oneself. To return to one’s position is to admit: this was put on me by him. You do not need to say it all at once; first say one sentence to God: “Lord, this one thing, you gave me.”
@@7056@@
The fearful type says: “If my origin, or my past, becomes known, I will be left behind.” When he passed by, she was still in her blood. He did not wait for her to be clean. The fearful type needs to learn to replace “I am afraid of being left behind” with “he has already said live.” You can start with one very small thing: to one person you trust, say one small thing you have never dared to say.
@@7057@@
The ashamed type says: “My past is too dirty, I do not deserve to be loved.” He put the corner of his garment over her, covering first, and swearing after. Shame says “you were abhorred,” the gospel says “you are covered.” The ashamed type does not need to wash herself clean first; today, only let his garment cover you.
@@7058@@
The wounded type says: “No one had pity on me, and I no longer trust anyone.” Verse 5 says: “No eye pitied you.” He did not tell you to hurry up and get better, did not blame you for being wounded; he only passed by, stopped, and saw you. A companion should not hurry to say “you must forgive” or “you must be strong,” but only say: “I am here, I will walk with you.”
@@7059@@

It does not need to be big; one small thing is enough, such as a friend, a job, or today’s meal. Then say to God: Thank you. Next, choose one small action you can do today: send a message to someone who once “passed by and stopped” in your life, and say: Thank you. This action is the answer of chapter 16: receive first, then respond. At the next small group meeting, just say: This week I received one thing and gave thanks for it; no need to say what it was.
@@7069@@
Chapter 16 has a very special arc. It begins with the abandoned baby in the open field; in the middle is the bride who grew up, was adorned, and then turned away; and at the end is an everlasting covenant. From blood to a beautiful crown, to forgetting, to being remembered, this is a journey of a God who does not let go.
@@7070@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is the one who passes by, stops and says “live”; he also sees our forgetting, and still remembers. In Jesus he came among us, he bent down and covered us with his garment, and on the cross he also bore our shame.
@@7071@@
So please remember three points of this lesson today: he said “live” first; beauty was put on us by him; and he firmly establishes his covenant with us. The focus of this lesson is not how disgraceful your origin is, not how much talent you have, and not how others see you, but Jesus, who said “live” to you in your blood.
@@7072@@

Remember us, firmly establish your covenant with us, and comfort everyone who has been left behind and ignored. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, amen.

@@7100@@
We all have times when we are not at ease: the water is already in our hands, yet we still want to find another channel; we have promised something, and under pressure we want to change course. Chapter 17 speaks of this with a riddle: a vine planted in good soil by many waters bends its roots toward a second great eagle. God’s answer is not comfort first, but a question that needs no answer: Will it prosper? Yet the chapter does not stop at withering. At the end, God himself takes a tender twig from the top of the cedar and plants it on the high mountain of Israel, so that it becomes a shade where every kind of bird dwells. Where people’s bending of the roots ends, God’s “I will” begins.
@@7101@@
When people bend their roots toward another patron, they will wither in the very beds where they were planted safe; and God will still plant a tender twig with his own hand, to make the dry tree flourish, so that people know that he is Yahweh.
@@7102@@
God has Ezekiel tell a riddle: the first great eagle cropped off the tender twig of the cedar and planted it as a low-growing vine; but it bent its roots toward the second great eagle. The answer: the king of Judah broke the oath he swore to the king of Babylon and turned to Egypt for help, and will not escape, for the oath was sworn by God. At the end, God himself takes a tender twig and plants it on a high mountain, to become a cedar that shelters all the birds.
@@7103@@
Whose hand the roots are under, to him the tree belongs; the covenant-breaker withers “in the beds where it grew” (17:10), and God’s hand turns high and low upside down.
@@7104@@
Grace in this chapter is God saying “I will”: when human schemes are used up, he himself crops the twig, he himself plants it, he himself makes it grow.
@@7105@@
Selflessness is no longer finding one more channel for the sake of security. Put the roots back in his hand, and admit: the water you give is already enough.
@@7106@@
“You do not need to find one more patron for yourself. I will plant it myself, and I will make it grow myself.”
@@7107@@
This week, find one thing for which you “want to look for another patron,” write it down, and say to God: “This thing, I put the roots back in your hand.”

<p><strong>חוּד חִידָה וּמְשֹׁל מָשָׁל——the verb and its object from the same root</strong></p><p>The original puts the verb and the noun right next to each other: “riddle a riddle, parable a parable.” This is a cognate-object construction, which adds emphasis and also carries a touch of wordplay.</p><p>There is no real translation dispute here. What is worth remembering is the order: first the riddle is told (17:3–10), then the riddle is explained (17:11–21), and the listeners have to wait.</p>
@@7136@@
<p><strong>God’s first instruction to the prophet is to tell a riddle.</strong></p><p>Not a declaration, not an accusation, but a riddle. Why? Because the listeners are “the house of Israel,” who were long used to treating the prophet’s words as someone else’s business. A riddle has a quality: you have to use your head, you have to turn back and ask, you have to put yourself in before you can guess it.</p><p>The riddle and the parable (מָשָׁל) are placed side by side in this verse. The same word, in chapter 18, becomes the “proverb” in the people’s mouths. First it is in God’s mouth, then in people’s mouths.</p><p>This is a gentle arrangement. God could say directly “you have broken the covenant,” but he first tells a picture, giving people a little time to see themselves. A picture is easier to swallow than an accusation, yet just as sharp.</p><p>What the prophet holds in his hand is not a verdict, but a story. A story has one advantage: when people listen, their guard loosens. By the time they find that the story is about themselves, there is nowhere to hide.</p><p>Landing: the words that truly strike you are often not those someone points at you, but when you hear a story and suddenly realize “that is me.” As you read this chapter, do not hurry to find who the eagle is; first ask: Which branch of the tree am I?</p>

<p><strong>① אֶרֶץ כְּנַעַן: “Land of Trade” or “Land of Canaan”?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Proper name “the land of Canaan”</strong>: the literal rendering</li><li><strong>Common noun “land of merchants”</strong>: in some places in the Old Testament כְּנַעַן refers to merchants, and the CUV translates “land of trade” accordingly</li><li><strong>Double meaning</strong>: both senses are present</li></ul><p>In this verse “city of merchants” and “land of trade” stand side by side, and it is hard for the reader not to hear “merchants.” Ezekiel 16:29 also uses the same word for Chaldea.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: land of merchants.</strong> The point of the passage is not geography, but that it is a place where everything can be priced.</p></blockquote>
