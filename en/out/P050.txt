@@16825@@
This chapter has three Hebrew words worth looking at slowly. The first is רוּחַ (ruakh), in verses 5 to 10 and verse 14. One word, three meanings: breath, wind, spirit. The one that comes “from the four winds” in verse 9 is wind; the one that enters the bones in verse 10 is breath; the one put inside the people in verse 14 is “my Spirit.” You control none of these three. You breathe even while you sleep, and not one breath is your own decision; the wind never takes orders from anyone; the Spirit is his. Life, from beginning to end, is something we receive.
@@16826@@
The second is “hope,” תִּקְוָה (tiqvah), in verse 11. This word belongs to the same family as “waiting,” and elsewhere it also means “cord.” Hope is not just a feeling; it is a line pulling you forward. Once the line snaps, a person is like a kite whose string has been cut. But please notice that the people who said this were speaking to God. They brought their snapped-line words before God, and God received them.
@@16827@@
The third is “know,” יָדַע (yada'), in verses 6, 13 and 14. “You will know that I am Yahweh” is said three times in these verses. This word does not mean knowing a piece of information; it means knowing a person. People often come to truly know God not in smooth times, but at the moment the grave is opened. This does not mean suffering is a good thing; it means that even inside the grave, God has a place where he wants to be known.
@@16828@@
Put the three words together and you have the road of this chapter: hope is cut off (tiqvah), his Spirit comes in (ruakh), and people come to know him (yada'). He did not hurry to explain why the bones were dry. He first put breath into them.
@@16829@@
Does the resurrection in this chapter mean Israel’s return, or the dead rising?
@@16830@@
Through the centuries there have been three readings: the restoration of Israel; also pointing to the hope of bodily resurrection; or both read together. These notes set out the different views, tie it only to the connections the New Testament itself makes, set no timetable, and pass no judgment on any modern nation. All three readings agree on two things: the breath is his, and the one who opens the graves is also he. What matters more for you today is this: which plain are you standing on, and do you hear the words “my people”? You do not have to solve every question of interpretation before you can receive this comfort. You can begin by reading this verse three times. Just read it; do not discuss it.
@@16831@@
IV. Two Sticks, One King, One Sanctuary (37:15–28)
@@16832@@
Those who have come to life still have one problem: they are divided from one another. In verse 16, God told the prophet to take two sticks of wood, writing on one “For Judah, and for the children of Israel his companions,” and on the other “For Joseph, the stick of Ephraim, and for all the house of Israel his companions.” Two pieces of wood, two names. The names were written on them; Judah is still Judah, and Joseph is still Joseph. Unity does not wipe the names away.
@@16833@@
Verse 17: “Then join them for yourself to one another into one stick, that they may become one in your hand.” By verse 19 the subject has changed: “they will be one in my hand.” From the prophet’s hand to God’s hand. In this passage the word “one” (אֶחָד, 'ekhad) keeps coming back. Unity is not something we achieve by squeezing two pieces of wood together; it happens in an obedient hand, and also in a greater hand.
@@16834@@
Verse 23 ties unity and cleansing together: they will no longer defile themselves with idols and detestable things, and God will save them and cleanse them. So unity here does not mean “everyone, stop quarreling.” Those who have been hurt do not have to swallow the hurt first for the sake of unity. Before unity stands a God who first cleanses and first saves; and on the road to unity, each of us must first ask in our own heart: do I have an idol in there?
@@16835@@
Verses 24 to 28 are about the king and the sanctuary. “My servant David will be king over them. They all will have one shepherd.” Who is this “David”? Through the centuries some have read him as a king from David’s house, some as pointing to Christ, and some have read both layers together. We do not point to any modern figure. We hold on to only two things: he is called “my servant,” and he is “one shepherd.” The New Testament says Jesus is the good shepherd.
@@16836@@
Finally, God says he will make a covenant of peace with them, and his sanctuary will be among them forever; “My tent also will be with them. I will be their God, and they will be my people.” The whole chapter begins with a plain and a heap of bones, and ends in a tent: God dwelling with people. This sanctuary, too, has been read in different ways through the centuries, and we do not set a timetable for building a temple. We see only this: the end of resurrection is not people standing up and that is that; it is God living among them.
@@16837@@
These two sticks are to be held in the hand “before their eyes” (verse 20). It is not a decision made by one person in a private room; it is an act done before everyone’s eyes. God’s unity can be seen: you can touch it, you can point at it and say, these two, now they are one.
@@16838@@
I have been hurt deeply by people, yet the church says we should be “united.” I can’t do it. Does that mean I am not spiritual enough?
@@16839@@
No. Each stick had its own name; unity did not erase the differences, and it does not mean the victim must forgive first. The passage places unity in God’s hand, and joins it to cleansing. You can first let your hurt be heard; keep a safe distance from that person; and when needed, ask a pastor or a professional to walk with you. If physical harm is involved, safety always comes first. Unity is something God accomplishes in his own hand; it is not a smile you force out. What a wounded person needs is time and safety, not being hurried. You only need to say honestly to him: Lord, I can’t yet; please do it. Take your time. He is not rushing you, and the church should not rush you either.
@@16840@@
The first distortion is telling someone who has lost a loved one, “Just have faith and he will rise,” or using chapter 37 to urge people not to cry. This chapter never tells anyone not to mourn. There is only one test: after hearing it, does the person feel accompanied or hurried? If hurried, stop.
@@16841@@
The second distortion is turning the vision of dry bones into a formula for healing: pray the right way and a serious illness will certainly improve; if a long illness is not healed, your faith was not enough. The bones are a figure of speech (verse 11), not a formula. If a sermon makes a person with a long illness feel “it’s my fault, I don’t have enough,” it must be changed.
@@16842@@
The third distortion is treating the words in verse 11, “our hope is lost,” as unbelief, sin, or something to be corrected, or telling the discouraged and the depressed, “Be strong” or “Have hope.” God received those words exactly as they were, and only then answered. What this chapter writes first is bones, not smiling faces.
@@16843@@
The fourth distortion is pointing at a person or a church and saying “there is no breath yet”: “You only have the outward form and no life.” That sentence is a mirror for ourselves, not a label for others. It also includes using this chapter to calculate the time of the return, or to match it with some modern nation or figure.
@@16844@@
The fifth distortion is preaching the unity of the two sticks as meaning no one may hold a different opinion, or that wounded people must reconcile at once; or preaching “breath” as if people could bring someone back to life by method or eloquence. The breath is his, unity is something in his hand, and we do not do it in his place.
@@16845@@
Someone close to me has lost a loved one. May I use this chapter to comfort them?
@@16846@@
Accompany first, speak afterward. At Lazarus’s grave, Jesus wept (John 11:35); he did not first teach people not to cry. You can say: I miss him too, and I’m here. You can sit quietly; you can help by cooking a meal. If they themselves ask, “Is there still hope?”, then gently tell them: the grave is not bigger than his word. Do not say, “He will rise, so don’t be sad,” and do not say, “Be strong.” Accompaniment needs no explanation and no conclusion. If the person cries, do not rush to stop them, and do not press for details; check in on them once more privately after the meeting. Your presence carries more weight than your words.
@@16847@@
The first question: we are very used to “sharing the good news and hiding the bad.” When someone asks how we are, we say “Fine,” because we do not want our family to worry, and we do not want anyone to think our faith is not enough. So the words in verse 11, “our hope is lost,” can only be said in the heart. But in this chapter, God received those words exactly as they were. You can begin like this: find someone you trust, and in one sentence say one thing in which your heart has had no hope for a long time. Do not dress it up, and do not add “but I know God is faithful.” When you have said it, stop.
@@16848@@
The second question: we are very used to “harmony is precious.” For the sake of peace, we do not bring up hurtful things, and we cover the cracks with smiles, so that on the surface everyone is fine. But the two sticks each had a name, and God did not cover up the wound; he himself joined the two together. True unity is not about not talking; it is about having a greater hand when we do talk. You can begin like this: think of one relationship that is “fine on the surface but cold inside,” and this week do just one small thing: a greeting, or a prayer for them. Do not seek immediate reconciliation, and do not ask the other person to change first.
@@16849@@
Chinese people say, “Don’t let others worry.” If I honestly say I have no hope, will I burden others?
@@16850@@
You can choose whom to tell; you do not have to tell everyone. One trusted person is enough. You are not throwing a heavy load on your family; you are refusing to keep the words bottled up alone in your bones. The people in verse 11 spoke their words out, and God received them first; only then came “my people.” You can say to that person: right now I don’t need you to fix anything, I only want you to know. If there are thoughts of not wanting to live, please do not carry them alone; tell a pastor, a doctor, or a professional counselor. Speaking out is not weakness; it is the first step. You do not have to say it beautifully, only truthfully.
@@16851@@
VII. Living Out 321: Bones, Breath, Unity
@@16852@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He came to Lazarus’s grave, first wept, and then said, “Lazarus, come out!” He did not avoid the grave, and he did not urge people not to cry. Those who follow him learn his way: draw near to death, mourn first, then speak. We open our mouths, and he breathes.
@@16853@@
The Bible is my Standard. As we read chapter 37, we say what the text says: when God’s word came, bone came together with bone; God said “my people,” and we also call those who suffer by that name. What the text does not say, we do not say for him, nor do we use it to set a timetable.
@@16854@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. Verse 14 says: “I will put my Spirit in you, and you will live.” Resurrection is not people pulling themselves together; it is his Spirit coming in. Each day, take a quiet moment, not asking for a feeling, and only say: Lord, let your Spirit be with me.
@@16855@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of the hand that says “the bones must be put together by me” and “unity must be brought about by me.” Speaking is my position; the breath is not mine to manage. The sticks become one in his hand, not in mine.
@@16856@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If a heap of dry bones comes to life, do not say, “I held on and made it happen.” He opened the grave, and his Spirit came in. We open our mouths, and the glory goes to the Lord who breathes.
@@16857@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is one shepherd, one flock, one dwelling place. Life first: the breath enters; then relationships: the two sticks become one; then ministry: the sanctuary in the midst. The order cannot be reversed; we do not put the bones together first and then wait for the breath.
@@16858@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “I only take care of speaking”?
@@16859@@
The relationship is very direct. A king’s work is to open graves, breathe breath, make two sticks one, and set a sanctuary among the people; a servant’s work is to hear, to speak, and to obey. We often say with our mouths that Jesus is King, but when we are disappointed, our first move is to put things together ourselves: do a little more, endure a little more, urge a little more. You can practice like this: every morning say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to put together today; whom I most want to control today; today I place them back in your hands. This is not doing nothing; it is giving back to the King the part that does not belong to me.
@@16860@@
The proud type says: “The bones depend on me to put them together; this family, this church, this child, I must save.” The text says to him: the breath is not yours to manage; speaking is your part. Pride is leaving one’s position and grasping God’s power. To return to one’s position is first to say one sentence to God: “Lord, you know; I let go.”
@@16861@@
The fearful type says: “I dare not hope again; I’m afraid that when the hope is over it will be one more disappointment.” The text does not tell you to hope at once. God first asked the prophet: can they? The prophet only said: you know. The fearful type can begin with the smallest step: just say to God, I am afraid. He hears.
@@16862@@
The ashamed type says: “I have only the outward form and no breath. Am I not spiritual enough?” “Only there was no breath in them” is the second act of the story; it is not your name. Shame says “you’re finished”; the gospel says “my people.” You do not have to get well first before you can stand before him.
@@16863@@
The wounded type says: “I have been hurt by someone, and you want me to be united with that person? I can’t.” God does not blame you for being unable. The sticks each had a name, and unity does not mean you must forgive first. A wounded person can slowly find someone truly safe and tell your story; one who accompanies does not rush to say “you must forgive,” but only says: I’m here.
@@16864@@

@@16868@@
Fifth, find someone who is grieving or has been ill for a long time, and greet them once. Only greet; do not press, do not advise, do not explain. Say: “I thought of you, and I’m here.” Give the name and the date.

@@16874@@
Chapter 37 is a plain, and it is also a tent. It begins with bones, in the middle is breath, and at the end are the words “my dwelling place will be with them.” From bones, to breath, to unity: the bones are joined together, the breath enters, the two sticks become one, and God dwells with people. This is a journey in which he is the one moving all the way through.
@@16875@@
The most special thing about chapter 37 is that it never asks anyone to pull themselves together first. The prophet did not pull himself together, the people did not, and the bones certainly did not. All the verbs fall on him. In our lives, too, there are probably days when all we can do is be carried along like this.
@@16876@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is able, that he is willing, and that he receives even our hardest words. That God, in Jesus, walked to Lazarus’s grave, wept, and then spoke. He is the Lord of the breath, and he is our shepherd.
@@16877@@
So please remember the three key points of today’s lesson: bones, breath, unity. The focus of this lesson is not how much hope you have, not whether your faith is enough, and not how others see you, but Jesus, who puts breath into the bones.

<p><strong>⑧ How should the three reflexive verbs (Hithpael) be read?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Self-revealing view</strong>: Hithpael is a reflexive stem; God “makes himself great and holy.”</li><li><strong>Seen-by-people view</strong>: the weight is on the eyes of the nations; God’s greatness and holiness are recognized before people’s eyes.</li><li><strong>Combined view</strong>: he shows himself, and people truly see; both sides are in the sentence.</li></ul><p>The CUV’s “magnified and sanctified” places two Hithpael verbs side by side, keeping the reflexive force. These notes follow the CUV.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: combined: the subject is God, the action is his, and the purpose is people’s eyes and people’s knowing</strong></p></blockquote>
@@16966@@
<p><strong><strong>The closing of the whole chapter is one sentence: they will know that I am Yahweh.</strong></strong></p><p>The terrain of the war, the list of the coalition, the names of the disasters: in verse 23 all of it is gathered into one purpose: “I will magnify myself and sanctify myself.”</p><p>The Hebrew has two reflexive verbs (Hithpael): he makes himself great, and makes himself holy. Greatness is not something people lift him up to; holiness is not something people add to him. He shows himself.</p><p>Then look at “in the eyes of many nations.” Holiness here is public; it is not kept in a corner of the temple. He wants the nations to see, to “know.”</p><p>The main line of the whole book appears once more: “they will know that I am Yahweh.” Even Gog’s coming stands under this sentence. Who comes is not the final point; the final point is: who was seen.</p><p>Landing: what do you most want to be seen for in your own life? The text gives you a very light way out: you need not be magnified; let him be magnified. Selflessness is not nothing; it is letting him receive all the glory.</p>
