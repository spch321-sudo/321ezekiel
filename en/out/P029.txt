<p><strong>God says they are not silver, they are dross.</strong></p><p>The one who refines silver waits in the fire for silver. Bronze, tin, iron and lead are the impurities in the silver; when the fire gets hot, the impurities float on top and are skimmed off, and the silver is what remains. In this passage, we can only stop at the diagnosis.</p><p>Verse 19 has a very ironic word: קָבַץ, “gather.” In chapter 20 this word is a word of salvation (20:34, 41). <p><strong>God is looking for one person. This sentence is the quietest in the whole chapter, and also the most painful.</strong></p><p>For twenty-nine verses, judgment falls layer by layer: the city, the dross, the prophets, the priests, the princes, the people. Then, in the last two verses, the story pauses, and God himself speaks: I sought for a man among them.</p><p>To seek (בָּקַשׁ) is a very human verb. He is not pronouncing sentence; he is searching. The person he wants has two actions: “build up the wall” (גָּדַר, to build up what has been broken) and “stand in the gap” (עָמַד בַּפֶּרֶץ, to stand where the wall is broken). It is a military picture: the wall is broken, the enemy is about to pour in, and the one who stands in the breach blocks it with his own body. He does not stand there to win; he stands there to block, and he blocks so that God may not destroy.</p><p>The text ends: “but I found no one.” The weight of this sentence lies in God’s original heart: he wanted to find someone. Behind this verse is a God who did not want to destroy. His anger is poured out only after “I found no one.”</p><p>But we should not read this sentence as making one person responsible for a city. Ezekiel does not say who “should” step forward; he tells us God’s heart: he is looking for an intercessor.</p><p>Readers of the New Testament stop when they reach this point: the one who truly stands in the breach has already come. He stands between God’s judgment and us, and he has not walked away.</p><p>Landing it: you do not have to hold up a whole city alone. What you can do is first stand in one small gap: a family, a person, a prayer. When you stand, you are not alone, because the one who “stands in the gap” stood first, and is still standing.</p>

I. A City of Blood Is Put on Trial (22:1–16)
@@9773@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. On a ridge between rice fields in the countryside, there is a gap. When a heavy rain falls at night, the water gushes out through that gap, and by morning the water in a whole field has drained away. This is what farmers fear most. He does not stand at the edge of the field and ask: whose ox trampled it? Who did not repair it sooner? He picks up his hoe first, goes down in the dark, and plugs that opening with mud. In this chapter, God is also looking for such a person. Only this time, the field ridge is a city.
@@9774@@
In verse 2, God tells the prophet: “Will you judge the bloody city?” Please notice that it is “judge,” like questioning point by point in a courtroom. God first has the prophet lay the case file open, so that she may know all her abominations. Verse 3 says that this city sheds blood within herself, and makes idols against herself to defile her. Shedding blood is a word that appears again and again in this chapter, from verse 2 at the beginning to verses 6, 9, 12 and 27 later on. Blood is the city’s greatest charge.
@@9775@@
From verse 6, God reads them out one by one: the princes, each according to his power; those who treat father and mother with contempt, those who oppress the foreigner, those who wrong the fatherless and the widow; despising his holy things, profaning his Sabbaths. Please notice this order: first those in power, then parents in the home, the outsider in the city, the fatherless and the widow with no one to depend on. Those who are oppressed are all the people with the least strength to speak for themselves. Verse 12 adds: taking interest and increase from your neighbors, greedily gaining by oppression, and “have forgotten me.” Forgetting God is the root of all the charges.
@@9776@@
Part of the list in verses 9 to 11 touches the most private harm within a family. We will not explain it word by word, and we will not describe the pictures; these verses are not preaching at the wounded. God names these things because he has seen them, and he stands on the side of the wounded. If these verses touch your wound, you may stop; you do not have to finish reading, and you do not have to say anything out loud. You did nothing wrong. You do not need to forgive first, and you do not need to “be strong.”
@@9777@@
The content of Ezekiel 22:9–11 is very heavy. How should I handle it in a small group or on the pulpit?
@@9778@@
Touch on it briefly; do not magnify it, do not explain it word by word, and do not describe the pictures. You can say something like this: God sees that the corruption of this city has reached the most private places, and he stands on the side of the wounded. Then keep moving on. Some suggest skipping it entirely, but saying nothing at all may make the wounded feel that God did not see; so one sentence of acknowledgment is better than a long explanation. Before the meeting, the leader should prepare: who needs individual care afterward? If someone discloses a painful experience, do not press for details, and if necessary help him seek counseling and professional help.
@@9779@@
Verse 13 is easy to misread: “I have struck my hand at your dishonest gain which you have made, and at your blood which has been within you.” Striking the hands together sounds like gloating, but this gesture also appeared in the previous chapter, and it was a hand-strike of grief. God is not venting his anger; he is sighing. The question in verse 14 is more like a father asking: “Can your heart endure, or can your hands be strong?” on that day. Verses 15 and 16 say that he will scatter you, and consume your filthiness out of you, so that you will know that he is Yahweh. At the end of judgment is “knowing,” not “destroying.”

II. Dross in the Fire of the Furnace (22:17–22)
@@9781@@
Verse 18, God changes the picture: “the house of Israel has become dross to me. All of them are bronze, tin, iron, and lead in the middle of the furnace. They are the dross of silver.” Have you ever watched someone fry fish? After the fish is fried, a layer of black burnt bits is left at the bottom of the pan, and no matter how you scrape, you cannot get it clean. There is something like that in the silversmith’s furnace too. When silver melts, the impurities float on top, and the craftsman skims them off with a ladle. But God says that this time the whole workshop is dross, and not one decent piece of silver can be found.
@@9782@@
This sentence is heavy. We must first hold on to one thing: this is God’s diagnosis of the condition of Jerusalem at that time, not a declaration of anyone’s worth. We do not use “dross” to describe any person today, any kind of people, or any group. People are made by God and carry the image of God. What the text says is that, in the silversmith’s eyes, the house of Israel had become dross. Please notice that it is “to me”: God is the silversmith, and he is still looking. He has not turned and walked away.
@@9783@@
In verses 19 to 22, the verbs of the fire follow one another: gather, blow the fire, melt, pour out. God gathers people into Jerusalem like gathering metal into a furnace, blowing the fire, and melting. As we read this, our hearts tighten. But please look at one more thing: the furnace is not there to destroy the silver; the furnace is the place where dross and silver are separated. This chapter stops at the diagnosis and does not speak of the cleansing that comes later; that has to wait for later chapters. We need not rush ahead, nor pretend there is comfort here; we only honestly say: this passage hurts.
@@9784@@
There was a brother whose business failed, and in church someone told him: this is God refining you. He said that this was the sentence he could least bear. At that time he did not want to be refined at all; he just wanted someone to sit with him. The fire of the furnace in chapter 22 is a judgment on a city, not an explanation for every person who suffers. If someone is suffering, seriously ill or bereaved, this passage is not there to confirm his pain.
@@9785@@
My situation is very hard right now. Is God refining me with fire?
@@9786@@
Not necessarily, and there is no need to hurry to find an answer for your own suffering. The fire of the furnace in chapter 22 is God’s judgment on Jerusalem at that time; it is a description of the condition of a group, not an explanation for every sufferer. Elsewhere the Bible treats trials as a process that makes people more like Christ, but that is in other passages, and cannot be carried over directly. If you are in a hard place, you can tell God: I do not know why this is happening, but I need you. Then find someone willing to sit with you. If suffering has left you without strength to live, please also seek help from a pastor and from professionals.

III. Light from the Original: Blood, Dross, Gap (22:2, 18, 30)
@@9788@@
There are three words in this chapter that are easy to miss without the original. The first is “blood,” דָּם (dam). In this chapter it often appears in the plural. In verse 2, “the bloody city” is עִיר הַדָּמִים in the original, literally “the city of bloods.” Plural blood is not the blood of one person; it is blood accumulated drop by drop. It means: this city did not have one murder; the everyday life of the whole city had grown used to hurting people. This phrase can ask us a question within thirty seconds: is there a “habit” around me that makes someone bleed every day, and I do not notice it?
@@9789@@
The second is “dross,” סִיג (sig), verse 18. This word refers to the impurities that float up when silver is melted. What is interesting is that it appears in the same verse as “silver” (כֶּסֶף), where God says: “They are the dross of silver.” We can see that beside the dross is silver; the craftsman is looking at the dross with the eyes of “silver.” God sees the dross, and remembers that this was originally silver. This is not defending sin; it is a reminder that God is a silversmith, not a garbage collector.
@@9790@@
The third is “gap,” פֶּרֶץ (peretz), verse 30. This word means a breach or a crack, referring to a hole knocked in a wall. The verb in this verse is וָאֲבַקֵּשׁ (va-avaqqesh): I sought. God says: “I sought for a man among them who would build up the wall and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I would not destroy it; but I found no one.” Please look at this verb: “sought.” This is a God who still wants to keep people, searching everywhere for a reason.
@@9791@@
Put the three words together and you have this chapter: blood (dam) is the city’s everyday life, dross (sig) is God’s diagnosis, and the gap (peretz) is God’s last searching. The words of judgment are spoken for twenty-nine verses, and the last two verses are a quiet verb.
@@9792@@
Does “I found no one” in “I sought for a man” mean that God is powerless, or that God shows mercy only when someone waits for him?
@@9793@@
No. What the text presents is God’s heart: he wants to keep people, so he seeks; that he found no one is the people’s gap, not God’s powerlessness. God’s mercy was already great; it does not begin only when someone steps forward. Please note that “that I would not destroy it” are God’s own words, which shows that he had long been looking for a reason not to destroy. When we read this verse, we should come to know God’s heart better, not worry on God’s behalf. You can pray like this: Lord, you have always been searching; thank you for not giving up on this city, and for not giving up on me. Then be quiet for a moment, let this sentence rest in your heart, and do not rush to do anything.
@@9794@@
IV. Every Level and the Gap (22:23–31)
@@9795@@
From verse 23, the camera pulls back. God says to the land: “You are a land that is not cleansed, nor rained on in the day of indignation.” Then he calls out in order: the prophets, the priests, the princes, the people. This list is not for ranking, and not for pointing out others, but to say: every level has left its position.
@@9796@@
The prophets: verse 25 says they are like a roaring lion ravening the prey, devouring souls, and making many widows in the land. Verse 28 says that the prophets plastered for the people with whitewash, seeing false visions, saying, “The Lord Yahweh says,” when Yahweh has not spoken. When I was young, I watched a craftsman plaster a wall: the plaster has to be soaked through first, and then it holds when applied. A wall with plaster that was not soaked through looks just as white on a sunny day, but when it rains, it blisters and peels. Words that did not come from God, however pleasant they sound, cannot stand up to a rain. This verse is for those on the pulpit to examine themselves: what I say, is it what the text says, or what I say?
@@9797@@
The priests: verse 26 says they did violence to the law, and made no distinction between the holy and the common, and caused men to be unable to discern between the unclean and the clean. The princes: verse 27 says they are like wolves, getting dishonest gain. The people: verse 29 says they oppress without ceasing, troubling the poor and needy, and wrongfully oppressing the foreigner. You see, the last group comes back again to the fatherless and widow, the poor and needy, and the foreigner. The whole list runs from the most powerful to the least powerful; at every position, someone has left his position.
@@9798@@
Then, in verse 30, God says the quietest sentence in the whole chapter: “I sought for a man among them who would build up the wall and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I would not destroy it; but I found no one.” This is not asking everyone to carry a whole city. This is God’s heart, not the debt of each one of us. That he found no one is the people’s gap, not God’s powerlessness. Please notice that God says “before me”: the one who stands in the gap stands before God. This is intercession.
@@9799@@
When Christians turn back and read this verse, they see Jesus. He is the one who truly stood in the gap, not outside the wall, but under the blade, shielding those who did not deserve it. Our intercession today is a participation in him, not making up for what he lacks. The list of people has been laid open, and God’s heart has been laid open; the gap among people is still there, and another’s body is already standing there. The original meaning of the text and the reading Christians give looking back must be kept distinct, and also connected.
@@9800@@
The end of verse 31 is still judgment: “I have poured out my indignation on them.” The chapter stops here. Grace is yet to unfold in later chapters; we need not rush ahead of it here, nor pretend that this chapter has no weight. We only need to remember: God’s last action is in verse 30, not in verse 31; he seeks first, and then judges.
@@9801@@
So many people in this chapter are guilty. How do I avoid always blaming others and forgetting myself?
@@9802@@
You can use the question in verse 28 to examine yourself: what I say, is it what the text says, or what I say? The list of prophets, priests, princes and people is not for counting others, but for every person to return to his own position. Those on the pulpit examine themselves first; elders at home look at whether they are oppressing; those in business look at whether they have dishonest gain; each person looks at whether he has forgotten God. This week you can say one specific thing to God: Lord, in which position have I left my place? Then change only one thing, not ten.
@@9803@@
The first deviation is to preach verse 30 as “God needs you to carry a whole city alone,” making listeners feel guilty and exhausted. This verse speaks of God’s heart, not each person’s debt. The test: after listening, do people love the Lord more, or do they feel more that “it is all because I have not tried hard enough”? If the latter, it needs to change. We want people to find rest from God’s searching, not to be called out.
@@9804@@
The second deviation is to use “dross” as a label for any modern person, group or nation, or to say that people have no worth. This is God’s diagnosis of the condition of the exile and of Jerusalem at that time. The test: does the sermon point at some kind of people and say “they are just dross”? If so, delete it. We must also say at the same time: people are created, and are sought by God.
@@9805@@
The third deviation is to magnify the pictures of family sins in verses 10 and 11, or to preach at the victims, or to ask the wounded to “stand in the gap” and intercede for the one who harmed them. Those verses are God speaking up for the wounded. The test: after listening, is the wounded person protected, or is his wound opened again and is he told to be strong? We want the wounded to be accompanied first.
@@9806@@
The fourth deviation is to preach the fire of the furnace as “you are suffering now, so God is punishing you,” or to preach God’s hand-strike as gloating, or to preach only judgment and not the purpose of “you will know that I am Yahweh.” The fire of the furnace is judgment on Jerusalem at that time, not an explanation for every sufferer; the hand-strike is a sigh of grief. We want people to see God’s heart, not only God’s anger.
@@9807@@
The fifth deviation is to use verse 28 to attack some pastor or speaker as a “false prophet,” or to treat this chapter as predicting judgment on some modern city or country, or to draw up a timetable, or to use fear to push people to stand in the gap. This verse is for those on the pulpit to examine themselves, not a weapon for naming people. We want people to come closer to the Lord and to be more patient, not more anxious and suspicious.
@@9808@@
I saw someone in my church say untrue things from the pulpit. What should I do?
@@9809@@
Do not rush to name anyone, and do not talk behind his back. Verse 28 is for those on the pulpit to examine themselves; it is not a weapon for others to identify false prophets. You can first compare it with the whole Bible; then gently ask the person privately: what is the basis for this sentence? If it involves major doctrine or harm, take it to the church’s pastoral co-workers, and examine it together. The purpose of the whole process is to come closer to the Lord, not to live in suspicion. At the same time, please ask yourself first: can the words I have said stand up to such a comparison?
@@9810@@
The first problem: we are very used to “it is none of my business.” An elder at home plays favorites, someone at the company is bullied, someone in the church is isolated; we see it, but think: this is not my business. The gap in verse 30 of chapter 22 often appears just like this: it is not that no one saw, but that everyone thought someone else would repair it. You can begin like this: this week, for one person you have seen but never spoken to, do one very small thing, send a message and ask: are you all right? There is no need to solve anything; just let him know that someone has seen.
@@9811@@
The second problem: we are very used to “saying what God has not said, for the sake of harmony.” An elder asks about your future, and you say: God will surely bless you; a friend meets a difficulty, and you say: this is all God’s good will. The intention is kind, but verse 28 reminds us: saying what God has not said as “The Lord Yahweh says” is a grave matter. You can examine yourself like this: next time before I say “God says,” pause and ask myself: did the text say this, or do I wish it were so? It is all right to say you are not sure. To say “I do not know, but I am with you” is also honest.
@@9812@@
In Chinese churches, speaking too directly is avoided for fear of hurting harmony. Does this conflict with “not saying what God has not said”?
@@9813@@
No conflict. Not hurting harmony and not speaking falsehood can be done at the same time. What verse 28 opposes is hanging one’s own opinion on God’s name. You can still be gentle, and you can still listen first and keep company first. You can say: my own view is..., I am not sure whether this is God’s will, let us pray together. Such words are more honest, and safer, than a sentence “God says.” For church leaders, this is an even more important reminder to themselves: better to say “I do not know” than to say your own intention as God’s word. Such honesty will slowly build the trust of brothers and sisters in you.
@@9814@@
VII. Living Out 321: Standing in the Gap Is Participation in Him
@@9815@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the one who truly stood in the gap; on the cross, he took the blade for those who did not deserve it, and when he was nailed there he also said: “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34). Those who follow him are not there to prove they have tried hard enough, but to learn from him: first to see people, and then to stand before the Father for them.
@@9816@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 22 not to list who most deserves judgment, but to let God’s word first question ourselves: is there a habit around me that hurts someone every day? What I say, is it what the text says, or what I say? The standard of verse 30 is this: God is seeking, not looking for someone to carry it for him; he is looking for someone willing to stand before him.
@@9817@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lets us see the gaps around us, and it is the Holy Spirit who tells us, this is the position I can stand in today. We do not need to stand in every gap, only the one the Holy Spirit points out to us. Every day, take a quiet moment and ask: Lord, today, who around me needs me to stand in front of him for a while?
@@9818@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of two attitudes: “someone else will repair it” and “only I can repair it.” A person who lets Jesus be King does not push it onto others, and does not take it all on himself; he knows that the one who truly stood in the gap has already stood there, and he is only following, in him, to stand for a short stretch.
@@9819@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If at home, at work or in the church we have stood for one person once, let us not say “I saved him.” All the glory goes to the Lord who stood in the gap. If we have not stepped forward, let us not say “I can do nothing,” because he has already stood first, and from here we can begin again.
@@9820@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is each person returning to his position: those on the pulpit speak what the text says, those in power do not oppress, those with strength look after the fatherless, the widow and the foreigner, and where there is a gap, someone steps out in him. First life is renewed, then relationships are restored, and afterward ministry is entrusted; such a fellowship is a small, living wall of God on the earth.
@@9821@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “standing in the gap”?
@@9822@@
The relationship is very direct. When people want to repair a gap, there are often two postures: one is “I do not care,” and the other is “it is all my responsibility.” Both put oneself in the position of king. Letting Jesus be King is to admit: the one who truly stood in the gap is he, and I am only following him to stand for a short stretch. Then I do not have to carry a burden I cannot carry, and I do not have to stand by with my hands folded. Every morning you can say three sentences to Jesus: what I most want to push onto others today; what I most want to take on myself today; and today please tell me which gap I should stand in.
@@9823@@
The proud type sees a gap and thinks: “This is their problem; I would never do that.” The text says to him: prophets, priests, princes, people; no level can push it onto another. Pride is leaving man’s position and grabbing God’s authority, wanting to take God’s place: either standing in another’s position to put him on trial, or thinking one can take God’s place in repairing the gap. Returning to our position is admitting: I am on the list too.
@@9824@@
The fearful type reads “I found no one” and is very afraid: “If I step out, will I get dragged in? If I do not stand, is it that the one God was looking for did not appear?” God’s word does not push you to stand; it tells you his heart. The fearful type can take the smallest step first: pray for one person for one minute, and then see where the Holy Spirit leads you next.
@@9825@@
The shame type hears “dross” and lowers his head: “So I am the dross.” Please pause. Verse 18 speaks of the condition of the house of Israel at that time, not of your worth. God is the silversmith; he sees the dross, and he also sees the silver. The shame type does not have to make himself good first before daring to be sought by God; you are the very one he is looking for.
