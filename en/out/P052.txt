I. The Bow Struck Down, Falling in the Open Field (39:1–8)
@@17745@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever watched a tug-of-war in which the rope suddenly snaps at the last moment? A second before, both sides are straining, faces red, heels dug into the ground; the next second, a whole row of people fall backward in a heap. The strangest thing is that no one was hit and no one was pushed; the rope they were leaning on was simply gone. The opening of chapter thirty-nine is like this picture. The first thing God says to Gog is the very thing he already said in chapter thirty-eight: “Behold, I am against you.”
@@17746@@
Please notice those verbs. Verse 2 says: I will turn you around, and will lead you on, and will cause you to come up from the uttermost parts of the north; and I will bring you onto the mountains of Israel. The subject of every one of these verbs is God. Gog thought he was the one leading an army out to war, but every turn of his road was arranged for him by someone else; he thought he was going to seize prey, but in fact he was being brought. A person who does not know who holds the rope that pulls him will take being pulled along for his own ambition.
@@17747@@
Verse 3 goes on: I will strike your bow out of your left hand, and will cause your arrows to fall out of your right hand. The judgment begins with the hands. What he relied on most is taken away, one thing after another, and he does not even get to draw the bow. Verses 4 and 5 say that he and his army will fall, and the text states the reason in one sentence: “for I have spoken it.” This chapter does not ask us to linger on the picture; it asks us to linger on this sentence: what God has spoken, God himself watches over. Who Gog is, and when, has been read in different ways down through the years. Some say it has already been fulfilled in history, some say it is a matter of the last days, and some say he is a symbol of every power that opposes the people of God. We set them side by side and respect one another; we do not name any modern nation, and we do not set any timetable.
@@17748@@
Verses 6 to 8 put the weight on a name. God says: “I will make my holy name known among my people Israel. I won’t allow my holy name to be profaned any more.” His holy name was profaned because his people were taken into exile, and the nations looked at them and said: their God could not save them. So this falling is not to let off steam; it is so that people may know him again: he is Yahweh, the Holy One in Israel. If you ask what this passage has to do with you, the answer is: is there a “bow” in your hand that you have always thought could never be taken away?
@@17749@@
A friend told me that when he was young he started a business, and he had a very fine client list in his hand. He said that was his bow. Later the company ran into trouble, and overnight the list was of no use. He said that for that month he discovered, for the first time, that he had been putting his sense of security in the list and not in God. When the bow is struck down it hurts very much. But sometimes that is the first time we see what we have been holding in our hands. This chapter does not want us to be afraid; it wants us to see: what he has spoken will not fail, and his name will not be trampled.
@@17750@@
Who exactly is Gog? Must I settle that first before I can read this chapter?
@@17751@@
No. This chapter has traditionally been read in three main ways: some regard it as something already fulfilled in history, some regard it as an actual battle that will take place in the last days, and some regard Gog and Magog as symbols of every power that opposes the people of God. All three readings respect the text, and all three hope for God’s victory. These notes do not name any modern nation and do not set any timetable. You can put the question aside for now and ask only what the text itself wants us to see: God’s word will be fulfilled (39:5), and he acts for the sake of his holy name (39:7). If after reading you love the Lord more and have more peace, you have read it rightly; if you fear the world more, please slow down.
@@17752@@
II. Firewood, Burial, and the Feast (39:9–20)
@@17753@@
The picture in verses 9 and 10 is very quiet. The people of the cities of Israel go out, not to fight, but to gather. Gather what? Shields and bucklers, bows and arrows, war clubs and spears. These were things made to hurt people, and now they are made into fires. The text even says they will take no wood out of the field and cut down none in the forests, because the weapons are enough to burn. What once made people afraid ends up as the fire in the stove. As for the number “seven years,” different schools read it in different ways; we do not use it to set dates or calculate years, and we keep only one impression: this is long, something that has to be burned slowly until it is done. Every one of us probably has a drawer at home full of old things we do not know whether to throw away. One day you make up your mind, take them out one by one, and discover that for many of them, throwing them away is actually a relief.
@@17754@@
Verses 11 to 16 are about the burying. There are bones on the ground; some have to bury them, some have to search, and when a man sees a bone he sets up a sign beside it and waits for the undertakers to come. This is not something done quietly by one person. The text says “all the people of the land will bury them,” so everyone does it together. Nor is it done in a day: after seven months, they still have to search. I think of a neighborhood parking lot after a heavy rain. The water has gone down and left a layer of mud. The caretaker takes a hose and washes it down space by space, finishing one and then moving to the next, and no one says: hurry, if we can’t see it, that’s good enough. Cleansing is like this: slow, careful, and it takes many people.
@@17755@@
Verses 17 to 20 are the feast. God calls the birds of the air and the beasts of the field to gather and eat the flesh of the mighty men. This section is very heavy, and I will not magnify any picture. If your heart tightens as you hear this, you can pause, and you do not have to force yourself to find it beautiful. I will keep only one sentence: at this table, the host is God, not man. The horses and chariots people once relied on turn out not to be the last thing to rely on. Judgment belongs to God, not to us, and we do not have to make this feast livelier in our hearts on his behalf.
@@17756@@

@@17757@@
You can stop. This passage is heavy to begin with, and being uncomfortable does not mean you are unspiritual; it means your heart is honest. If you have been through violence, or the loss of a loved one, or have seen a picture you cannot forget, you do not have to go back and look at it again today. You can do just one thing: tell God this unease just as it is. He does not need you to tidy it up first, or to say it beautifully. After that, find someone you trust and say only one sentence: I read a passage and my heart is troubled; can you sit with me for a while? You do not need to tell the whole story. Later in this chapter there is a “now,” and we will walk there together. If the distress lasts for many days, please do not carry it alone; find a pastor or a professional counselor.

III. Now, I Will Not Hide My Face Any More (39:21–29)
@@17759@@
From verse 21, the camera pulls back. God says: “I will set my glory among the nations.” The nations see the judgment he has executed, and from that day forward the house of Israel will know that he is Yahweh their God. Verses 23 and 24 put it very plainly: the nations will know that the house of Israel went into captivity for their iniquity; they trespassed against me, and I hid my face from them. This was said to a covenant people who broke the covenant; it was not said to any suffering individual. Please do not say “hiding the face” to someone who has lost a loved one or is seriously ill, as if to say: this is because of you. That is not what this verse means.
@@17760@@
Then verse 25 has two words that are the real turning point of this chapter: “Now.” “Therefore the Lord Yahweh says: ‘Now I will reverse the captivity of Jacob, and have mercy on the whole house of Israel. I will be jealous for my holy name.’” The earlier sentence is hiding; the later is mercy. The earlier is captivity; the later is return. The text does not stay long at the hiding; as soon as he opens his mouth, he turns. God’s face turned away, but not to turn away forever.
@@17761@@
Verses 26 to 28 describe what it looks like after the return: they dwell securely in their land, and no one will make them afraid; they bear their shame; he gathers them back to their own land, and will not leave one of them behind among the nations. I think of a tour guide on a bus. When it is time to leave, he does not ask, “Is everyone here?” He takes his list and calls the names one by one. If one is missing, the bus does not go. “Not one left” is this kind of roll call. If you feel you are the one left behind at the end, please hear this sentence. What he counts is not your achievements; it is your name.
@@17762@@
Finally, verse 29 says: “I won’t hide my face from them any more; for I have poured out my Spirit on the house of Israel.” Notice that “have.” This is not a promise to be won by human effort; it is something he says he has already done. The judgment is settled, and the return and the pouring out of the Spirit are settled. Grace is not a reward after people behave well; it is a fact after he has spoken.
@@17763@@
God says he “hid his face.” Does that mean he leaves us whenever he is angry?
@@17764@@
No. Verses 23 and 24 speak of the history of the covenant community’s captivity and explain why it happened; they do not say that he is a God who walks away in a moment of anger. Look at verse 25: he goes straight on to say “Now,” and that he will bring them back, have mercy, and be jealous for his holy name. More importantly, verse 29 says he will hide his face no more. If you are feeling that God has cast you aside, tell him just as it is, because the last word of this chapter is not hiding, but no more hiding. For someone who is suffering, do not use this passage to explain his suffering, and do not say, “You must have done something wrong.” Being with him matters more than explaining.
@@17765@@
IV. Light from the Original Language: Cleanse, Holy, Pour Out (39:12, 7, 29)
@@17766@@
There are three original words in this chapter worth a close look. The first is “cleanse” in verse 12, the Hebrew טָהֵר. The text says the house of Israel will bury them for seven months, “that they may cleanse the land.” This word is not bleaching, and not covering up the dirty; it is finding things one by one and dealing with them one by one. Verse 14 says they will still “search,” searching even after seven months. Here is the thirty-second transform: if there is an old matter in your heart that is not yet dealt with, you have not failed; cleansing simply needs searching. Slow does not mean not begun.
@@17767@@
The second is “holy” in verse 7, the Hebrew קָדוֹשׁ. The text says: I am Yahweh, the Holy One in Israel. The word means set apart, different from everything else. For his name to be profaned means that people see him as no different from other gods. So when God says he will “make my holy name known,” it is not self-centredness; it is so that people may see that he is truly different. Different in what way? He can strike down Gog’s bow, and he can also bring the captives home one by one. Holy is not far away; holy is dependable.
@@17768@@
The third is “pour out” in verse 29, the Hebrew שָׁפַךְ. The basic meaning of the word is to pour out, to empty everything from the cup. It has another use in Ezekiel: in 36:18, the same word is used for pouring out wrath. One word, two ways of pouring. Now, what he pours out of the cup in his hand is not wrath, but his Spirit. If you feel you have already been emptied out, please know that another hand is holding another cup.
@@17769@@
These three words have one more thing in common: the subject of the action is not us. The order of cleansing is set by him; the guarding of the holy name is done by him; the pouring out of the Spirit is something he has spoken. We often want to do a little more for God, but this chapter first asks us to learn to stand in our place and let him do it. An elderly sister said that it was only after seventy that she learned that prayer is not telling God what to do, but asking him to put his hand on her back.
@@17770@@
Put the three words together, and this is the order of the chapter: cleansing is something he does slowly together with people; holiness is something he guards for his own name; the pouring out is his Spirit poured from the cup onto people. People only need to stand under his cup; they do not need to make a cup of their own.
@@17771@@
Does the “Spirit poured out” require me to have some kind of feeling to count? I do not have that feeling; is it not enough?
@@17772@@
No. Verse 29 says “I have poured out my Spirit.” The subject is God, and the action is his; it is not an achievement that people obtain by some method. The understanding of this verse is not uniform: some see it as fulfilled at the return, some as realized after Pentecost, and some as having a part not yet complete. We set them side by side and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. The shared center is this: God does not hide his face any more, and the Spirit is poured out by him personally. If you feel weak, even spiritually dry, this sentence is an invitation, not an examination. You can say every day: Lord, you said you have poured out your Spirit; I am willing to stand under your cup.
@@17773@@
The first deviation is to use chapter thirty-nine to point at some modern nation or people and say: they are Gog, and they will fall. This is labeling, and it manufactures hostility. What the text wants us to see is God winning for the sake of his own name, not our choosing targets on his behalf. If the name of a modern nation or people appears in a sermon, please delete it. We want the congregation to love the Lord more and have more peace, not to fear others more.
@@17774@@
The second deviation is to use “seven years” or “seven months” to set a timetable, to calculate years and count days. Different schools read these in different ways, and the text does not ask anyone to calculate. Teaching this way makes people anxious about “how many years are left” and forget that the point of this chapter is that the cleansing is thorough and done by everyone together. We want the congregation to wait with more patience, not to calculate with more anxiety.
@@17775@@
The third deviation is to describe the feast in vivid detail, or to treat it as encouragement to “look forward to the enemy’s fall.” For those who have been through violence or the loss of loved ones, this is a second wound. The point of the text is that strength is not the last thing to rely on before God, and judgment belongs to God. We want the wounded to be protected first, not frightened by pictures.
@@17776@@
The fourth deviation is to say to those who have lost loved ones or been hurt by violence: be strong, forgive, look forward. Burying and cleansing are by nature long, careful work done by many together, and no one can set a deadline for another. We want people to be accompanied, not hurried.
@@17777@@
The fifth deviation is to take the “hid my face” of verses 23 and 24 and turn it into “your suffering is because of your sin.” That was said to a covenant community taken into exile, not to any suffering individual; and the text goes straight on to say “Now,” that he will bring them back and have mercy. We want people to hear his turning, and not only the hiding.
@@17778@@
How can a pastor or small-group leader teach this chapter without labeling anyone and without frightening people?
@@17779@@
First ask three questions. First, does the sermon contain the name of any modern nation or people? If so, delete it. Second, does it contain years, dates, or calculations? If so, delete them. Third, does it magnify the pictures? If so, protect the listeners first, and then go on. Then put the ending on the word “Now”: he does not hide his face any more, he pours out his Spirit. After you finish, you can leave one minute of silence and not rush to close. If someone comes to you privately after the meeting and says they thought of their own wound, do not press for details, and do not urge them to be strong; sit with them for a while, and if needed help them find professional counseling.
@@17780@@
The first question: we are used to saying “it’s past, don’t bring it up again.” Elders often say: it’s all over, don’t think about it any more. That sentence has love in it, but it often turns into a push to hurry. The burying in 39:11–15 is careful, long, and done by many together: some search, some set up markers, some bury the bones in the valley. You can begin like this: choose one old matter you have never dared to touch, write only one sentence, then choose one person you trust and say to him: I need someone with me; I do not have to tell the whole story. The one who accompanies does not need to hurry to give reasons; he only needs to say: I am here.
@@17781@@
The second question: we are used to the idea of retribution. When we see others fall, we say heaven’s justice is plain; when we meet suffering ourselves, we ask: did I do something wrong? The “hiding” in this chapter was said to a covenant community, not to find a cause for every sufferer. You can practice like this: the next time someone meets misfortune, do not ask for the cause first; say only one sentence: I am with you. And when the voice in your own heart says “is it my fault?”, bring it before God and hear him say: Now, I will not hide my face any more.
@@17782@@
Chinese families often say “family shame must not be spread abroad.” How do I begin to talk about an old wound?
@@17783@@
Start with the smallest thing. You do not have to say it at a family gathering, and you do not have to write a long piece. Find a brother or sister you trust, who will not pass it around, and say one sentence first: I have something I would like you to pray for me about; you do not need to know the details. If he understands, he will say yes, and keep it confidential. Then you can set a regular time, ten minutes a week, and say a little at a time. Like burying, a little each time; do not aim to finish all at once. If the matter involves safety or abuse, or gives you thoughts of harming yourself, please find a pastor or a professional counselor quickly, and do not carry it alone. Not spreading family shame is to protect the family; letting one reliable person know is to protect you.
@@17784@@
VII. Living Out 321: Those Who Are Led Back
@@17785@@
Jesus is my Role Model. On the cross he said: Father, forgive them. He did not take up a bow, and he did not call the birds and the beasts. He handed the judgment to the Father and kept forgiveness for people. Those who follow him learn his way: not taking God’s vengeance for him, and not taking revenge for themselves; handing the hurt into God’s hands, and at the same time asking forgiveness for those who hurt us.
@@17786@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter thirty-nine not to piece together a picture of the end times, but to let the text finish speaking for itself: he has spoken, and he has done it (39:5); he hid his face, and he also says “Now” (39:25); he has poured out his Spirit (39:29). If the way we teach makes people fear the world more, then it is not this chapter. If it makes people love the Lord more and have more peace, it fits the text.
@@17787@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. Verse 29 says the Spirit is poured out by him, not an achievement we obtained by asking. So we do not compare whose spiritual power is enough; we only say one sentence quietly each day: Lord, your Spirit is within me; lead me today to take one smallest step of obedience.
@@17788@@
Let Jesus be King is to lay down the “bow” in our hands (39:3). Each of us has a bow in our hands: education, connections, eloquence, savings, temper. These are not bad things; only when they become the last thing we rely on do they have to be handed over. A person who lets Jesus be King no longer relies on his own bow and arrows to protect himself; his safety is in his hand.
@@17789@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory is to let his holy name be honored as holy (39:7). If we have been led back, let us not say: I finally made it through. All the glory belongs to the Lord who made the captives return and does not leave one of them behind among the nations. Our story is a witness to his name.
@@17790@@
To build God’s system is a community of people led back one by one (39:25–28). This community buries together and searches together: some accompany, some set up markers, some do not hurry. Life first, the Spirit poured out; then relationships, cleansing and accompanying one another; then ministry, so that the nations know he is Yahweh. A fellowship like this is a small piece of God’s system on earth.
@@17791@@
What is the connection between “Let Jesus be King” and “lay down the bow in our hands”?
@@17792@@
Verse 3 says God will strike down Gog’s bow. Gog’s problem is not that he had a bow, but that he thought the bow was his guarantee. To let Jesus be King is to admit: my real guarantee is not in my hand. You can practice like this: each morning write down the one thing you rely on most today. It may be your eloquence, your plans, your income. Then say one sentence: Lord, I put these in your hands for now; if you take them away, I still have you. This is not telling you to stop working or stop planning; it lets you know that a bow can be struck down, but a person who has been led back will not be abandoned.
@@17793@@
The proud type says: “My bow is strong enough; I don’t need to be led back, and I don’t need to be cleansed.” The text says to him: I will strike your bow out of your left hand. Pride is leaving one’s position and grasping God’s authority, even wanting to carry out vengeance on God’s behalf with one’s own hands. To return to one’s position is to admit: judgment belongs to him, and the return belongs to him too. First say one sentence to God: “Lord, I give my bow to you.”
@@17794@@
The fearful type says: “If his judgment is this heavy, what will I do?” The text lets him see that after the judgment there is a “Now”; he speaks of mercy, of return, of no more hiding. The fearful type needs to learn to trade “afraid of being judged” for “being led back.” You can begin with one very small thing: loosen your clenched hand a little.
@@17795@@
The ashamed type says: “What I have done, even if it is buried, I cannot get past it myself.” Verse 26 says they will bear their shame, but before that, he has first led them back. Shame says “you do not deserve to be led back”; the gospel says “I will not leave one of them behind among the nations.” The ashamed type does not need to tidy himself up first; he only needs to stand today in his roll call.
@@17796@@
The wounded type says: “I have been through great loss, and I do not want to look at the pictures in this chapter again.” You do not have to look. He is not asking you to go back and look again; he simply sees you. A wounded person can slowly find someone who truly loves you and say what you are willing to say. The one who accompanies should not hurry to say “you must forgive”; just say: “I am here, and I am with you.”
@@17797@@
First, each day take one minute to say to God: “You do not hide your face any more; lead me back.” No need to polish it; say just as it is the feeling of being cast aside today, and write down the date.
@@17798@@
Second, write down one old matter you have never dared to touch, in just one sentence; then write another sentence: “I do not have to do it alone,” and write down the name of the person you are willing to tell.
@@17799@@
Third, find someone who is in loss or grief, and say to him only one sentence: “I am with you.” Give no advice and no reasoning; say his name and the time.
@@17800@@
Fourth, sort out one long-delayed pile of clutter or one old object, as a small “cleansing of the land,” write down which day you did it, and when you finish, say thank you to God.
@@17801@@
Fifth, in your service, do one unseen “searching”: make a phone call to check on someone you have not contacted for a long time, asking nothing in return, and say his name.
@@17802@@
What is my smallest, most doable step this week, so that I do not stop at only listening to the sermon?
@@17803@@
The smallest step is to take a piece of paper today and write one sentence: The old matter that I need to “bury properly” is ______. You do not have to show it to anyone. Then say to God: Lord, you do not hide your face any more; please be with me. Next choose one small action you can do today: send a message to someone you trust and say, “Could you pray with me this week?” Or put one old object you have kept for a long time into the recycling bin. This action is small, yet it is the answer of chapter thirty-nine: cleansing does not begin with a great resolve; it begins with one search, one marker. At the next small-group meeting, just say: This week I did one thing, and I do not need to say what it was.
@@17804@@
I declare: God brought Gog down for the sake of his holy name; I will not take vengeance for him, and I will not use this chapter to label anyone.
@@17805@@
I declare: I give the bow in my hand to him; if he takes it away, I still have him.
@@17806@@
I declare: he says “Now,” and he does not hide his face from me; he will lead me back, and will not leave me behind among the nations.
@@17807@@
I declare: he has poured out his Spirit; I will not compare, I will not be anxious, and I will only open myself to him each day.
@@17808@@
The road of chapter thirty-nine is a road from falling to return. It begins with God saying: I am against you; Gog’s bow is struck down, and he falls in the open field. In the middle are the burying, the firewood, and the cleansing that everyone slowly does together. At the end comes a “Now”: he will reverse the captivity, have mercy on the whole house of Israel, hide his face no more, and pour out his Spirit. The story does not stop at Gog’s grave; it stops with those who are led back. If after reading this chapter today you remember only the picture of falling, please read the last verses again; the last sentence of the text is not falling, but the Spirit.
@@17809@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see his holy name, to see his care, and to see his turning. That God, in Jesus, came among us; on the cross he took up no bow and arrows, and yet prayed for those who hurt him. He is our Role Model, and he is also the Lord who leads people home one by one. Not one of us walked back by our own bow; he came himself to lead us.
@@17810@@
