@@2270@@
God will not share a seat with another god. In his temple there is no word “also.” There is no “he and so-and-so,” only him. This is the opposite of “Let Jesus be King”: people split the throne and give half of it to something else. Today we do not set idols up in the temple, but we have set up more refined ones: success at work, our children’s future, other people’s praise, that screen in the phone we cannot put down. They are not always bad things, but the moment they sit down on that chair, the temple is defiled.
@@2271@@
Verse 12 makes the three parts plain: one third by pestilence and famine, one third falling by the sword, one third scattered to every direction, and God drawing a sword after them. This takes us back to the three piles of hair in verse 1. But verse 13 has a sentence very few people notice: my wrath toward them will rest, and I will be comforted. God’s anger comes to rest; it does not burn forever. His anger has a purpose, and when it is accomplished, it stops. Then comes “zeal,” the very word we just looked at. Next to the judgment there is always a wounded heart standing by.
@@2272@@
Verses 14 and 15 say that Jerusalem will become a desolation and a reproach in the sight of the nations, a taunt, an instruction, and an astonishment. All who pass by will see it. This is the most heartbreaking part of the passage: the city that was set in the middle of the nations to be a mirror is still a mirror, only now what it reflects is a warning. But notice how verse 15 ends: “I, Yahweh, have spoken it.” He does not hide behind anyone. He openly owns it: I said it, I did it. He is not helpless; he is responsible.
@@2273@@
Verses 16 and 17 speak of evil arrows, famine, evil animals, pestilence, blood, and the sword. Among them is this line: “they will bereave you.” I must read those words carefully. We must not take them and say them to anyone who has lost a child. The text is describing where sin ends up; it does not stand at the door of every home that has lost a child and post a charge sheet. If you happen to be the one who is weeping, remember: this verse does not blame you. The God who swears by his own life later swears by the same life: “I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live” (33:11). The same life, sworn twice.
@@2274@@
God says, “My eye won’t spare, and I will have no pity.” Has he become cold and heartless?
@@2275@@
No. The “will not spare” of verse 11 means that he will no longer hold back to save face. He will say it all the way through and bring the judgment to completion. But please read verse 11 together with verse 13: his wrath comes to rest, he is comforted, and he says he has spoken in his zeal. Later, in 33:11, he swears by his own life again that he has no pleasure in the death of the wicked. So “not sparing” is aimed at the matter of idols sitting down in the sanctuary, not at the people themselves. If you read this passage and find God frightening, please do not stop here. Read on through the whole book. The purpose of his judgment was never destruction, but that people would turn and live.
@@2276@@
The first error is to take the “one third” of 5:2 and preach it as a ratio that can be calculated, matched to a certain year or event. The text does not give us that key, and interpreters read it differently. We want the congregation to fear God more and be more willing to turn back, not to become more eager to do arithmetic.
@@2277@@
The second error is to describe “fathers will eat their sons” in 5:10 in as much detail as possible, using the picture to shock the congregation. That is a picture of where sin ends up; it is not God’s delight. We touch on it lightly and do not enlarge it, because among those listening there may be someone who has lived through loss.
@@2278@@
The third error is to quote “they will bereave you” in verse 17 to someone who has lost a child, hinting that it is the result of his sin. That uses the text as a charge sheet. After the sermon, the one who has lost needs to be accompanied, not blamed. If you could not say it to someone who has just been crying, do not say it.
@@2279@@
The fourth error is to preach God’s “not sparing” as coldness and cruelty. Ezekiel 5:13 says at the same time that his anger comes to rest and that the judgment comes from his zeal, and 33:11 says he has no pleasure in the death of the wicked. We want the congregation to see both how serious he is and his wounded heart.
@@2280@@
The fifth error is to preach this chapter as a prophecy about a particular modern nation or group, or to use “a few of them in the fire” in verse 4 to frighten people about their assurance of salvation. The effect of this chapter should be to love the Lord more and to turn back more willingly, not panic. If a sermon contains any sentence that names a modern group, delete it.
@@2281@@
Sometimes when I read judgment chapters like this one, I become very afraid, and I even doubt whether I am saved at all. What should I do?
@@2282@@
First, this kind of fear is not always a bad thing, but it must not stop at fear. The “a few of them” in 5:3–4 tear down the false comfort of “I belong to God, so I do not need to turn back”; they do not take away a believer’s assurance. You can do three things. First, say your fear out loud and tell God: Lord, I am afraid. Second, find one verse of assurance and read it aloud. Third, ask yourself: does my fear push me toward him, or away from him? If this fear has already left you unable to sleep or eat, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please speak to a pastor or a professional as soon as possible. Do not carry it alone. You are not alone.
@@2283@@
The first question: we are used to thinking “if I do things, I am safe.” Going to church, giving, serving, reading Scripture, we keep a record of our own, as if, once we have saved up enough, nothing can happen to us. The hairs that were tucked into the edge of the garment in 5:4, and then some of them taken again and thrown into the fire, are a direct question to this way of thinking. You can examine yourself like this: this month, is there one thing I did for the sake of “being safe” rather than out of love for him? Find someone you trust and say it in one sentence, without defending yourself and without comparing. Seeing it is the beginning of turning back.
@@2284@@
The second question: we are used to “not airing the family’s dirty laundry,” and the deviation slowly becomes a habit in the home that no one dares to speak about. Ezekiel 5:7 says that she did not even keep the rules of the nations. In a family or a church, if everyone has seen it but everyone has quietly agreed not to speak, the habit becomes the quietest form of drifting away. You can start like this: this week, say one true sentence to a family member or co-worker you trust: “I feel that we have gotten used to something lately, and it is not quite right.” It is not an accusation, it is an invitation. Name your own one first, then ask about theirs.
@@2285@@
In a Chinese family, would saying “we have gotten used to something that is not quite right” damage the harmony?
@@2286@@
It may be uncomfortable for a while, but harmony is not the same as having no truth in it. This passage in chapter 5 reminds us that the quietest drifting often happens in the places where no one speaks. You can reduce the hurt like this: first, choose a quiet time when no one is in a hurry; second, name your own one first, not the other person’s; third, say only one thing and do not bring up old accounts; fourth, leave a little silence after you speak so the other person can respond. If the other person becomes angry, do not defend yourself. First say: I said this because I care. If the matter involves violence, abuse, or safety, please do not handle it alone, but first seek the help of a pastor and professionals.
@@2287@@
VII. Living Out 321: Only He Is in the Temple
@@2288@@
Jesus is my Role Model. The prophet shaved himself to be a sign for everyone; Jesus is the one who was truly stripped of everything. Isaiah says he was like a sheep silent before its shearers. He was judged, yet not for himself; he walked to the very end and still asked only for the Father’s will. Those who follow him learn not how to avoid the fire, but how to return to the Father even in the fire.
@@2289@@
The Bible is my Standard. Ezekiel 5:6 says she rejected my ordinances. We read this chapter not to analyze the proportions of judgment, but to let God’s word put back in our hands the sentence that we have weighed: is there a verse I know but have left on the shelf? The Bible is not a decoration; it is the standard. Doing what it says is the way to honor it.
@@2290@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. God says it comes from his zeal, and that is a wounded love. It is the Holy Spirit who makes us notice when another chair has appeared in our own temple. Be quiet for one minute each day and ask: Lord, today what is sitting in your place? The Holy Spirit will point it out, not to condemn us, but to lead us back.
@@2291@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of the self-assurance that says “I have ordinances and I serve, so I am safe,” and giving up every other chair in the temple. Letting Jesus be King does not mean he sits together with others; it means he sits alone. Selflessness is not having nothing; it is no longer clinging to the things that will be taken and burned, and entering the King who is never burned.
@@2292@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we are left behind and preserved, let us not say “I am better than others.” The hairs tucked into the edge of the garment were not tucked there because they were special, but because that hand could not bear to let them go. All the glory goes to the One who holds us close, and who does not give up even in the fire.
@@2293@@
Build God’s system. God placed Jerusalem among the nations so that she would be a witness to “living out God’s system.” God’s system is letting man be man and God be God: first life, then relationships, then ministry. If a family or a fellowship can remind one another that “only he is in the temple,” and can speak the truth without comparing or judging, that place becomes a small mirror God has set among the nations.
@@2294@@
“Only he is in the temple” sounds very lofty. How do I live it out in everyday life?
@@2295@@
Start from a very small place. First step: find your “chair.” Time, money, the screen, praise: which one most easily sits down in the place that belongs to God? Second step: do not kick it out all at once, but make the smallest adjustment first, for example, do not look at your phone while eating, and keep the first five minutes of the morning for him. Third step: say one sentence to him each day: Lord, come and sit. It does not have to sound very spiritual, and it does not have to go well every day. Habit is the quietest form of drifting away, so coming back also happens in the quietest and smallest steps. God looks at the direction, not at a single score. After a week, look back and you will find the place is slowly being cleared.
@@2296@@
The proud person says: “I have ordinances, I serve, I should be kept by God more than others.” The text says to him: the more you have received, the heavier the responsibility; even what is tucked into the edge of the garment must pass through the fire. The proud leave their position and grasp at power, deciding for God who is safe; returning to one’s position is admitting: I am also one hair, the one he could not bear to let go. You can begin by saying to God: “Lord, I am no better than others; I am only held by you.”
@@2297@@
The fearful person says: “This chapter is too frightening. I dare not read it, for fear I will be burned too.” His anger comes to rest, his judgment comes from zeal, and he swears by his own life that he has no pleasure in anyone’s death. What the fearful person needs to learn is not to be more afraid, but to draw near: read verse 13 first, then read 33:11. Exchange “afraid of being burned” for “held by a hand that could not bear to let go.”
@@2298@@
The shamed person says: “I have drifted away, I have thrown away his word, I have no right to go back.” Verse 6 speaks of that city’s choice; verse 13 speaks of his zeal. Shame says: you weighed him and then threw him aside; the gospel says: he still burns for your temple to this day, and will not hand you over to another god. The shamed do not have to clean themselves up first before coming back. Today, just say: “Lord, I have come back. Come and sit.”
@@2299@@
The wounded person says: “I lost someone very important to me, and this chapter seems to say that I deserved it.” No. This chapter is not speaking to you. If you are weeping, please do not read verse 17 for now. The wounded can slowly find someone to walk with them, say the name, and not have to give the details; the one who accompanies should not rush to say “You must have faith,” nor “You must forgive,” but only say: “I am here. I am with you.”
@@2300@@
First, every day take two minutes to be quiet and say to the Lord: “Lord, come and sit.” Write down the date, and do this for one week, one sentence a day, not looking for a feeling.
@@2301@@
Second, write down one deviation you have gotten used to (praying less, obeying less, hoping less). Only name it; do not blame yourself. Say which day this week you will bring it before God.
@@2302@@
Third, find a family member or co-worker and say one true sentence: “Lately we have gotten used to something, and I would like to start changing it from myself.” Say the person’s name and the date.
@@2303@@
Fourth, sort out one thing you have “put ahead of God” (time, the screen, money). Make the smallest adjustment first, and write down which day it is and how much you will adjust.
@@2304@@
Fifth, ask someone who is tired or has lost a loved one: “How can I be with you today?” Do not give answers and do not reason; just be with them. Say their name and the time.

@@2310@@
The one most easily left undone is often the third, saying a true word to family or co-workers, because we fear awkwardness and fear damaging the peace. You can lower the bar first: do not think “I must explain everything at once,” just say one sentence this week, and speak of yourself first. If you cannot say it out loud this week, you can write down the one sentence you want to say, pray for three days, and then find a suitable time. If two weeks in a row you have not done it, do not blame yourself and do not give up. Just say in the next small group: I am stuck here. That is another shape of turning back: not pretending you did it, only honestly saying where you are. He loves honest people.

Chapter 5 begins with a razor and ends with an oath by the living God. At the beginning, the prophet sits in front of everyone and shaves himself with an ordinary blade; in the middle there are three piles of hair, a pair of scales, and a few hairs tucked into the garment; at the end God swears by his own life that his eye will not spare, and also that his anger will come to rest, because he is zealous. @@2312@@

<p><strong>The prophet is told to speak to the mountains, because the people will not listen.</strong></p><p>After the blade and the hair of chapter 5, God changes his method: he no longer has the prophet act it out with his body, but tells him to turn around and speak to the mountains. “Set your face” (שִׂים פָּנֶיךָ) is a common prophetic gesture: the face is fixed in place, not looking elsewhere, until the words are finished.</p><p>The audience is the mountains. This arrangement is a little absurd, and also has a little depth. The mountains are where the people worshiped idols. The mountains are quieter than people, and also more honest: every altar on the mountains is evidence. People can pretend not to know what they have done; the mountains cannot.</p><p>Also, the prophet is in the land of captivity, far from those mountains. He cannot see them. He can only, by God’s word, speak to mountains that are not in front of him the words of a God who is in front of him. This is the other side of faith: to remain faithful in finishing the words to people and places you cannot see.</p><p>Landing: there are words we speak when we have no audience. To a child, to family far away, to someone who will not turn back. As long as it is God’s word, saying it is not wasted. The weight of the word is not in whether anyone listens, but in who is speaking.</p>

I. Turn Around and Face the Mountains (6:1–5)
@@2693@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever stood at the edge of a valley and shouted toward the mountain across from you? The first time I did this was on a very foggy morning. I shouted a “hey,” and a second later, the mountain threw that sound right back at me. At that moment I suddenly felt that the mountain had heard. Whatever I shouted, it gave back to me, not one word less. In today’s chapter, God has the prophet do something too: turn around, face the mountains, and speak to them. But what God wants the prophet to say is not “hey”; it is a declaration.
@@2694@@
First, the background. Chapter 5 has just ended, and the prophet sat in front of everyone and said very heavy words with a razor, scales, and three piles of hair. But the people still did not truly take it in. Ezekiel 6:1–2 says: Yahweh’s word came to me, saying, “Son of man, set your face toward the mountains of Israel, and prophesy to them.” God did not give up speaking; he changed the one he spoke to, and said it again. It is very much like a mother whose child lowers his head and will not listen: she does not close her mouth, she turns toward another direction and says it again, so that the child hears what is being said about him.
@@2695@@
Verse 3 says: “You mountains of Israel, hear the word of the Lord Yahweh! The Lord Yahweh says to the mountains and to the hills, to the watercourses and to the valleys.” Please notice this string of names: mountains, hills, watercourses, valleys. God does not speak only to the one highest mountain; he calls them one by one, and not even the lowest watercourse is left out. He says: “Behold, I, even I, will bring a sword on you, and I will destroy your high places.” High places are the places on the mountains where idols were worshiped. Why speak to the mountains? Because the mountains are witnesses. People can refuse to admit it, can say, I did not; but every altar on the mountains is right there, one after another, each one evidence.
@@2696@@
Why does God have the prophet prophesy to the mountains rather than to the people? Is it a figure of speech or real?
@@2697@@
The text does not explain, and scholars have three readings: one, a rhetorical turn, where the people will not listen, so God changes the object to the mountains and sharpens the accusation; two, a witness of place, where the mountains are the sites of idol worship and the altars on them are themselves the evidence; three, another symbolic action, like chapters 4 and 5, which does not need to match a real audience on the spot. These notes take this stand: the mountains are more honest than people. You can use it like this: do not rush to ask which reading is right; first see that God will not stop speaking. If you have spoken for a long time and no one has listened, do not be discouraged; God has spoken like that too. Their not listening is not your failure, and continuing to speak is not your achievement; you only need to be faithful. He knows better than you who is listening.
@@2698@@
Verses 4 and 5 say: Your altars will become desolate, and your incense altars will be broken. I will cast down your slain men before your idols; I will lay the dead bodies of the children of Israel before their idols. These are very heavy words, and we do not enlarge the picture. There is only one thing to see: people staked their security on things made by hand, and now those things will be pulled down before their eyes. The altar was built by people and seemed to be their last support. God says, this last support, I will pull down.
