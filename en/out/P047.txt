@@15788@@
The third distortion is to say to the one who has been hurt: God will surely repay, so just wait and watch. This leads people to set their hope on someone else’s disaster. The point of the text is “I have heard it.” After hearing it, the wounded person should be more sure that they have been heard, not more anxious to see someone fall.
@@15789@@
The fourth distortion is to press the wounded: you must forgive, you must be strong. This chapter does not say that. God speaks to Edom so that the hurt will know he has heard, not to hurry the hurt into getting better. Walking with someone matters more than correcting them. Let people be heard first, and then talk about the next step.
@@15790@@
The fifth distortion is to preach “rejoicing” as a sin that cannot be admitted, so that people suppress every feeling and do not even dare to speak of that small flicker inside. The text asks people to see honestly, and then hand it over to God. If a sermon leaves people so ashamed that they cannot speak, it has gone off course. If a sermon lets people say, “Lord, I saw it,” that is the right direction.
@@15791@@
I carry hatred in my heart toward the one who hurt me. Is this what “perpetual hostility” means?
@@15792@@
Not necessarily. There are two kinds of hatred. One is the pain that comes after being hurt. It shows up at night, it leaves you speechless, and it needs to be heard and given time. The other takes that pain and stores it up as a long-term house, and keeps waiting for that person to fall. This is the “perpetual” in verse 5. There is a simple way to tell them apart: when you think of that person, is it more “It hurts so much,” or “I want to see him fall”? The first needs company; the second needs letting go. You can bring both to God. You do not have to sort yourself out before you come to him. He will not shut the door because you are still hating.
@@15793@@
The first question: we are used to “don’t air the family’s dirty laundry,” yet we say it quietly behind people’s backs. Chinese society values face. Grudges between brothers are not spoken to the person’s face, but they get passed along, one sentence at a time, at the relatives’ dinner table and in group chat messages. The insults in verses 12 and 13 are spoken out in just this way. You can begin like this: this month, whose bad side have I spoken of behind their back? Ask yourself about just one name. You do not have to explain yourself to the whole world; only say to God: Lord, you heard this sentence. Then ask yourself one more thing: would I be willing to say the same words to his face?
@@15794@@
The second question: we are used to “comparing,” and when someone else stumbles, there is a tiny sigh of relief inside. Classmates, colleagues, relatives, co-workers are all our comparison group. A child’s exam, a promotion, a marriage: these occasions most easily make a person stir inside. Chapter 35 does not say this is a small matter; verse 15 puts the “rejoicing” on the record. You can examine yourself like this: this week, was there any piece of news that made something stir in my heart? Do not scold yourself. Just say honestly: Lord, I saw it. Then pray the shortest prayer for that person, without needing to feel anything.
@@15795@@
In the church I also feel secretly that I am better when another person’s ministry stumbles. What should I do?
@@15796@@
First admit it: this is a shape many people share, and it does not mean you are especially bad. Step one, say it out loud: Lord, I see that what rose up in my heart was comparison and relief. Step two, ask yourself: do I love the Lord more, or do I look down on people more? Step three, do one very small thing for that co-worker, for example send a word of encouragement and say: I see how hard you have worked. Serving is not a competition. Edom in chapter 35 treated his brother’s fall as his own ranking. If we can admit it first, that flicker will not grow into “perpetual” hostility. Please also notice that admitting it is not for blaming yourself, but for letting the light come in. What is touched by the light no longer needs to hide in order to grow.

VII. Living Out 321: Putting “He Deserved It” into His Hands
@@15798@@
Jesus is my Role Model. When he was crucified, he said, “Father, forgive them.” He did not seek vindication for himself, and he did not rejoice over anyone’s desolation. Chapter 35 speaks of God’s judgment on Edom, yet when Jesus faced those who persecuted him, he chose to pray. When we face the one who hurt us, we also learn from him: we do not carry it out for him; we hand that person over to the Father.
@@15799@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 35, not to find a mountain to blame, but to let the text look at us first. Verses 12 and 13 say, “I have heard it.” The standard of this chapter is: what I say, he hears; what rises in my heart, he also sees. Let this sentence be a pause before I open my mouth.
@@15800@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lights up that flicker in our heart when we hear that someone has lost position, and it is also the Holy Spirit who gives us the power not to follow it. The next chapter, chapter 36, says that God will put his Spirit within us. Today you can first say to him: Lord, I saw it; please lead me in the next step.
@@15801@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is to put down the calculation of “it must come to me,” and also to put down the breath of “he deserved it.” The seat of justice must be returned to the King, and we only go back to our own position. If he is King, I do not have to carry it out for him.
@@15802@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If I have been hurt by someone, and later I see God bring justice, I will not say, “See, I told you so.” All the glory goes to the Lord who heard. And if someone is set free from hatred, that is also his grace, not because we are so big-hearted.
@@15803@@
Build God’s system. In God’s system, brothers are not rivals. Life first, relationships next, ministry last: first let him change that flicker in my heart, then deal with my relationships with people, and only then talk about ministry. If a small group can say to one another, “I see the comparison in my heart,” and everyone hands it over to him together, that is a small God’s system. The brothers in it do not calculate against one another; they intercede for one another.
@@15804@@
What does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with “that flicker in my heart when someone else falls”?
@@15805@@
The relationship is direct. That flicker is my little throne: I am comparing, I am calculating, I am working out who is higher than me and who is lower. To let Jesus be King is to return this seat to him. You can practice like this: when you hear someone’s bad news, first pause for three seconds. Do not rush to speak, and do not rush to pass it on. Say to him: Lord, I see what is rising in my heart. Those three seconds are three seconds of returning the throne to him. After a while you will find that you still have feelings, but the feelings no longer lead you away. This is the most real form of selflessness: I am still here, but the steering wheel is not in my hands.
@@15806@@
The proud person says, “I am stronger than he is; he deserved it.” The text says to him: you said, “These two nations will be mine, and we will possess it,” whereas Yahweh was there. @@15807@@

@@15808@@
The shame-prone person says, “To think I have this secret gladness in my heart. Am I rotten to the core?” The text does not want you so ashamed that you dare not pray. God wants you to see honestly, and then hand it to him. Shame says, “You do not deserve to come near.” The gospel says, “Come, hand that flicker to me.” The shame-prone person does not need to become good first. Today just say: Lord, I saw it.

The enemy says: “The ancient high places are ours in possession!” They think the mountains have no master, and no one will speak for a mountain.</p><p>But God tells the prophet to speak to the mountains: “Hear Yahweh’s word.” What was taken away is first called by name; what was despised is first spoken to.</p><p>The “hear” of verse 1 has one more layer: mountains have no ears, yet they are told to hear. God’s word leaves a place even for what cannot respond. What is abandoned and left desolate is first treated with him as someone who can be spoken to.</p><p>Hearing what others say about you and hearing what God says about you are two different things. When people have decided “it’s over” for you, do not hurry to argue back; first listen to what he says.</p>

This is not asking people to live in performance, but to know: your failure is not God’s failure, and since he has respect for his own name, he has respect for you.</p>

@@15916@@
<p><strong>“I do this not for your sake, but for my holy name”: this is the most important sentence of grace in the whole book, and also the least pleasing one.</strong></p><p>We all want to be loved because we are lovable. God’s answer is: not so. The root of his action is not whether you deserve it, but who he is.</p><p>Thinking of it this way, you may feel pushed away; but think carefully, and this is the greatest security. If grace depended on my performance, then on the day I made a mistake, it would be taken back. If grace depends on his name, his name does not change, and neither does his grace.</p><p>“Holy name” does not mean he cares about saving face. The name is his character and his faithfulness, the way they are seen by the nations. To “sanctify” (<em>qiddash</em>) is to let people see what kind of God this God is. Chapter 20 also says again and again “for the sake of my name.”</p><p>This also explains why the new heart, the new spirit, and the new land that follow are none of them rewards. A reward depends on performance; a gift depends only on the giver.</p><p>For the undeserving, there is a way back. The glory goes to him, not to us, and this is exactly where people can breathe out: I do not have to deserve it; I only have to come back.</p>

<p><strong>God’s verbs, one after another: take, gather, bring, sprinkle, cleanse.</strong></p><p>Verse 24 first says “bring you into your own land,” and verse 25 then says sprinkle water. <p><strong>God does not repair the old heart; he replaces it.</strong></p><p>A stony heart does not respond, does not yield, does not hurt, and does not change. Some fear becoming too sensitive; the text says a heart that can hurt is a heart that is alive.</p><p>In chapter 18, verse 31, the prophet once commanded people: “Make yourselves a new heart and a new spirit.” That was a command people could not carry out. Here the same thing turns from command into promise: not “you must change,” but “I will give you.”</p><p>And he does not only replace the heart; he also puts “my Spirit” within people so that they can obey. The new birth Jesus spoke of to Nicodemus in John chapter 3 is this promise. When the heart is changed, it is not that one more rule has been added, but that one is moved again.</p><p>Selflessness is not digging out the heart by yourself, but receiving the one he puts in.</p>
