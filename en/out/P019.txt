I. A Branch Cut Off (15:1–3)
@@6533@@

He cut branch by branch, and the cuttings piled up at his feet, crooked and twisted, like a heap of tangled hemp. I asked him: What do you use these for? He glanced at me, smiled, and said: Nothing. I dry them and burn them. I asked again: Can’t you make a fence, or a chair? He shook his head: Grapevine wood is soft and crooked. You can’t make anything from it. It has only one use, and that is to bear fruit. If it can’t bear fruit, it is firewood.
@@6534@@
He said it so plainly, yet I stood there for a good while. Because today’s reading is a chapter just like that. Ezekiel chapter 15 has only eight verses, one of the shortest chapters in the whole book. There are no four living creatures, no wheels of fire, no sound of wings. There is only one question, dropped in front of the prophet like a stone thrown into water.
@@6535@@
Verse 1 says: “Yahweh’s word came to me, saying.” The sentence is short, yet steady. The prophet was in a land of exile, with no temple to go to and no altar to stand at, and still God’s word found him. Then in verse 2, God calls him “son of man” and asks: “What is the vine tree more than any tree, the vine-branch which is among the trees of the forest?” Please notice how the question is put. God did not ask whether the vine bears fruit. He asked about the wood: where is this wood stronger than other wood?
@@6536@@
Verse 3 asks even smaller: “Will wood be taken of it to make anything? Will men take a pin of it to hang any vessel on it?” From beams and pillars down to one wooden peg for hanging things, each step smaller than the last, and every time the answer is: No. The listeners knew the answer at once, because they had all seen vine branches. No one builds a house with them, and no one makes a door frame from them.
@@6537@@
A retired brother told me that in his first month after retirement, he still woke up on time every morning, got dressed, and sat in the living room, not knowing where to go. He said: Pastor, I only found out then that I had always been using “being busy” to be sure of who I am. That day he was very quiet, and I did not hurry to comfort him. Because at that moment he was standing right in front of the question in verse 3: if everything you can do is taken away, what do you have left?
@@6538@@
This is not shaming the vine. The vine never thought of being a beam; it was planted for something else. God asked the question so narrowly in order that the listeners would say the answer themselves: the value of this branch was never in the wood, but in the fruit.
@@6539@@
God asks, “What is the vine tree more than any tree?” Is he saying that people have no worth to begin with?
@@6540@@
No. Please notice two details. First, this question asks only about the wood, not about people. God used vine wood as a comparison to show that a branch that does not bear fruit has no other role that can take its place. Second, this question was put to the inhabitants of Jerusalem (they are named only in verse 6), and it is not a general verdict on everyone. A person’s worth, throughout the Bible, is given by God: he made people in his own image, and he himself planted this vine. So this verse is not saying “you are worthless,” but “your worth is not in your wood.” You can practice it this way: think of one thing you often use to prove that you are useful, and then say to God: Lord, my worth is in your hands, not in this thing.

It is not that these things are unimportant, but that they are only the names of the trunk; what God wants to see is whether there is one fruit slowly growing on the branch.
@@6548@@
If I am suffering, and I read “already cast into the fire” and feel very afraid, how can I read this verse?
@@6549@@
You can read it this way: first put this verse in its own place. It is a warning God spoke to a people who relied on their identity and left him, not a diagnosis for those who suffer. In the Bible there are many people in the fire with God by their side, like the three men in Daniel chapter 3, where there was a fourth in the fire. So if you are in the fire now, do not use this verse to judge yourself, and do not ask “Did I do something wrong?” You can simply stop at one sentence: God has not given up on his vine. If this verse makes you more afraid and more sure that you are useless, please put it down first, find someone you trust to talk with, and if necessary ask a pastor or a professional to walk with you for a while.
@@6550@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Vine, Work, Face (15:2, 4, 7)
@@6551@@
There are three words in this chapter worth a look, because each one can be turned, within thirty seconds, into how a person lives today. The first is “vine,” the Hebrew גֶּפֶן (gefen). Verse 2 says: “What is the vine tree more than any tree?” In the Bible this word often refers to the people God planted, so what God is asking about is not an ordinary tree. Yet this chapter asks only about its wood. Seen this way, the meaning of gefen is clear: a vine that has only wood left is already no longer a vine. Ask yourself today: Do I have only wood left? Is the life that is connected to the vine still in me?
@@6552@@
The second is “work,” the Hebrew מְלָאכָה (melakhah). This word appears several times in this chapter, in verses 3, 4, and 5. It means work, use, something made by hand. Have you noticed that this is exactly the word our age loves to ask: What can you do? What are you good for? God puts this word in front of the vine wood and says: even by your best standard of measure, it cannot be measured as having worth. This is not saying that people need not work, but that a person’s worth is not in melakhah. You can try it today: do not ask “How much did I do today?” but ask “Whom am I connected to today?”
@@6553@@
The third is “face,” the Hebrew פָּנִים (panim). Verse 7 says: “I will set my face against them.” The root meaning of this word is face, and in the Bible the greatest blessing is that God’s face shines on people (Numbers 6:25: “Yahweh make his face to shine on you, and be gracious to you”). But in this verse the same face has turned in another direction. The face has not changed; what has changed is the relationship between people and him. This gives us a very practical reminder: the most frightening thing is not that days are hard, but that God’s face is turned away and I do not notice. So every morning, ask yourself first: Today am I facing him, or turning my back to him?
@@6554@@
Joining the three words together gives the path of this chapter: gefen, a tree that was born for fruit; melakhah, what it is finally asked about, what use the wood is; panim, the God who planted it turns his face to meet it face to face. From the tree, to usefulness, to the face: this chapter asks us to lay down “What am I good for?” and return to “Whose face am I in front of?”
@@6555@@
The original says that גֶּפֶן often refers to the people God planted. What does that mean for me today?
@@6556@@
It reminds us that we did not grow ourselves. A vine is planted: someone prepared the ground, someone gathered out the stones, someone waits. So a person’s life begins in God’s hand, not in their own effort. This has two very practical meanings. First, we do not need to “do enough” to earn qualification, because we were planted from the start. Second, we also cannot use “I was planted” as an excuse for not bearing fruit, because we were planted for the sake of fruit. You can pray like this: Lord, thank you for planting me; I do not have to prove myself. And please let me bear the fruit you want, not the results I want. Put these two sentences together, and that is the proper position of the vine.
@@6557@@
IV. I Will Set My Face Against Them and Make the Land Desolate (15:6–8)
@@6558@@
In verse 6, God makes the picture plain: “Therefore the Lord Yahweh says: ‘As the vine tree among the trees of the forest, which I have given to the fire for fuel, so I will give the inhabitants of Jerusalem.’” In the first five verses there is not one personal name or place name, until here. The picture is stretched out so long because people need time before they are willing to admit that the picture is themselves.
@@6559@@
Please notice two things. First, the subject is “I.” God says: I am the one who gave it to the fire as fuel. He does not pretend to be absent; both judgment and grace are done by his own hand. Second, the scope is “the inhabitants of Jerusalem,” not all of God’s people. The text itself narrows the circle. So do not take this one picture as God’s final verdict on everyone, and do not use it to evaluate any specific person.
@@6560@@
Verse 7 says: “I will set my face against them. They will go out from the fire, but the fire will devour them.” This sentence is not easy to understand, and interpreters hold two views. Some say it refers to those already exiled, who will still face greater judgment. Some say it refers to the survivors, who must not think all is well just because they escaped once. Both views have grounds, and these notes do not make either one a condition of faith. The two views share one point: escaping once does not mean receiving grace. What matters is not escaping, but turning back.
@@6561@@
Have you ever had someone who loves you very much turn their face away from you? When I was little and had done something wrong, my father did not scold me. He only turned, walked into his room, and quietly closed the door. I cried very hard that time. Only later did I understand that this one turning away weighed more than any scolding, because I knew he truly cared. God’s “setting his face against” is not the kind of losing his temper that we imagine; it is a Father who loves very deeply, waiting for the child to turn his head back.
@@6562@@
Next comes the heaviest sentence of the whole chapter: “Then you will know that I am Yahweh, when I set my face against them.” This is the heartbeat of the book of Ezekiel. Everything God does has only one purpose, that people may know him, and even judgment is for this. He turns his face away not because he hates, but because he loves too seriously to pretend he does not see. A God who did not love would not ask this question; he could have said nothing at all and let the tree dry up on its own.
@@6563@@
The last verse, verse 8, is only one sentence: “I will make the land desolate, because they have committed a trespass,” says the Lord Yahweh. This chapter gives no ending, and no “yet.” It is deliberate: it is waiting for the later chapters, one after another, to speak that “yet.” If your life is now walking through a chapter without a conclusion, please do not hurry to write a conclusion for it. There is no need to say “I must be useless,” and no need to say “There is a good purpose in all of this.” You can say only this: Lord, I am still here.
@@6564@@
How should we understand “They will go out from the fire, but the fire will devour them”?
@@6565@@
This verse really is understood in different ways. The first: it refers to those already exiled, who will still face greater judgment. The second: it refers to the survivors, who must not think they have received grace just because they escaped one disaster. Neither gives a date, and neither asks us to work out a timetable. We can take the point they share: thinking “it is over” is not the same as really coming back to God. If you have come through an illness or a disaster, this verse is not telling you “there is more fire.” What it wants to say is: do not treat “escaping” as “being saved,” and treat “turning back” as the way out. You can practice like this: in a quiet moment, let God’s face be toward you, and ask him: Is there something I have said is “already past” that I have not really faced?
@@6566@@
The first deviation is teaching this chapter as “people who suffer are useless firewood.” The fire in verses 4 and 5 is a warning to a people who left God, not said to anyone who is in the fire. We want those who suffer to be accompanied, and not to carry an extra layer of shame, “I am useless.”
@@6567@@
The second deviation is teaching “What is the vine tree more than any tree?” as saying that people’s worth is very low. God asks this question to bring people back to the proper place of bearing fruit, not to belittle them. We want the congregation to be more sure that they have worth in him, and not more convinced that they are worthless.
@@6568@@
The third deviation is using “I will set my face against them” to frighten the congregation. This is a heavy word of judgment, but the purpose of the whole book is that people may know Yahweh. The result we want is patience and a deeper love for the Lord, not panic, and certainly not people being afraid every day of being thrown away.
@@6569@@
The fourth deviation is matching “the fire” to any people, events, or groups of today, or using it to set a timetable. The text is judgment on the inhabitants of Jerusalem at that time. We do not name names, we do not put labels on people, and we do not count days.
@@6570@@
The fifth deviation is simply equating Ezekiel 15 with John 15. Both passages use the vine, but the emphasis is different: John is an invitation to “remain,” while Ezekiel is a warning to the fruitless. The two can complement each other, but they are not the same word; also, “bearing fruit” is not ministry numbers, but the quality of a life that the Spirit bears: love, joy, peace, patience, and the like.
