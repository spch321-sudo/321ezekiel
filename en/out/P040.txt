@@13397@@
Verse 1 begins very simply: “Yahweh’s word came again to me.” That word “again” carries weight. God’s word does not come only once. It comes again and again, patiently. Then comes a command: “Son of man, prophesy, and say, ‘The Lord Yahweh says: “Wail, ‘Alas for the day!’” You should wail.” What the prophet is sent with is not a long doctrine, but one cry: “Alas.” Then a reason is given: “For the day is near, even Yahweh’s day is near. It will be a day of clouds, a time of the nations.”
@@13398@@
“The day of Yahweh” is used by several prophets in the Old Testament. It is not a square on the calendar, but the moment when God himself acts, when he is silent no longer. Some people long for this day and think that on that day God will stand up for us. But the prophet Amos said long ago, “Woe to you who desire the day of Yahweh! Why do you long for the day of Yahweh? It is darkness, and not light.” Those who long for that day should be careful, because on that day God does not come to prop up our pride. He comes to deal with all pride, including our own. So this phrase is not meant for frightening others. It is meant for examining ourselves. In this chapter, the day comes on the nations, not on one people only.
@@13399@@
Verses 4 and 5 read like a list of names: “A sword will come on Egypt… Ethiopia, Put, Lud, all the mixed people, Cub, and the children of the land that is allied with them, will fall with them by the sword.” These were Egypt’s allies, the helpers Egypt had gathered to give itself courage. Verse 6 puts it plainly: “They also who uphold Egypt will fall.” In the last chapter, Egypt was a reed staff. In this chapter, it is a whole row of them. A person tries to lift himself up on other people’s shoulders, and the shoulders all fall together. We do not need to make those scenes bigger than they are. We only need to notice one thing: the people you gathered to give you courage may not be able to stand either.
@@13400@@
Verse 9 pulls our eyes farther south: “In that day messengers will go out from before me in ships to make the careless Ethiopians afraid.” The phrase “careless” is interesting. When people feel that they are far away and that the matter has nothing to do with them, they feel at ease. But when God’s messengers go out in ships, no place is too far. This reminds us that nobody is too far from God’s day, and nobody can say, “All this has nothing to do with me.”
@@13401@@
This chapter is heavy, and I say so honestly. But please notice that it does not have a single word telling us to make a timetable, a single word telling us to match events to it, and a single word telling us to point at someone and say, “You are finished.” It wants us to hear two things: God is Lord of the nations, and he speaks so that people will know him. Verse 8 says, “They will know that I am Yahweh.” The day of Yahweh is not the goal. Knowing that I am Yahweh is the goal. When someone sees the sky growing dark, there are two reactions. One is panic, running in every direction. The other is to look up and say, “Lord, you are here.” This chapter is meant to lead us to the second.
@@13402@@
“The day of Yahweh” is so serious. I feel afraid when I read it. Is that normal?
@@13403@@
It is normal, and there is no need to rush to suppress it. The text itself uses words like “alas” and “wail,” and it does not ask anyone to pretend to be at ease. But please tell two kinds of fear apart. One kind pushes a person into panic, hunting for an escape, for answers, for dates. The other kind brings a person to God, so that he says, “Lord, I need you.” The Bible wants us to walk in the second kind. Ezekiel 30 does not have one word asking us to draw up a timetable, and it does not ask us to point at others and say that they are being judged. You can practice it like this: when you are afraid, you do not have to understand every detail first. Just say one sentence to God: “Lord, you are the Lord of the nations and also my Lord. I give this unease to you.” If this fear keeps you from sleeping or affects your daily life, please tell your pastor or a trusted companion, and seek help together.
@@13404@@
II. The Rivers Dry Up, City by City Named (30:10–19)
@@13405@@
Verse 10 says, “The Lord Yahweh says: ‘I will also make the multitude of Egypt to cease, by the hand of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon.’” This king of Babylon did not know God, yet the text says that he was in God’s hand too. Verse 11 says that he and his people with him were “the terrible of the nations.” Please notice that this is not praising his violence, and it is not saying that war is good. There is an important distinction here: God’s ruling over history does not mean that God approves every method. He can use people, and he can also judge them. Later Babylon itself came under his judgment.
@@13406@@
Do you remember who the first sentence of verse 12 was spoken to? “I will make the rivers dry.” In the last chapter, that Pharaoh said, “The river is mine.” In this chapter, God says, “I will make the rivers dry.” The river was his pride, his food, and his name. But God made the river, and he can give it and take it back. It is a very quiet declaration, with no shouting, just one sentence: “I will.”
@@13407@@
Then the text reads out a string of city names: Memphis, Pathros, Zoan, No, Sin, Aven, Pibeseth, Tehaphnehes. These are Egypt’s major cities. We do not need to look up each one on a map. We only need to see one thing: God names them one by one. He does not say vaguely, “Egypt will be punished.” He speaks city by city. This means that he sees clearly, and he knows every place. He is not losing his temper from far away. He sees up close.
@@13408@@
Have you ever been truly seen by someone? Not seen for your achievements, but seen for the lane where you live, your name, and how you pass your day. Being seen like this is sometimes a comfort and sometimes a reason for reverence. For a proud city, it is a matter of reverence: he sees even the pride you hide behind your walls. For the humble, it is a comfort: he sees you in the same way, and has never left you out.
@@13409@@
Verse 13 says, “I will also destroy the idols, and I will cause the images to cease from Memphis. There will be no more a prince from the land of Egypt.” Idols and kings are the two things a nation values most: one is what it worships, the other is what it relies on. God takes away both. This is not mocking another people’s culture. It is saying that if a person gives the place of “what holds me up” to a created thing, that thing will in the end fail to hold. Verse 18 is striking: “At Tehaphnehes also the day will withdraw itself, when I break the yokes of Egypt, there. The pride of her power will cease in her. As for her, a cloud will cover her.” A cloud: the very word at the beginning of this chapter comes back.
@@13410@@
Finally, verse 19 is like a full stop: “Thus I will execute judgments on Egypt. Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” You see, from verse 1 to verse 19, everything circles around the same sentence. Judgment is the means. Knowing him is the goal. We read this passage not to see who will fall, but to ask ourselves: do I have an idol in my hand that “holds me up”? Have I also made some city, some power, my guarantee?
@@13411@@
The text says that God “names” every city. Does this mean that God is terrifying, always watching people for their mistakes?
@@13412@@
No. In the Bible, naming is often the language of care. A good shepherd also names his sheep one by one, and Jesus said that his sheep hear his voice and that he calls them by name. In chapter 30, the ones named are proud cities, so that they would know: he has not left anyone out, and he has not misunderstood. This has two meanings for you. First, if there are things you have been trying to cover up, you do not need to keep holding them up by yourself. He saw them long ago, and you can safely speak them out. Second, if you feel very small and unseen, he knows your name just the same. You can try this today: in prayer, say your own name to God and say, “Lord, you see me, and I want to see you too.”
@@13413@@
III. Light from the Original: Cloud, Uphold, Bind Up (30:3, 6, 21)
@@13414@@
This chapter has three original words worth looking at closely. The first is “cloud” in verse 3, in Hebrew עָנָן (anan). Elsewhere in the Bible, this is the cloud of God’s presence: when Israel came out of Egypt, the pillar of cloud led them by day. The same word here becomes the cloud of judgment. This reminds us that God’s nearness is a different sky for different people. For those who rely on him, the cloud is guidance. For those who hold on to themselves, the cloud is a shadow pressing down. The cloud has not changed. What has changed is where we stand.
@@13415@@
The second is “uphold” in verse 6, in Hebrew סֹמְכֵי (somkhe), from the root סָמַךְ (samakh), meaning to lay a hand on, to lean on. When people made a sacrifice, they laid a hand on the head of the animal, and the word used is this one. So “uphold” is not a light kind of help. It means pressing down with your whole weight. The sentence means that those who uphold Egypt are the ones who put their weight on Egypt. The fault is not in upholding. The fault is in the weight that is pressed down, and in what it is pressed on. Where is your hand resting?
@@13416@@
The third is “bind up” in verse 21, in Hebrew חָבַשׁ (khavash). The text says, “It has not been bound up, to apply medicines, to put a bandage to bind it.” This is the doctor’s word for bandaging a wound. Psalm 147:3 says that God “heals the broken in heart, and binds up their wounds,” using the same root. It is a very gentle word. It says that there is a pair of hands whose very work is to bandage what is broken. But no one binds Pharaoh’s arm. This is the result of pride: no one dares to come and bandage him, because he never admits that he is wounded.
@@13417@@
Put the three words together and you have the journey of this chapter: the cloud (anan) presses down, which means God has come near. The thing on which a person has laid his weight (samakh) cannot hold. And the one who can truly bind up what is broken (khavash) is that God. Cloud, uphold, bind up: these are three movements. See that he is coming, see where your hand is resting, and let him bandage the wound.
@@13418@@
“Uphold” and “lay a hand on” come from the same root in the original. What does this mean for my life?
@@13419@@
It means that every day we are “laying a hand on” something. The only question is where. You can try a very simple exercise: at night before sleep, stretch out both hands and count in your heart: today, on whom did I put my weight? It may be a boss, a spouse, a sum of money, a plan. After counting, there is no need to blame yourself, and no need to let go of everyone at once, because God also uses people to hold us up. You only need to finish with one sentence: “Lord, today I laid my hand on these people, and I also lay my whole self in your hand.” This sentence separates the “whole” from the “part.” After some time, you will find that when you lose something, your heart does not collapse along with it.
@@13420@@
IV. The Broken Arm and the Supporting Hand (30:20–26)
@@13421@@
Verse 20 changes the date: “In the eleventh year, in the first month, in the seventh day of the month, Yahweh’s word came to me.” Verse 21 is very direct: “Son of man, I have broken the arm of Pharaoh king of Egypt. Behold, it has not been bound up, to apply medicines, to put a bandage to bind it, that it become strong to hold the sword.” The arm is the symbol of strength, the hand that holds the sword. God says, “I have broken it.” It is the tone of something finished, not something that will be done in the future. And no one has bandaged it.
@@13422@@
Please notice this picture: a broken arm, with no medicine applied and no bandage. This is not only about judgment. It is also a very deep loneliness. When a proud person is wounded, no one dares to come near, and no one can come near, because he keeps saying, “I’m fine.” We all know people like this, and perhaps it is ourselves: I can hold on, I’m fine, I don’t need anyone.
@@13423@@
But we must be careful not to read this verse as something else. A broken arm does not mean that everyone who is disabled, hurt, or in a broken relationship has been broken by God. This chapter is speaking about the power on which a proud person relies, not about the situation of anyone who is suffering. If your arm really hurts right now, please do not hear in this verse, “It is my fault.” God says that your wound is to be bound up.
@@13424@@
Verse 22 says, “Behold, I am against Pharaoh king of Egypt, and will break his arms, the strong arm, and that which was broken. I will cause the sword to fall out of his hand.” Verses 23 and 26 repeat one sentence: “I will scatter the Egyptians among the nations, and will disperse them through the countries.” Repetition is how the text stresses something. Verses 24 and 25 say, “I will strengthen the arms of the king of Babylon, and put my sword in his hand; but I will break the arms of Pharaoh.” You see, two arms: one is held up, one is broken, and the subject of both is “I.” Even the strength of an arm does not belong to anyone himself.
@@13425@@
The theme of this passage is actually quite simple: who holds up and who breaks are both in his hand. But please do not read it as God enjoying the breaking. The text puts a fact before us: the strength people rely on, whether one person’s arm, a nation’s army, or your and my connections and qualifications, can all break. The arm that truly holds people up comes from God. Isaiah says that his arm brings salvation for us.
@@13426@@
So the end of this chapter echoes the last one: Egypt will become a place that is “no longer relied on by the nations,” and Israel must learn to reach out its hand to another hand. Every time the text says, “They will know that I am Yahweh,” it is not a threat but an invitation: to know him is to find the arm that does not break.
@@13427@@
God says that he is “against Pharaoh king of Egypt.” Does this mean that he does not love the Egyptians?
@@13428@@
No. The last chapter already said that the Egyptians will be gathered, and that God has not thrown them away forever. This chapter keeps saying, “They will know that I am Yahweh,” which shows that the purpose of judgment is for people to know him. God is against Pharaoh in the sense of being against that pride, not against the people. You can look at yourself with the same eyes: God does not like the pride in me, my idols, the reeds I rely on, but he loves me. Both things are true at the same time. If you feel that “God is against me,” you can say to him, “Lord, if there is some place in me that is pride, please help me see it, and please hold me up.” His purpose has never been to destroy, but to heal.
@@13429@@
The first deviation is to treat “the day of Yahweh” as a tool of intimidation, creating panic and pushing people to believe quickly or else they are finished. What Ezekiel 30 wants us to hear is: “They will know that I am Yahweh.” We should lead the congregation to know God more and love the Lord more, not to be more panicked.
@@13430@@
The second deviation is to use this chapter to draw up an end-times timetable, to calculate dates, or to say of some modern event, “This is the day of Yahweh.” The text does not tell us to calculate days, and it does not name any modern country. We should help the congregation learn patience and watchfulness, not keep staring at the news and matching events.
@@13431@@
The third deviation is to point at a particular country, people, or group and say that they are being judged by God, and to tell those who have suffered a disaster that it is retribution. When suffering people finish listening, they should be comforted, not accused. We should help people look at themselves more humbly, not hurry to judge others.
@@13432@@
The fourth deviation is to use “a day of clouds,” “the sword,” and “captivity” to explain the situation of people who have been through war, bereavement, or a great loss, or to preach “the broken arm” as if everyone who is hurt has been broken by God. This chapter speaks of the power on which the proud rely, not the situation of the suffering. We should first accompany, without questioning, without telling them to “be strong,” and without having them use this chapter to blame themselves.
@@13433@@
The fifth deviation is to preach “by the hand of the king of Babylon” in 30:10 as if God approves of violence and aggression, or to preach the judgment on idols as mocking other people’s faith and culture. The text speaks of God’s rule over history. It does not bless killing, and it does not tell us to look down on anyone. We should help the congregation revere God’s sovereignty more, and also respect every person more.
@@13434@@
I am very afraid of the wars and disasters in the world. Does this mean that “the day of Yahweh” is near?
@@13435@@
These notes do not draw up a timetable, and they do not say of any modern event, “This is the day of Yahweh.” Elsewhere the Bible also reminds us that we do not need to calculate the time of that day. Your fear is real, and you do not need to pretend it is not there. What this chapter can give you is not a timetable, but three things. First, God is Lord of the nations, and nothing has spun out of control. Second, he sees every place up close, including those who suffer. Third, he speaks so that people will know him, not so that they will panic. If this worry is already affecting your sleep, appetite, or work, please talk with a pastor or a professional. This is not weakness. It is wisdom.
@@13436@@
The first question: we are very used to “alliances.” Chinese society values relationships. For everything we look for someone: business has a backer, children have connections, and when trouble comes someone helps. This is our wisdom, and also our safety net. All of this is useful in life, and God often uses people to take care of us. But Ezekiel 30:5–6 reminds us: when a nation’s sense of security rests entirely on alliances, when the allies fall together, everything falls together. You can examine yourself like this: if the three people I rely on most were all gone next month, what would I have left? If the answer is a blank, you can start today, and each day leave one more place for God.
@@13437@@
The second question: we are very used to “not letting people see my wounds.” Chinese people care about face, so even when hurt we endure it, when wronged we say it’s nothing, and even when broken we say, “I’m fine.” But the arm in verse 21 that no one bound up is exactly like this: it is not that no one was willing to bandage it, but that he did not let anyone come near. You can begin like this: find someone you trust and say just one sentence: “Lately there is one place in me that is hurt, and no one has bandaged it yet.” No need for details, no need for reasons. You will find that admitting a wound is not shameful, but lets the bandaging hand come in.
@@13438@@
I am not used to letting people see my wounds, but I also very much want someone to be with me. What should I do?
@@13439@@
This is a very common feeling, and there is no need to blame yourself. You can start from the safest place: first tell God, because he has seen it all along. Then choose someone who is not in a hurry to give advice and who will not repeat what you say, and say only the smallest sentence, for example: “I’ve been a bit tired lately.” Then see how he responds. If he is willing to listen quietly, you can say a little more. If he hurries to persuade you, you can choose another person. It is not that you were wrong; this person is just not yet the right one to bandage you. Bandaging, like healing, comes step by step. If your wound has to do with bereavement, being hurt by someone, or depression, please also consider seeking professional help in addition to a trusted person. This is another pair of hands God has given.
@@13440@@
VII. Living Out 321: Neither Panicking Nor Boasting
@@13441@@
Jesus is my Role Model. In the storm, he could still sleep at the stern of the boat. When he woke, he only said that the wind and waves should be still. At the darkest hour of the sky, he did not panic, and he did not boast, because he knew that the sky and the clouds were in the Father’s hand. We who follow him learn this pattern: when the sky is dark, we do not run in all directions, we only look up.
@@13442@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read Ezekiel 30 not to find news to match, and not to frighten people, but to let God’s word ask us: who is my arm? Who are my allies? What is my idol? Behind every word of judgment there is a sentence, “They will know that I am Yahweh.” That is God’s invitation.
@@13443@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who keeps us from being gripped by fear when the sky grows dark, and who gently reminds us when our hand rests in the wrong place. Take a quiet moment each day and ask one question: “Lord, today where have I laid my hand?”
@@13444@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is letting go of the assurance that says, “I have people, I have power, I can hold on.” It is not throwing away relationships and resources, but giving the place of King back to him. My strength, my reputation, and my backing can all be used, but they are not my king.
@@13445@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we are held up and bound up, let us not say, “I am finally better.” Everything is the grace of the Lord who has the arm, in Jesus. If we stand, the glory goes to him.
@@13446@@
To build God’s system. A group that belongs to God does not treat any nation or any person as a reed staff, and does not let anyone carry a broken arm alone. Every place and every brother and sister is in his care. Only after we are held up by him do we have strength to hold others up. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: when our life has first been held up by the Lord, we can become the hands that bind up one another’s wounds.
@@13447@@
Believers relying on one another and holding one another up: does this not contradict “do not rely on reeds”?
@@13448@@
It does not contradict. The Bible clearly says that we should care for one another and bear one another’s burdens. What Ezekiel 30 rebukes is the proud putting all their weight on power and allies and removing God’s place. When believers support one another, we are admitting: I am also being supplied, and I will become part of someone else’s arm. You can tell the difference like this. One: when I rely on him, is there thanks to God in my heart? Two: when he falls, will my whole self collapse, or will I grieve but still stand? Three: when someone relies on me, do I admit that I too am a reed and need the Lord who can hold? When you ask these three questions clearly, relying on one another becomes a blessing, not a new idol.
@@13449@@
The proud type says: I have people, I have power, I have money, I can hold on. The text says to him: “They also who uphold Egypt will fall.” Pride leaves the human position and seizes God’s authority, wanting to become its own guarantee. Returning to our position means admitting: I also have an arm that can break, and I need the hand that does not break.
@@13450@@
The fearful type says: The sky is so dark, I’m afraid I won’t get through. God is the Lord of the nations, and he sees every place up close, even the lane where you live. The fearful type does not need to hurry to understand every detail, but can first just look up and say one sentence: “Lord, you are here.”
@@13451@@
The ashamed type says: My arm is also broken, and I dare not let anyone see. The text does not rebuke every wounded arm. What verse 21 speaks of is the proud one who would not let anyone bind it. Shame says, “Don’t let anyone see.” The gospel says, “There is a hand whose very work is to bandage.” The ashamed type does not have to get better first. Letting just one person see is the beginning of being bound up.
@@13452@@
The hurt type says: The people I relied on let me fall hard, and now I don’t want to rely on anyone. This wound is real, and God has not asked you to pretend that nothing happened. To those who accompany: please do not say, “You should forgive,” and do not say, “You relied on the wrong person.” Only say, “I’m here.” To those who are hurt: you can slowly find someone who will not leave, and tell your story.
@@13453@@
First, for three minutes each night before sleep, stretch out both hands and say to the Lord: “Today I laid my hand on these people, and I also lay my whole self in your hand.” And write down the date.
@@13454@@
Second, write down your three “allies” (people, money, position), and next to each write one sentence: “This is something he gave me to use, not my guarantee.” Say who you are willing to let remind you.
@@13455@@
Third, find someone you trust and say one sentence: “Lately there is one place in me that is hurt, and no one has bandaged it yet.” Ask him to pray for you, and write down the name and the time.
@@13456@@
Fourth, when the sky is dark, practice not panicking first but looking up first, and say to God: “Lord, you are here.” Write down which day it was and what you were facing.
@@13457@@
Fifth, greet someone who has recently been hurt but never lets anyone see. Say one sentence: “I’m here.” Write down the name and the date. Give no advice, and do not say “be strong.”
@@13458@@
I declare: Yahweh is Lord of the nations; he sees every place up close, and he sees me too.
@@13459@@
I declare: I will not put all my weight on people, power, or idols; I place my whole self in your hand.
@@13460@@
I declare: I will not panic, I will look up; you speak so that I may know you.
@@13461@@
I declare: You will bind up the broken and hold up the weak; I am willing to let you bandage me, and I am willing to become hands for others.
@@13462@@
The simplest thing is tonight, before sleep, to stretch out your hands and say to God: “Today, on whom did I lay my hand?” Then take a piece of paper and write three names, without giving reasons. Then choose one small action you can do today: send a message to one of them to say thank you, or say to someone you trust, “Lately there is one place in me that is hurt.” This action is Ezekiel 30’s answer: look up, admit, and let the hand rest in the right place. At the next small group meeting, just say, “This week I did one thing,” without needing to say what it was. You do not need to let go of all your staffs at once, and you do not need to tell all your wounds at once. Just today, lay your hand in the right place once.
@@13463@@
Ezekiel 30 begins with one cry, “Alas,” and ends with one broken arm. The beginning is God knocking: Yahweh’s day is near. The middle is the rivers drying up and cities named one by one. The end is an arm no one has bound up. From the cloud to the arm, from the list of names to the wound, this is a journey of a God who sees up close and is also willing to bandage.
@@13464@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is Lord of the nations. God wants us to look up, not panic, and not boast. And God also sees every broken arm. That God came among us in Jesus. He did not come to frighten people. He is the Lord who said in the storm, “Be still,” and also the Lord whose very work is to wrap wounds.
@@13465@@
So please remember the three key points of this lesson: day, break, arm. The focus of this lesson is not how near the day is, not who will fall, and not how afraid you are, but Jesus, who holds the cloud, holds the nations, and holds us in his hand.
@@13466@@
Lord, thank you. You are Lord of the nations, and our Lord too. Please forgive us for laying our hands where we should not, and for refusing to let others bind up our wounds. Make us look up, neither panicking nor boasting, and heal everyone who is in war, loss, and loneliness. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ. Amen.

<p><strong>Even the great waters stopped for it.</strong></p><p>This is the quietest passage of the whole chapter. On the day the tree went down to Sheol, God said, “I caused a mourning.” God covered the deep for it and restrained the rivers. It is a very solemn funeral, and even Lebanon mourned for it.</p><p>We easily imagine judgment as a cold verdict, but the text says that God himself arranges the mourning. The other side of judgment is not delight, but solemnity.</p><p>Verse 16 says that the other trees “were comforted.” This sentence is not easy to explain, so we let it stay open. What matters is that we do not need to feel pleased for any tree that has fallen.</p><p>There is one more detail: those who had been its arm went down with it. A person’s pride often takes others down with him.</p><p>Think about that detail: God “covered the deep and restrained its rivers.” The water that made it grow is now stopped too. The one who gave is also the one who takes back. This is not cruelty, but order.</p><p><strong>Landing:</strong> think about whom your pride would drag down. Those around you who “were your arm”: can they hear a word of thanks and an apology from you today?</p>

<p><strong>① What does “lie in the middle of the uncircumcised” mean?</strong></p><ul><li>It refers to the destination of those regarded as foreigners and unclean, an irony aimed at Pharaoh</li><li>It refers to the universality of death: whatever one’s status, all are in the same place</li></ul><p>In the Egyptian view, honor after death was extremely important, and here he is deliberately placed in the least honorable place.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: this is a reversal of honor, used to tear down Pharaoh’s height.</strong> It is not to be taken as a verdict on any other person.</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>The question of the whole chapter has its answer.</strong></p><p>Verse 2 asks, “Whom are you like?” Verse 18 turns back and asks once more: “To whom are you thus like among the trees of Eden?” The answer is: you will go down together with them. The end of comparing is the same place.</p><p>“Lie” is a very quiet word. All heights in the end become level. The tallest tree and the smallest tree lie on the same ground.</p><p>This chapter is not a curse. It is a very gentle call: while you are still standing, be a little lower. Before God, no tree needs to be higher than another.</p><p>Jesus said, “Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.” He is the one who first came down.</p><p>From beginning to end, this chapter has only two questions and one answer. Between the question and the answer is the life of one tree. After reading, we should all ask: am I also on the same road?</p><p><strong>Landing:</strong> today try to do one “low” thing: let someone speak first, give up a place to someone else, admit one mistake. Let man be man, and let God be God.</p>
