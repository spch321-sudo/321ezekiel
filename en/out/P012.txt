@@4117@@
Let me first connect the context. In chapter 8, the prophet was carried by the Spirit of God to the temple in Jerusalem and saw four scenes: the idol of jealousy at the north gate, the carvings on the walls of the dark rooms, the women weeping for Tammuz, and the men with their backs to the temple, bowing down to the sun. Again and again God asked him the same question: Have you seen this? When he had seen it all, the chapter simply stopped there. Chapter 9 has no cushion. It begins with a shout: “Cause those who are in charge of the city to draw near, each man with his destroying weapon in his hand.”
@@4118@@
Please notice that it is God who is shouting. It is not the prophet speaking. God cries out with a loud voice, straight into the prophet’s ears. In the previous chapter God asked; in this chapter God shouts. When people keep ignoring a low voice, what follows is a loud call. This is not a temper. It is a father standing at the foot of the stairs who has called three times and no one has come down, and who finally raises his voice.
@@4119@@
Then suddenly six men come in from the way of the upper gate, which lies toward the north, each with a weapon in his hand. They did not run in. They “went in, and stood beside the bronze altar.” The bronze altar is where the burnt offering is made, the place where people meet God. Judgment did not come from the wilderness. It rose up from beside the altar. A person can walk into the temple every day, stand beside the altar every day, and yet his heart has long since left that place. So those who stand beside the altar must be all the more watchful.
@@4120@@
But among the six, there is one more man. The text says: “One man in the middle of them was clothed in linen, with a writer’s inkhorn by his side.” Linen is the cloth priests wear, and the inkhorn is a scribe’s tool. This man has no weapon in his hand. The text does not tell us his name, and does not say who he is. Some say he is an angel, some say he is a type of Christ, and some say the text does not tell us, so we should not fill it in for the text. We only see his clothing, his tool, and the task that comes next.
@@4121@@
Then comes verse 3: “The glory of the God of Israel went up from the cherub, whereupon it was, to the threshold of the house.” The glory did not leave all at once. It first rose up and stopped at the threshold. Have you ever watched an elderly person at home getting ready to go on a long journey? He does not just leave. He first stands up and slowly walks to the entrance, one hand on the shoe cabinet, and stands there. He does not put on his shoes, and he does not open the door. He has not gone. He is waiting, waiting for someone to call out, “Dad, don’t go.”
@@4122@@
Then, what is the first thing God does? It is not to tell the men with weapons to begin. It is to call “the man clothed in linen, who had the writer’s inkhorn by his side.” Look carefully at the order: the first one God calls is the one who will record the names. Before the judgment has begun, the one who keeps the record is already standing before him.
@@4123@@
This chapter has so many words about killing, and I find it very uncomfortable to read. May I skip it?
@@4124@@
You may stop. You do not have to force yourself to read it all in one sitting. Especially if you have experienced violence, lost a child, or lost a loved one, the pictures in this chapter can hurt deeply. Please take care of yourself, and stop at any time. But I also want to invite you to read slowly and not skip it, because although this chapter has swords in it, its theme is “recorded first.” As you read, you can put your attention on three places. First, God shouts loudly because he asked many times and no one turned back. Second, the man in linen has no weapon in his hand, only an inkhorn. Third, the glory is still standing at the threshold. It has not gone. If you feel heavy in your heart after reading, you can pray: Lord, I cannot go on reading; please read with me. You can also find someone you trust and read a passage together.
@@4125@@
II. Light from the Original Language: Mark, Sigh, Ah (9:4, 8)
@@4126@@
Verse 4 is the heart of this chapter. Yahweh says to the man: “Go through the middle of the city, through the middle of Jerusalem, and set a mark on the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry over all the abominations that are done within it.” There are three words in this verse worth looking at slowly. The first is “mark,” the Hebrew תָּו (tav). This word is itself the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and in ancient times it was a cross-shaped sign, meaning a “mark.” In Job 31:35 it is also used for a person’s “signature,” the sign he puts at the end. Some people see this crossed shape and think of the cross. We appreciate that thought, but we do not build an argument on the shape of a letter. We only need to hold on to its meaning: this is a signature. Before the judgment, on a person’s forehead, God signs a name and says: This one is mine.
@@4127@@
The second word is “sigh,” the Hebrew נֶאֱנָחִים (ne’enachim), and after it comes “cry,” נֶאֱנָקִים. The two words together make a vivid picture. A sigh is that long breath let out from the chest, with no sentence, no reasoning, and no conclusion. Exodus 2:23 says that when the children of Israel were in bitter labor in Egypt, they also sighed, and God heard. A sigh is a kind of prayer when you do not know what to say. You sit in a hospital corridor, in someone’s living room, or in a corner of the company meeting room, your heart is heavy, and you cannot say anything. God understands that one breath.
@@4128@@
Please notice that what God records is not “the one who speaks the loudest,” nor “the one who does the most,” but “the one who sighs over these things.” The text does not say what they could stop, and does not say what they did. They could change nothing. They were only heartbroken. But being heartbroken is evidence of being of one heart with God, not a condition for being saved. God records first, and only then does that breath carry weight.
@@4129@@
The third word is in verse 8, the first word the prophet speaks: אֲהָהּ (ahah), which the CUV renders as an exclamation, “Ah.” “Ah Lord Yahweh!” It is a sound that makes no sense, a sound that falls out of the throat. The prophet is lying on the ground. He does not start with theology and does not start with analysis. He first lets out a sound. Many times we think we must say our words in a complete way before we can pray, but we do not have to. Before God, there is no need for complete sentences. One “Ah, Lord” is a prayer.
@@4130@@
Put the three words together: God writes the תָּו on the forehead because he has heard the נֶאֱנָחִים, and in the midst of the sound of killing, the prophet also let out his own אֲהָהּ. The mark is his hand, the sigh is our breath, and the “ah” is our voice. Today you can do just one thing: over the thing that breaks your heart, say to God, “Ah, Lord.”
@@4131@@
I feel numb, and I do not even have the strength to sigh. Does that mean I am not among those he has marked?
@@4132@@
No. What this chapter says is: God sees. He records those who sigh, and he also sees those who do not even have the strength to sigh. Numbness is often a door that a person closes from the inside after too much pain and too long a weariness. It is not your sin, and it does not mean you were left out. The ones who are the real problem are those in verse 9 who say, “Yahweh has forsaken the land, and Yahweh doesn’t see.” They were not without strength. They did not want God to see. If you are tired, you can say to God: Lord, I cannot sigh right now; please keep a record for me. You can also find a companion to sit quietly with you for a while, without talking. God’s mark is not held up by your strength. It was drawn by his own hand. If your numbness has lasted a long time and affects your sleep, your eating, and your daily life, please let a pastor or a medical professional walk with you.
@@4133@@
III. “Don’t Let Your Eye Spare” and “Begin at My Sanctuary” (9:5–7)
@@4134@@
Verses 5 and 6 are the hardest part of this chapter to read. God says to the others: “Go through the city after him, and strike. Don’t let your eye spare, neither have pity.” Then the text says to begin at the sanctuary and kill utterly, but do not come near any man on whom is the mark. We must be honest: this passage is heavy. We will not go around it, and we will not make it bigger than it is.
@@4135@@
The setting is the previous chapter. In 8:18 God has already said: “My eye won’t spare, neither will I have pity. Though they cry in my ears with a loud voice, yet I will not hear them.” Four scenes, step by step, turned the temple into a temple of idols. Then 9:9 says again that the land is full of blood and the city full of perversion. This is a city that refused to repent again and again and let blood be shed. The judgment is not without cause. It is the result of long patience.
@@4136@@
But we cannot read only this passage and say “God is cruel,” and we also cannot cover it over with the single sentence “God is righteous.” This chapter has to be read together with other places. In Ezekiel 18:23 God says: “Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked? ... and not rather that he should return from his way, and live?” And Ezekiel 33:11 says again: “I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live.” The same God has said both kinds of words. We cannot keep one side and throw away the other.
@@4137@@
Someone asks: why begin at the sanctuary, why start with God’s house? There are three ways of putting it: the more a person has received grace, the more is required of him; the temple is where God dwells, so it must be cleansed first; and in the New Testament, 1 Peter 4:17 also says: “the time has come for judgment to begin with the household of God.” The view of these notes is this: a position is an entrustment, not an amulet. The nearer a person is to God, the sooner he is asked: Have you been sighing over these things? This is not to make those who serve blame themselves, but to invite us to step into the light first.
@@4138@@
Since “God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked,” why does this chapter still kill infants and women? How can these two statements be put together?
@@4139@@
Honestly, we cannot untie this question completely, and we do not need to pretend that we have. The text has two voices: on one side, “Don’t let your eye spare,” and on the other, “I have no pleasure in death.” A healthier way to read it is to keep both sides. First, the setting of chapter 9 is a city that refused to repent again and again, and the land was full of blood. Second, God’s heart is stated very clearly in chapters 18 and 33: he delights in people turning back and living. This chapter is a picture of judgment, not the whole of God’s heart. Third, if your heart aches because of this passage, that is very natural. Even the prophet himself fell on his face and cried out, “Ah.” You can ask God honestly, and you can also stop. If this passage touches an old wound, please do not try to explain the whole matter by yourself. Find a pastor you trust to read with you.
@@4140@@
IV. The Prophet on His Face and the Man Who Reported Back (9:8–11)
@@4141@@
The sound of killing begins, but the prophet says in the text: “While they were killing, and I was left, I fell on my face.” I read this sentence over and over many times. He is not someone who escaped. He is someone who was left. Others fell in the city, and he was in the temple.
@@4142@@
What does he cry out? “Ah Lord Yahweh! Will you destroy all the residue of Israel in your pouring out of your wrath on Jerusalem?” He asks: Will you destroy all of them? This is a very big question, a question full of tears.
@@4143@@
How does God answer? Some say the prophet was left in order to be a witness. Some say it was in order to intercede. Others say it was both. The view of these notes is that it is both. He was left to see, and also to cry out. And there is one more thing worth noticing: God did not rebuke his plea. He did not say, “How dare you ask?” He heard the question to the end, and then he answered him.
@@4144@@
God’s answer is not light: “The iniquity of the house of Israel and Judah is exceedingly great, and the land is full of blood, and the city full of perversion; for they say, ‘Yahweh has forsaken the land, and Yahweh doesn’t see.’” Do you see it? The deepest sickness of these people is this one sentence: he doesn’t see us. They built what they did on the assumption that “God is not looking.” But in this chapter, he sees very clearly. He sees the blood in the city, he sees the wrong, and he also sees every person sighing in the dark, one by one, recorded on the forehead.
@@4145@@
Finally, there is verse 11: the man clothed in linen, with the inkhorn by his side, comes back and reports: “I have done as you have commanded me.” Did you notice? This man added nothing and took nothing away. He did not show off and did not defend himself. He simply did as he was told, one thing after another, and then came back to report. This sentence is one of the few quiet sentences in the whole book of Ezekiel.
@@4146@@
This sentence reminds us of another. In John 17:4, Jesus says to the Father: “I glorified you on the earth. I have accomplished the work which you have given me to do.” On the cross he said: It is finished. This one who walked through all the sighing did one thing after another as the Father commanded. We do not say that the man in linen is Christ, because the text does not say so. But this man’s quietness and faithfulness are like a mirror, letting us see the true Lord who obeyed completely.
@@4147@@
Why did God not rebuke the prophet’s plea, but hear the question to the end before answering?
@@4148@@
Because what God wants is not a person who dares not ask, but a person who is willing to tell the truth before him. The prophet spoke real pain: Will you destroy all the residue of Israel? God did not tell him to be quiet. In the Bible he is not the only such person. Abraham asked for Sodom, Moses pleaded for the people, and the psalmists asked again and again: How long? Prayer is not hiding your questions. It is bringing them to God. If there is a question you have kept in your heart and never dared to ask, you can say it quietly today. You do not have to say it beautifully, and you do not have to have a conclusion. He hears the question to the end before he answers. He has his own time. What we must learn is not to answer for him first, and not to close the door for ourselves first.
@@4149@@
The first deviation is to teach the mark in 9:4 as “the condition for being saved is sighing,” as if sighing were a lesson, and only sighing enough counts. The text says God sees those who sigh. He sees their hearts. He does not turn the sigh into a price paid in exchange for grace. The main line of the whole book of Ezekiel is that God, for the sake of his own name, personally becomes the shepherd and gives a new heart and a new spirit. We want to let the weary know they are still in his care, not make those who have no strength to sigh feel that they have been left out.
@@4150@@
The second deviation is to draw the mark in 9:4 and the seal in Revelation as one and the same picture, and then work out who will have it, who will not, and when. Each of the two books has its own setting. To hear an echo is fine. To stack them into one timetable is not. Whenever a sermon uses a pattern like “so we can know that at such a time such a thing will happen,” it has gone off course. We want to make the congregation love the Lord more and be more patient, not more panicked.
@@4151@@
The third deviation is to dramatize the killing in verses 5 and 6, describing the old, the young, and the infants one by one, in order to frighten people. If the hearers are left with only a horror picture and do not know more of God’s righteousness and his marking, this passage has been taught wrongly. We do not enlarge it, and we do not gloss over it lightly.
@@4152@@
The fourth deviation is to use this chapter to suggest that those who have lost loved ones, or who were left behind, are so because they were not good enough, or because God’s judgment fell on them. No one’s death can be explained by this chapter. Being left behind is not a fault. Do not press for details, and do not urge “be strong”; let those who have lost someone still be able to weep before God.
@@4153@@
The fifth deviation goes in two directions: one side says “God is cruel,” and the other, in order to defend God, shuts people’s questions with the single sentence “God is righteous.” The first sees only the sword, and the second suppresses the prophet’s plea. The prophet fell on his face and asked: Will you destroy all of them? God heard the question to the end. If we are more anxious than God to close this question, we have gone off course. We must leave room for people to ask honestly, and at the same time let God’s heart and God’s righteousness be seen together.
@@4154@@
I have seen people in the church who were hurt or met with great injustice, and no one spoke up for them. Am I also one of those who “did not sigh”?
@@4155@@
Not necessarily. Sighing does not have only one form. Some people say it on the spot, some cannot say it on the spot and only weep after they go home, and some do not know what to do in a situation and only understand after a long time. This chapter is not meant to measure whether you have hurt “enough.” You can first ask yourself three things. First, have I become used to it? Second, do I have someone I can tell? Third, what is one small step I can take? Perhaps it is quietly asking a question in private, perhaps praying for that person, perhaps sharing a meal with him. If you have been hurt in such a matter, please take care of yourself first. You do not need to rush to forgive, and you do not need to rush to explain. God’s mark does not depend on what you show. He sees you first.
@@4156@@
The first problem: we are very used to “being used to it.” When there is unfairness at home, we say: Forget it, don’t air the family’s dirty laundry. When something is wrong at work, we say: Less trouble is better than more. When something at church breaks people’s hearts, we say: Don’t disturb the fellowship, don’t make anyone stumble. After a while the sighing is hidden inside, and we do not even know that we are sighing. Verse 4 of chapter 9 tells us that what God pays special attention to is those who “sigh over these things,” not those who are “used to it.” You can begin like this: this week, find someone you trust and say in one sentence: I have been very sad about something lately, I just have not said it. You are not looking for an answer, only to be heard.
@@4157@@
The second problem: we do not dare to ask God questions, thinking that asking means not believing. Especially when we have lost a loved one and are in pain in body and mind, the people around us often say: Don’t think too much, this is God’s will. But the prophet in verse 8, in the midst of the sound of killing, cried out a very big question, and God did not rebuke him. You can practice like this: write on a piece of paper the question you have not dared to ask, and as the first sentence write: Lord, I have a question. You do not need to write an answer, only write it out. Then say to God: Please hear my question to the end. If someone near you is in this kind of situation, please do not hurry to say “You must have faith.” First sit with him for a while.
@@4158@@
My family member has passed away, and people often say “This is God’s will.” I feel very upset inside. What can I do?
@@4159@@
You may feel upset, you may cry, and you do not have to feel that you are lacking in faith because of it. This chapter is not meant to explain your loss. No one’s death can be explained by this chapter, and please do not let others use it to speak to you in that way. The prophet fell on his face in the midst of the sound of killing and cried, “Ah, Lord,” and God did not rebuke him. You can do the same and simply say: Ah, Lord, I am in great pain. As for those who say “This is God’s will,” most of them want to comfort you but do not know how. You can tell them: Right now I only need you to be with me. If your grief is so deep that you cannot sleep, cannot eat, or have thoughts of hurting yourself, please find a pastor, someone you trust, or professional help as soon as possible. God remembers you, and he remembers the one you love.
@@4160@@
VII. Living Out 321: The Inkhorn and Lying on the Ground
@@4161@@
Jesus is my Role Model. Luke 19:41 says that when Jesus “saw the city,” he “wept over it.” He is not a bystander. He is the one who truly walked through all the sighing: he wept, he interceded, he bore it, and in the end he said, “It is finished.” Those who follow him learn his way: to see the weight of sin not with the eyes of someone on high, but with eyes full of tears.
@@4162@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 9 not to analyze weapons or calculate times, and not to defend God, but to let the text ask us: Have I become used to it? Do I want to be the judge, or am I willing to be one who sighs? Every time you read about the sword, please also read about the inkhorn. Every time you read “don’t spare,” please also read about the one who records the names.
@@4163@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who makes us sigh over sin, and it is also the Spirit who, when we cannot find words, intercedes for us “with groanings which can’t be uttered” (Romans 8:26). Each day, take a quiet moment and ask: Lord, what is there today that you want me to sigh over? Then wait a while, and do not rush to do anything.
@@4164@@
Let Jesus be King. The prophet’s position is lying on the ground. He did not grab the power of “I will explain.” To let Jesus be King is, in the face of judgment and difficulty, to put down the urge to be the judge, and also to put down the pride of wanting to end the question with one sentence, and to hand the question over to him. Selflessness is my heart being one with his heart: not judging in self-righteousness, and not becoming used to it in numbness.
@@4165@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The man in linen said only: “I have done as you have commanded me.” He did not say, “I did well.” If in some matter we intercede for someone, accompany a person, or keep a silence, please do not say “look what I did.” All the glory goes to the Lord who recorded our names first.
@@4166@@
Build God’s system. The six men and the one man each have their own entrustment, and each does only the portion God commanded, no more and no less. A group that belongs to God has some who are like the prophet, willing to lie on the ground; some who are like the one who records names, willing to remember every person’s name and heartache; and some who simply do as they are told, one thing after another. In such a fellowship, those who sigh are not treated as a nuisance but as people seen by God.
@@4167@@
How is “It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” related to the “falling on the ground” in this chapter?
@@4168@@
The relationship is very close. To lie on the ground is to remove myself from the center. When we meet something that breaks our hearts and that we cannot change, there are two easy reactions: one is to want to be the judge, and the other is to want to be a bystander. In this chapter the prophet does a third thing: he lies down and hands the question over. This is a small exercise in selflessness: I am not the last one to explain; I am only someone standing before him. You can practice like this: each day spend five minutes, and over one thing that breaks your heart, say only one sentence: Ah, Lord. Then name one person and intercede for them. After a while you will find that your heart is slowly drawing closer to his heart.
@@4169@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Mark
@@4170@@
The proud person says: “I see very clearly, this city deserves to be judged.” He likes to be the judge and does not like to be one who sighs. The text says to him: what God records are those who sigh and cry over these things. Pride is leaving man’s position, grabbing God’s power, and wanting to take God’s place. To return to our position is to admit: judgment is his business, and my place is to lie down. You do not have to say it all at once. First say one sentence to God: Lord, I often want to be the judge; please put my heart back in the place of sighing.
@@4171@@
The fearful person says: “If the judgment comes, will I be left out? Do I have that mark?” This chapter is not an exam. God’s mark is drawn by him, not bought by your performance. The main line of the whole book of Ezekiel is that God, for the sake of his own name, personally becomes the shepherd and gives a new heart and a new spirit. The fearful person can first stop counting and just say to him: Lord, I am afraid; please be with me.
@@4172@@
The ashamed person says: “I do not even have the strength to sigh, I am only numb, I must have been left out.” God sees those who sigh, and he also sees those who do not even have the strength to sigh. Shame says “you are not good enough,” and the gospel says “he saw you first.” The ashamed person does not need to make himself good first, and does not need to sigh loudly enough first. Today, just let yourself be seen: say one sentence to one person, or say one “Ah” to God.
@@4173@@
The wounded person says: “I was left behind, I lost people, and I do not know where God is.” God did not rebuke the prophet’s plea, and he will not rebuke yours. Being left behind is not a fault. You do not need to explain, and you do not need to rush to forgive anyone. You can be like the prophet: lie down and say your “Ah.” Those who accompany you should not hurry to say “You must be strong,” but only say: I am here, I am with you.
@@4174@@
First, this week, for five minutes each day, read 9:3–4, and then say to God: “Lord, you have seen my sighing.” Do not ask, do not argue; once said, that is enough. And write down the date.
@@4175@@
Second, write down one thing that breaks your heart and that you cannot change. Beside it, write two columns: this is a small step I can take; this is the part I cannot do and must give to him. Write down which day you wrote it.
@@4176@@
Third, find a brother or sister who is sighing, give no answers, and only say: “I see how hard it has been for you.” Then pray with him for three minutes. Say his name, and the time you plan to meet.
@@4177@@
Fourth, this week, deliberately give up one numbing habit once: scroll less once, turn away less once, and use that same time to pray for that matter. Write down which day and what time.
@@4178@@
Fifth, take one question you have never dared to ask God and write it on paper, with the first sentence: “Lord, I have a question.” Then say which companion or pastor you are willing to share it with, and say the date.
@@4179@@
I declare: God sees my sighing, and he has already recorded my name; I do not have to prove anything.
@@4180@@
I declare: I will not be a self-righteous judge; I am willing to be one who sighs and one who intercedes.
@@4181@@
I declare: I can lie down before him and say my question, and he hears the question to the end.
@@4182@@
I declare: the glory is still standing at the threshold; I am willing to turn back and invite him to stay.
@@4183@@
What is one thing I can do right away this week, so that I do not stop at only listening to the sermon?
@@4184@@
The simplest thing is to write one sentence on a piece of paper today: One thing lately that breaks my heart and that I cannot change is ______. You do not have to write much, and you do not have to show it to anyone. Then under that sentence, write one name: In this matter, whom can I intercede for? Next, spend three minutes reading that name aloud to God and say: Lord, you have seen. That is all; once written, it is enough. This is a small exercise from chapter 9: not solving, but being seen, and seeing others. At the next small group meeting, just say: This week I lay down before God for someone. You do not need to say the content. That is enough.
@@4185@@
What is most distinctive about chapter 9 is that it begins with a shout and ends with a quiet report. In between there are swords, there is killing, there is the prophet who was left, and there are the men who went through the whole city. But in the deepest place of the whole chapter there is one very small thing: a man with an inkhorn drew a mark on the foreheads of those who sigh and cry.
@@4186@@
God is righteous, and this chapter is not light. God also remembers people, and this chapter is equally not light. The ones he counts first are not the strongest, and not the most useful, but those who sigh in the dark. He also did not rebuke a prophet lying on the ground. He heard the prophet’s question to the end.
@@4187@@
So please remember three key points of this lesson. To shout: God calls out loudly. To record: he first records those who sigh. To leave: he leaves one person behind, lying on the ground, interceding. The focus of this lesson is not how heavy the sword is, not whether you have the strength to sigh, and not how others see you, but Jesus, who recorded our names first.
@@4188@@
Lord, thank you that you see our sighing and remember our names. Forgive our habits and our numbness, and forgive us for being too quick to want to be the judge. Be with everyone who is grieving, and with all who have been left behind, so that before you we may honestly say: Ah, Lord. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ. Amen.

<p><strong>The wheels and the cherubim move together, because there is one spirit.</strong></p><p>Two things to see in this passage. First, the prophet says: this is the living creature that I saw by the River Chebar. In chapter 1 he saw them; here he recognizes what is familiar.</p><p>Second, verse 17: “When they stood, these stood. When they mounted up, these mounted up with them; for the spirit of the living creature was in them.” The movement of the wheels and the cherubim is not separate. They are not two parts each doing its own thing, but are moved by the same spirit.</p><p>This is the most important sentence in the whole vision. What looks complicated is held together by one Spirit. One moves and all move; one stops and all stop.</p><p>We do not need to pin down the meaning of each of the four faces, and we do not need to draw a diagram. What this picture gives us is: there is One who leads, and all creatures move with him.</p><p>Landing: The church is many parts, each with its own task, and without the same Spirit it is chaos. With the same Spirit, you do not need to worry about your pace and others’ pace. First ask: Where is the Spirit going? Then follow.</p>

@@4269@@
<p><strong>This is the heaviest sentence in the whole book: he went out from the threshold.</strong></p><p>One verse, no adjectives. The glory went out from over the threshold of the house and stood over the cherubim.</p><p>There is no shouting and no earthquake. He simply went out. Like a person quietly stepping out of the house, not leaving in a burst of anger, but going out only after many times of explaining.</p><p>In chapter 8 people put idols into his temple and made him “go far off from my sanctuary.” In chapter 9 he stood up, and in chapter 10 he went out. At every step, people moved first and then he moved. He was pushed away, but he was not defeated. He went out himself.</p><p>This is the saddest moment in the Old Testament. If you feel that one day he is far from you, please remember that this sentence is not the end. The line of the whole book goes on: he will return, and he will give a new heart.</p><p>Landing: When something is heavy, we should not hurry around it. At this moment, stop for a while. If there was a time in your life when you felt God had left you, this verse is not a condemnation. It is an acknowledgment: the loss is real. He has not forgotten, and he will come back.</p>

@@4272@@
<p><strong>The glory stops at the east gate, and the departure is not yet complete.</strong></p><p>The cherubim spread their wings before the prophet’s eyes and lift off the ground, and the wheels go with them. They reach the east gate and stop. The glory is above them.</p><p>In this passage the prophet says a weighty sentence: “I knew that they were cherubim.” He has been watching all along, and at this moment he recognizes them. In chapter 1 he said only “living creatures.” Only here can he say the name.</p><p>One detail must not be missed: they went “straight forward.” Chapter 1 says so, and here also. It means: his leaving has a direction. He is not fleeing in panic. He is taking his glory to where he intends to go.</p><p>The east gate. This is the main gate of the temple, and also the direction of the sunrise. The glory goes east, and the next chapter continues. For now we see only this: he stops at the door, and he has not gone far.</p><p>Landing: Recognizing him often comes after the experience. The prophet took ten chapters before he said “I knew.” Perhaps you are still watching and have not yet recognized him. Do not hurry. Keep watching. He will not be far from you.</p>
