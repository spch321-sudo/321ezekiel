<p><strong>The mouth was locked by God, and it is opened by God.</strong></p><p>The last three verses gather the whole chapter together. God is giving that silence its use.</p><p>Landing it: if you are in a place of having no words right now, this verse is an invitation. You do not need to force yourself to speak. The one who made a person mute is the same one who lets a person speak again.</p>

I. Put the Cauldron on the Fire (24:1–5)
@@10698@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. I once visited an old sister’s kitchen. On the stove sat a very old iron pot, with a ring of brown rust spots on the bottom. She said her grandmother gave her this pot when she married, and she had used it for fifty years. The rust could not be scrubbed off. If it was scrubbed off, it grew back in a few days. She said: It doesn’t matter, the soup is still good. But as I looked at that pot, I thought: some things, however hard you try, are only covered over and not removed. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 24, which is a cauldron set on the fire, a cauldron whose rust cannot be removed.
@@10699@@
Verses 1 and 2 are very special. Yahweh’s word came to the prophet and told him to write down this day: “The king of Babylon drew close to Jerusalem this same day.” Please think about the prophet’s position. He lived in the land of exile, far from Jerusalem. The day he wrote down here was the very day the faraway city was put under siege. This is not teaching us to count days or make a timetable. It is a witness: God’s word was not a note added afterwards. On the very day it happened, it had already come to his servant.
@@10700@@
Verse 3 says to utter a parable to the rebellious house: put the cauldron on the fire, and pour water into it. Verses 4 and 5 say to put the pieces of meat in, every good piece, the thigh and the shoulder, and fill it with the choice bones. Take the choice of the flock, pile the wood under it, and make it boil. Please notice what is in this picture: good, choice, the best. What is in this cauldron is not spoiled meat. It is first-rate material.
@@10701@@
This is the most jarring part of the parable. The people living in Jerusalem believed they were safe, because they were the “best meat” in that cauldron: God’s city, God’s temple, God’s chosen people. They thought the cauldron protected them, but God said: the fire under the cauldron has already been lit. Sometimes our greatest danger is not knowing that we are not good. It is believing too much that we are good.
@@10702@@
I think of an elder who made very good soup. She said that putting good ingredients into a pot does not mean the pot is clean. The taste of the soup is often first the taste of the pot. We are often like this too: we put in the best, the best talents, the best service, the best reputation, and think that as long as the ingredients are good enough, the pot does not need checking. But what God looks at first is the pot. He does not ask what you put in. He asks what this pot is really like.
@@10703@@
Verse 2 tells the prophet to write down the day. Does this mean we can also count days and make timetables?
@@10704@@
No. The day in verses 1 and 2 is a record of an event that had already happened, and it echoes the account in 2 Kings 25:1. Its purpose is that later people could see: God’s word really came, and it was not for predicting the future. What the prophet wrote down was a witness, not a calculation. We also do not need to live in anxiety over dates because of “this day.” The fruit the text wants from us is patience and loving the Lord more, not panic. If you find yourself unsettled by some date or prediction, you can come back to this sentence: God’s word came to his servant, and he rules over history.

II. The Rusted Cauldron (24:6–14)
@@10706@@
Verse 6 is the first heavy note of the chapter: “Woe to the bloody city, to the cauldron whose rust is in it.” “Woe” is a sigh, not only a rebuke. The name of this cauldron is “the bloody city.” In the last chapter we just read that the last line of this city’s list was “you have forgotten me.” The rust on the cauldron is no accident. It was left behind over long years.
@@10707@@
We easily feel that rust is something in other people’s pots. But if you have seen the old pot in your own home, you know that rust does not grow in a day. It is left little by little, every time it is put away wet, every time we think, I will wash it tomorrow. God said the rust of this city is the same: not one great event, but year after year, pouring blood on the rock and not covering it with dust, until they are used to it and no longer feel it. Habit is the deepest rust.
@@10708@@
Verses 7 and 8 say that the blood shed in the city was poured on the bare rock, not on the ground, and not covered with dust. In ancient times, when blood was shed, people covered it with dust so that it would be taken away. But this city poured the blood on the smooth, bare rock, and let it lie exposed there, as if crying out to heaven: do you see? We will not describe those scenes. We only remember this one thing: what is not covered, God sees.
@@10709@@
Verses 9 to 11 are the fire: pile the wood high, kindle the fire, boil the meat until it falls apart, and even empty the cauldron and set it on the coals until it glows, to melt the filth inside and burn away the rust on it. Some people read this and think: does this mean suffering is to purify people? Please be careful. We do not draw the conclusion “suffering is cleansing” from here, because verse 12 right after it says it very plainly.
@@10710@@
Verse 12 says: “She is weary with toil; yet her great rust, rust by fire, doesn’t leave her.” Verse 13 says: “In your filthiness is lewdness. Because I have cleansed you and you weren’t cleansed.” See this diagnosis: rust that fire cannot burn off. This is not saying people are not worth saving. It is saying that people cannot make themselves clean by their own strength, or by suffering more. Cleansing is God’s work. It is not something a person gets by suffering a little more.
@@10711@@
The voice in verse 14 is heavy: what God has spoken will happen, he will not go back, will not spare, and will not repent. This is not God being willful. This is a Lord who, once he has spoken, always does it. Verse 13 also has a word of hope: “until I have caused my wrath toward you to rest.” Judgment has a time when it rests. In later chapters God will cleanse his people himself (see 36:25). Today in this chapter we only see this: the rust of this cauldron can be removed only by God.
@@10712@@
Since God says this rust cannot be removed even by fire, does suffering have any “purifying” effect on a person at all?
@@10713@@
Please be careful: chapter 24 is not saying “suffering is purification.” Verse 12 says it very clearly: this rust cannot be removed by fire, which means that a person cannot wash himself clean by suffering a bit more. This chapter is a diagnosis of the whole city of Jerusalem. It is not an explanation for a person who is suffering. If you are suffering, you do not need to say to yourself: I am suffering because I am not clean enough. Suffering is not the tool of cleansing. Cleansing is something God does himself. What you can do is hand over to him the pain you cannot swallow and the rust you cannot remove, instead of driving yourself with suffering.
@@10714@@
III. The Desire of Your Eyes (24:15–24)
@@10715@@
Verse 15 again says: “Also Yahweh’s word came to me, saying.” In this section we need to slow down. Verse 16 says God told the prophet: I will take away from you the desire of your eyes with a stroke, yet you shall neither mourn nor weep, neither shall your tears run down. Verse 18 says: in the morning I spoke to the people, and at evening my wife died. In the morning I did as I was commanded.
@@10716@@
These are the quietest and most painful lines in the whole book of Ezekiel. The text does not give us her name. It does not tell us how she went. It does not tell us what the prophet did that night. Three sentences, without one adjective. We do not press, and we do not add a single word for the text. Please remember one thing: God called her “the desire of your eyes.” She was not a tool, not a prop for a sign. She was the desire of the prophet’s eyes. God knew, and God spoke out that name.
@@10717@@
In verse 17 God told the prophet: sigh, but not aloud, make no mourning for the dead, bind your headdress on you, put your sandals on your feet, don’t cover your lips, and don’t eat mourner’s bread. These are all everyday things: a headdress, sandals, a meal. The prophet had to dress as usual, go out as usual, and face people as usual. There was a brother who, the day after a family member died, went back to the office, and a coworker said: Take care of yourself in your grief. He said thank you, and then opened his computer. He later told me: Pastor, that day what I feared most was someone telling me to be strong. There is a kind of pain where you must go out as usual and answer as usual, while inside everything has long since collapsed.
@@10718@@
Please remember this above all: this was a special sign for Ezekiel alone, not a rule for all who have lost someone. Before Lazarus’s tomb, Jesus wept (John 11:35). He did not tell anyone not to weep. So today, if someone has lost the desire of their eyes, cry if you want to cry, and be silent if you don’t want to speak. No one may use Ezekiel to make demands of you.
@@10719@@
In verses 19 to 24, the people asked the prophet: what do these things mean to us that you do so? God said: I will profane my sanctuary, the pride of your power, the desire of your eyes, and that which your soul pities. Do you see? God used the same phrase: the desire of your eyes. The prophet lost the desire of his eyes, and God would lose his sanctuary. Verse 21 says “my sanctuary,” and that is God’s own dwelling place. God was not watching from the side as the prophet hurt. He himself was also in this.
@@10720@@
Verse 23 says the people will also not mourn or weep, but will pine away in their iniquities and moan one toward another. The conclusion in verse 24 is: when this comes, you will know that I am the Lord Yahweh. We do not turn this verse into an explanation for anyone. This chapter does not explain why it was she. The text does not say, so we do not say it for the text.
@@10721@@
Stopping at the place where the text does not speak is not coldness, but respect. For someone in loss, the thing most feared is being explained to in a hurry. A sentence like “there is a reason for this” often turns a person’s grief into a problem that needs solving. What the text gives us is a different kind of quiet: the prophet’s wife was the desire of his eyes, and God knew; the prophet put on his sandals as usual and sighed as usual, and God knew that too. This is enough for us to sit down, keep company, and say nothing.
@@10722@@
Why did God take away the prophet’s wife? Does this mean every loss has a “reason” arranged by God?
@@10723@@
The text does not answer this question. It only says this was a sign, and does not explain why it was his wife. Many interpreters suggest that we stop where the text stops. We also do not turn it into a general rule, and say to anyone who has lost a loved one: there is a reason for this, or this is God’s plan. This chapter tells us only two things: first, God knew she was the desire of the prophet’s eyes; second, God himself also hurts in loss. If you are bereaved, you do not need a reason in order to grieve, and you do not need to be asked “how did she go?” You can be quiet, and you can cry.
@@10724@@
IV. Light from the Original Language and No More Mute (24:6, 16, 27; 25–27)
@@10725@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth looking at slowly. The first is “rust,” חֶלְאָה (khel'ah), in verses 6, 11 and 12. This word appears five times in the chapter, and it means rust, and also filth and built-up grime. If you have used an old pot, you know that rust is not grown all at once, but left behind day after day. This word reminds us: the things in our hearts that we “can’t scrub off however we try” do not need to be condemned right away. They need to be honestly seen: I can’t remove it, only he can.
@@10726@@
The second is “the desire of your eyes,” מַחְמַד (makhmad), verse 16. This word comes from the verb “to delight in, to desire.” It speaks of no ordinary thing. It is the person, the one thing, whose sight makes your eyes say “good” and your heart feel at rest. God chose this word, which shows he understands: what was lost was someone you felt was good the moment you saw her. He did not say “that was only a figure of speech.” He said: I know the desire of your eyes.
@@10727@@
The third is “no more mute,” תֵאָלֵם (te'alem), verse 27. This word means “to be shut up in the mouth” or “to be unable to speak.” The prophet’s mouth was shut in 3:26, and opened only when God spoke. In verse 27, God promised: you will speak to the one who escaped, and be no more mute. This sentence is itself a comfort: silence is not forever. About this “mute,” some think it is literally not speaking to the people, some think it is speaking only what God commands, and some think the point lies in the prophet’s role and timing. We set these views side by side, and do not make any one of them a condition of faith.
@@10728@@
Verses 25 and 26 say God will take away the glory they lean on and delight in, and the sons and daughters their eyes desire and their hearts treasure; that day, the one who escaped will come with the report. Verse 27 says the prophet will open his mouth. Do you see the order of this chapter? First the cauldron, then the loss, then a season without speaking, and at last the mouth will be opened again. We do not hurry any silent person to speak up quickly. God himself says: in his time.
@@10729@@
There was a sister who, after a family member died, could not say a single word of prayer for most of a year. Every Lord’s Day she sat in the last row, just sitting. She said that at that time she felt she was the least spiritual person in the church. Later an elder said to her only this: You came and sat, and that is enough. She said she has remembered that sentence to this day. A silent person does not need to be hurried. A silent person needs to be waited for. God himself waits in this way too.
@@10730@@
I can’t speak right now, and I don’t want to pray. Is this not spiritual enough?
@@10731@@
No. The “mute” in 3:26 and 24:27 is something God opens in his own time. It is not telling the prophet to hurry and speak. The days when you cannot speak do not mean you do not believe, and do not mean God has left. Romans 8:26 says: “the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings which can’t be uttered.” Even a sigh, the Spirit understands. You can say just one sentence to God: Lord, you know. Then pause, and there is no need to say more. If you have not been able to speak for a long time and it feels very heavy, please also find a pastor or someone you trust to be with you, and if necessary, seek professional help.
@@10732@@
The first deviation is to teach the “neither mourn nor weep” of verses 16 and 17 as how the bereaved ought to behave. This was a sign for Ezekiel alone, not a rule. When the bereaved hear this, they should be allowed to weep, not told not to weep; Jesus himself also shed tears.
@@10733@@
The second deviation is to say to the bereaved or the suffering: “be strong,” “this is God’s plan,” “she was taken for a reason,” or even to hint that it was someone’s sin. Chapter 24 does not explain this. This chapter is a judgment on Jerusalem, not an explanation of individual suffering. We should let those who have lost be accompanied, not explained to.
@@10734@@
The third deviation is to teach the cauldron and rust of verses 6 to 14 as “suffering is purification,” and to urge the suffering to hold on and they will get better. The text says this rust cannot be removed even by fire. Cleansing is God’s work, not something gained by people suffering more. We should let the suffering look more to God, not blame themselves more.
@@10735@@
The fourth deviation is to use the date in verses 1 and 2 to count days and make timetables, or to point the “bloody city” at some country or city today. This was a word to Jerusalem at that time. It does not name any place in the modern world for us. The fruit is patience and loving the Lord more, not panic.
@@10736@@
The fifth deviation is to speak only of judgment (the cauldron, the fire, no repenting) and not of God’s pain, or to teach the prophet’s obedience as saying that not obeying in pain is unbelief, or to teach “no more mute” as hurrying the silent to speak. In verse 21 God says “my sanctuary,” and he is also in this. The prophet’s sign is not a requirement for everyone, and silent people should be given time.
@@10737@@
God says “my sanctuary” will be profaned. Does God really hurt too? How should I understand it?
@@10738@@
Some stress God’s sovereignty and justice: God has the right to deal with his own sanctuary, and judgment is his seriousness about sin. Others stress that “my sanctuary” shows this is God’s own dwelling place, that he is not a bystander, and that his heart also hurts in it. Still others remind us that the prophet uses human language, and we need not read “pain” as exactly the same as a human emotion. The position of these notes is: God is not cold. He says “my sanctuary,” and he is in this too. If you are in loss, this is not asking you to feel sorry for God, and you do not need to take care of God’s heart. He has come to be with you. This picture is a comfort, and also a reminder: he is not a judge far away from you, but a Lord who is willing to enter your pain.
@@10739@@
The first question: we are very used to “restrain your grief” and “be strong.” In Chinese funerals, we care a lot about etiquette, appearance, and not losing composure, and when relatives say “restrain your grief and accept the change,” it often also means: don’t cry too long. Men don’t cry, the eldest son must hold up, the spiritual person must have a testimony. But the line in chapter 24, “Sigh, but not aloud,” was a sign for the prophet alone. It is not a standard we can use to demand things of others. You can begin like this: in front of someone who has lost, say one fewer “be strong,” and leave a little more quiet. Say only: I am here.
@@10740@@
The second question: we are very used to “taboo.” We feel that talking about death and loss is unlucky. So around someone who has lost, it is often very quiet, and relatives and friends do not know what to say, so they stay away. It is not that they don’t care. They are afraid of saying the wrong thing. But this leaves the bereaved lonelier. Chapter 24 tells us: God has no taboo at all. He called her “the desire of your eyes,” and he knew. You can begin like this: this week, send a short message to someone who has lost a loved one, and say: I thought of you. Don’t ask how she went, don’t advise, don’t explain.
@@10741@@
Someone near me has lost a loved one. I don’t know what to say so as not to hurt them.
@@10742@@
The safest is to say less and be there more. First, don’t press for details, and don’t ask “how did she go?” or “are you sad?” Second, don’t say “be strong,” “God’s plan,” or “she was taken for a reason.” Third, say just one sentence: I am here, you don’t need to speak, and you don’t need to leave. Fourth, after the meeting, check on him once alone, and ask: Right now, do you need quiet, or someone with you? Fifth, if he mentions hurting himself, a very long stretch of having no strength, or not being able to go on, please accompany him to find a pastor and professionals. Accompanying is not about saying things beautifully. It is letting him know: he has not been left to one side.
@@10743@@
VII. Living Out 321: Hand the Three Over to Him
@@10744@@
Jesus is my Role Model. Before Lazarus’s tomb, he wept. He did not tell anyone not to weep, and he did not use a verse to stop anyone’s tears. We who follow him learn his way: in other people’s grief, first keep company, then speak.
@@10745@@
The Bible is my Standard. When we read chapter 24, we do not add one word for the text. The text does not explain why she died, so we do not explain; the text says God called her the desire of his eyes, so we remember that one sentence. The standard the text gives us is to let people see God’s heart, and not to explain on God’s behalf.
@@10746@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. When we cannot speak, the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings that cannot be uttered. Each day, take a quiet moment, say nothing, and ask only one sentence: Lord, you know.
@@10747@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is admitting that three things are not what a person can do: the rust, I cannot remove; the pain, I cannot swallow; the mouth, I cannot open. No longer say “I will get through this by myself.” Hand these three over to him, and let him be King, and let him keep company with me.
@@10748@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we have walked through a very deep loss, do not say “I finally made it through.” It was he who walked with us, and he who, in his time, opened our mouths. Glory to the Lord who weeps with us.
@@10749@@
To build God’s system. A God’s-system community is not a community that forbids people to weep. It is a community where those who have lost are seen, accompanied, and sometimes quietly waited for. Life first, then relationships, then ministry: first be held by him, and then there is the stability of relationships, and then there are words to say to others. A fellowship like this is a small piece of God’s system on earth.
@@10750@@
What is the connection between “Let Jesus be King” and “do not get through it by yourself”?
@@10751@@
The connection is very direct. When many of us lose someone, the first thought is: I have to hold up, I must not cause others trouble, I have to take care of everyone. This holding up is often the opposite of selflessness. It thinks that by ourselves we can swallow the pain, scrub off the rust, and pry open the mouth. To let Jesus be King is to admit: these three things were never what a person can do. He is King, so he takes over the heaviest part, and I do not have to hold up. This is not irresponsibility. It is knowing that I am not God. You can practice like this: today say just one sentence to Jesus: This one thing I cannot hold up, I hand it to you, please keep me company.
@@10752@@
The proud type says: I can handle this myself, and I need neither people nor God. The text says to him: this rust cannot be removed even by fire. Pride is leaving man’s position, grabbing God’s power, and thinking that by oneself one can scrub off the rust and swallow the pain. To return to one’s position is to admit: this is not something I can do. You do not need to say it all at once. First say one sentence to God: Lord, this one thing, I will not carry by myself for now.
@@10753@@
The fearful type says: I am afraid that once I cry I won’t be able to stop, and that once I open my mouth I will fall apart. God’s answer is: I know the desire of your eyes, and I am here too. The fearful type should learn to change “afraid of being seen” into “being seen by him.” You can choose a very safe person and say just one sentence, without having to finish.
@@10754@@
The shame type says: I have a rust in my heart that I can’t scrub off however I try, and I am not worthy of God coming near. The sentence in verse 13 does not condemn you. It is a diagnosis: this rust cannot be removed by people, and only God can remove it. Shame says “you are dirty,” and the gospel says “he comes to cleanse.” The shame type does not need to scrub themselves clean first. Today, just hand that piece of rust to him.
@@10755@@
The wounded type says: I lost what mattered most, and I don’t want to hear anyone say anything anymore. God does not blame your silence, and this chapter is not asking you to be strong. The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you, and say the one sentence you are willing to say. Those who accompany should not hurry to say “you must have faith,” but only say: “I am here, I am with you.”
@@10756@@
First, this week find a quiet time, say nothing, and say just one sentence to God: “Lord, you know.” After saying it, pause, with no need to say more, and write down which day it was.
@@10757@@
Second, write down one rust you “can’t scrub off however you try” (a habit, a self-blame), and beside it write: This I cannot remove, please do it. Then put the paper where you can see it every day.
@@10758@@
Third, tell a companion one thing you have been holding up alone, in just one sentence, say the name and the time, and ask him to ask you about it once this week.
@@10759@@
Fourth, find someone who is in the middle of loss, and greet him once, only greet, do not press, do not advise, do not explain, and say: “I thought of you, I am here.” Say the name and the date.
@@10760@@
Fifth, if you are in pain yourself, set aside one hour for the grief or tiredness you have never had time to deal with, without demanding that you get better; and also tell a pastor or companion you trust, and do not hold up your service alone.
@@10761@@

The simplest thing is to find a quiet time today, say nothing, and say only to God: Lord, you know. Then pause for three minutes. Next choose one small action you can do today: send a message to someone in the middle of loss, writing only: I thought of you. Don’t ask, don’t advise, don’t explain. Or, if you yourself are in pain, tell just one person you trust: I have been very tired lately. No details are needed. At the next small group, say just one sentence: This week I did one thing, and there is no need to say what it was. This is a real beginning, and it does not need to be big or fast.
@@10766@@
Chapter 24 is a cauldron on the fire, and also a night when no one spoke. It begins with “put the cauldron on the fire,” in the middle is “the desire of your eyes,” and at the end is “no more mute.” This chapter is very heavy. We have not explained that loss, because the text does not explain it. We can only remember this: God called her the desire of his eyes, and God knew.
@@10767@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see his heart, God wants us to admit the rust we cannot remove, and God also wants us to know that he is in our loss. That God, in Jesus, came to the tomb and wept; and in his time he also opens our mouths.
@@10768@@
So please remember the three key points of this lesson: the cauldron, the desire of your eyes, no more mute. The focus of this lesson is not how strong you are, not whether you wept, and not what others think of you, but Jesus, who knows the desire of your eyes.
@@10769@@
Lord, thank you, you know the desire of our eyes. Please be with everyone who is in the middle of loss, so that they need not be strong, need not speak, and can still find rest before you. Remove the rust we cannot remove, and in your time, open our mouths. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, amen.

@@10797@@
Have you ever watched someone fall? The quickest thing in the heart is often not sympathy, but a soft “aha.” When Jerusalem fell, the neighbors around did not lift a hand. They only clapped, said “like all the nations,” remembered old accounts, and passed on the old hatred. Chapter 25 lets us see: at the moment when others hurt most, the sound that comes from the side, God hears it all. He first tells them to hear (25:3), then gives the reason, and every time ends with “they will know that I am Yahweh.” This chapter is not teaching us to count the neighbors’ sins. It asks us to look back at our own hearts.
@@10798@@
God hears the “aha” when someone falls. He keeps vengeance in his own hand, and he also wants us to step down from the judgment seat, and when others fall, to reach out and not clap.
@@10799@@
God told the prophet to face the four neighbors, Ammon, Moab, Edom and Philistia, and speak. Ammon said “aha” over the sanctuary profaned, the land made desolate and the people taken captive, clapping hands and stamping feet; Moab said “the house of Judah is like all the nations”; Edom and Philistia took vengeance and held hatred forever. Every nation has a “because” and a “therefore,” and every section ends with “they will know that I am Yahweh.”
@@10800@@
God hears the voice of the bystanders: contempt, scorn and old grudges are not small matters (25:3, 6, 8, 12, 15).
@@10801@@

Final integration:
Someone fell down.
The people beside him did not lift a hand.
They only said softly: aha.

No one heard.
Only one heard.

He said: hear.
He gave the reasons, one by one.
The hands that clapped, he remembers.
The words “like all the nations,” he remembers.
Old accounts, old hatred, he remembers it all.

He remembers, not to count your sins.
It is so that you will know: he is Yahweh.

Let man be man.
Let God be God.
In the place where others fall, do not clap, but bend down.

This is selflessness.
Selflessness is not having no feelings; selflessness is that I put down the judgment seat and return to his hand.

The definition of pride: leaving man’s position, grabbing God’s power, wanting to take God’s place. The pride of chapter 25 is clapping hands and stamping feet, announcing that others deserve it; it is holding old accounts in one’s own hand and refusing to hand them to God.
