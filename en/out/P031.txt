@@10214@@
Why “born of one mother”? Because Samaria was the capital of the northern kingdom and Jerusalem the capital of the southern kingdom. They were once one people, and later they split into two kingdoms, like one family dividing into two households. The same origin, the same covenant, the same choosing by God. Verse 3 also says that their habit of leaning on others began in Egypt, in their youth. God did not hide their early weakness, yet he did not strike them off the list because of it.
@@10215@@
The heaviest line in verse 4 is this: “They became mine, and they bore sons and daughters.” “They became mine” means they belonged to me. These few words are the easiest part of the whole chapter to miss. If a person had never been loved, there would be nothing to be abandoned from; this chapter hurts precisely because that belonging was always real. The one who leaves is walking away from a place that had her name on it.
@@10216@@
Now look at verses 5 to 10, the elder sister, Oholah. The text says she played the prostitute “when she was mine,” and doted on her neighbors, the Assyrians. Verse 6 describes those men: all clothed with blue, governors and rulers, all of them horsemen riding on horses. Please notice that the “backer” in this parable does not look evil at all. It is respectable, with uniforms, with rank, with horses, and it looks very capable. A backer never comes to us looking like an idol; it comes looking like “someone who can get things done.”
@@10217@@
A brother who ran a small business said to me that when he was young he worked hard to know powerful people, and he collected a whole box of name cards. He said: Pastor, it is not that I did not believe in God; I just felt that God was the last step and connections were the first step. He laughed first as he said it. I did not laugh, because I have a box of name cards of my own. Connections, savings, education, reputation: these things are not bad in themselves. What is bad is that slowly, in our hearts, we move them to a seat that only God may sit in.
@@10218@@
Verse 9 is the first turning point in the chapter. God says: “I delivered her into the hand of her lovers.” Whoever she leaned on, she fell into that one’s hand; the one she loved became the one who hurt her. This is not God taking pleasure in suffering; this is what the end of the road looks like. Verse 10 says her sons and daughters were drawn in with her. We will not paint those pictures; we will remember only one thing: when a person leaves God, it is never only his own matter. The people closest to him bear it with him. This is why God’s heart hurts so much.
@@10219@@
This parable in the chapter is so heavy. How should I read it so that I do not hurt myself or others?
@@10220@@
Remember three things first. First, this is a parable God speaks to two cities. It is about his covenant with his people being betrayed, and it is the cry of a forsaken heart; it does not describe or judge any individual person. Second, Ezekiel 16:60–63 says that God still establishes that everlasting covenant, so the direction of this parable in the end is restoration, not condemnation. Third, if you have been wounded, this chapter is not an accusation against you. You may stop, you do not need to finish reading, you do not need to say anything aloud, and you do not need to forgive first or be strong first. If it is hard for you to listen, please find a pastor or companion you trust and talk slowly.
@@10221@@
II. She Saw It, Yet Still Lusted (23:11–21)
@@10222@@
Now it is the younger sister’s turn. Verse 11 says: “Her sister Oholibah saw this, yet she was more corrupt in her lusting than she.” What stabs most in this verse is two words: she saw. It is not that she did not know. She stood on the same street, and she watched how her sister fell. But seeing is not the same as learning.
@@10223@@
I know a middle-aged brother. When he was young, his father put all his savings on one business partner, the business collapsed, and the family moved three times. From childhood he told himself: in my whole life I will never put my fate in another person’s hands. Then at forty he handed his savings to a “very capable friend.” Later he said to me: Pastor, I had clearly seen it before. He was not stupid. There was a voice in his heart that kept saying: my situation is different.
@@10224@@
Verses 14 to 16 are even more vivid. She saw men portrayed on the wall, the images of the Chaldeans portrayed with red, and as soon as she saw them she lusted after them and sent messengers to them. Please notice that what she fell in love with, at first, was only a picture on a wall. The eyes go first, the heart follows, and the feet go last. We do not use walls today; we have screens: other people’s houses, other people’s children’s report cards, other people’s seemingly secure lives. After looking long enough, we begin to feel that over there is safer, and that the God over here seems farther away.
@@10225@@
Verses 17 and 18 are a very quiet turning point: she got what she wanted, and found that her soul was alienated from them. Then God says: “my soul was alienated from her, just like my soul was alienated from her sister.” Please see the tone of this sentence. It is not a roar; it is a heart slowly turning away. God is not a cold judge; he is the one who has been abandoned. If you see this heart first, the chapter is no longer only a verdict. It is a letter written with tears.
@@10226@@
Verses 19 to 21 say that she remembered the days of her youth in Egypt, and walked the old road again. We will not explain these verses word by word; we will look at only one meaning: how deep a person’s habits go. When a person is young he learns where to lean for safety, and as an adult he thinks he has changed, but when the pressure comes, his feet walk back by themselves to that old road. This is not condemning anyone. It is saying that we cannot change ourselves just by watching what happened to others; what we need is to come back before God and let him change our hearts.
@@10227@@
I know clearly that the road leads nowhere, yet I keep walking back. Am I beyond saving?
@@10228@@
No. The “saw it, yet still lusted” in this chapter is not meant to condemn you; it asks you to be honest: knowing alone is not enough. You can do this. First, stop lecturing yourself and saying “How could I be so stupid,” because that only lets shame push you further away. Second, find out when your old road is easiest to walk back onto, whether when you are tired, lonely, or afraid, and write that moment down. Third, at that moment, stop for three minutes and say to the Lord: I have come. Fourth, tell a companion and ask him to check on you once this week. God has not put down your name. Come back; you do not have to fix yourself first.
@@10229@@
III. You Will Drink Your Sister’s Cup (23:22–35)
@@10230@@
In verses 22 to 27, God says that he will stir up against her those she once loved and then was alienated from. This sentence is very accurate: what she once wanted so much later became what troubled her most. We will not paint the pictures of verses 25 and 26; we will look only at why God says it: he wants her leaning to stop, so that she no longer looks up to Assyria and no longer remembers Egypt (verse 27). The purpose of judgment is to take away those false backers, so that she finally sees there is only One she can lean on.
@@10231@@
Then comes the key to the chapter, verse 31: “You have walked in the way of your sister; therefore I will give her cup into your hand.” In the Bible a cup is sometimes a cup of blessing and sometimes a cup of bitterness, and this one is a cup of bitterness. Verse 32 says the cup is deep and large, and it contains much. Nobody can drain a deep and wide cup in one swallow; it is drunk down little by little.
@@10232@@
I think of an old ship’s captain. He said that every reef on the sea chart is clearly marked. The ships that are wrecked are not ships without charts; the helmsman took one look and said: my ship is different. A person goes wherever the road leads, and no one needs to push him off; it is enough that he keeps walking forward. Some will say this is too cruel. But what is cruel is the reef. In front of the reef, God first sent prophets, one after another, to set up markers. The sister’s end is the marker set up for the younger sister to see.
@@10233@@
So please be careful not to preach this cup as God scaring people. This cup is the end of the road; it is not God’s wish. God himself said: “my soul was alienated from her” (verse 18). That is pain, not triumph. The effect should be to draw us closer to God, not to make us more afraid of being thrown away.
@@10234@@
Verse 35 gives the root: “Because you have forgotten me, and cast me behind your back.” To forget is not a problem of memory; it is to put God in a place where you cannot see him: behind you. The list in the previous chapter also ends with the same line, “you have forgotten me,” so the root of the two chapters is the same. When people put God behind them, the place in front is left empty, and of course something else fills it.
@@10235@@
This cup of bitterness was later drunk by One in person. In the garden the Lord Jesus prayed: “Nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire.” And he said to Peter: “The cup which the Father has given me, shall I not surely drink it?” (Matthew 26:39; John 18:11). The elder sister’s cup and the younger sister’s cup both came from turning away; Jesus’ cup was drunk to bring us home. We cannot keep ourselves from going astray all the way by our own strength; we come back to the Father’s house through a Lord who has already drunk the cup of bitterness.
@@10236@@
God hands people over into the hands of those they leaned on, and uses nations to judge. Does this mean God approves of violence?
@@10237@@
No. The text itself draws a line: whoever she leaned on, she was delivered into that one’s hand. This is cause and effect, the end of the road; it is not God encouraging or delighting in violence. Verse 24 says God committed the judgment to them, and they judged according to their own customs. God has the sovereignty to use nations, but that does not mean he approves of the nations’ cruelty. The ancient world also described war in very strong words, to warn, not to give a detailed account of what happened. If you yourself have experienced violence, please do not hear this chapter as “this is what you deserved.” God never stands on the side of the one who does harm; his heart is the pain of being forsaken.
@@10238@@
IV. Light from the Original Language and the Purpose of Judgment (23:4, 18, 31, 36–49)
@@10239@@
This chapter has three original words that can touch the heart within thirty seconds. The first is the names of the sisters, אָהֳלָה (Oholah) and אָהֳלִיבָה (Oholibah), verse 4. Many interpreters think both names are related to אֹהֶל (tent), hinting that God’s tent was among them from the beginning. Others say the meaning of the names is uncertain and should not be over-read. We keep both views in mind and do not make either one a condition of faith; what we can be sure of is what the text itself says: they became mine. Your name was called out; it was not thrown aside.
@@10240@@
The second is “alienated,” נָקְעָה (nakah), verses 17 and 18. The meaning of this word is to be wrenched apart, to be dislocated. If you have ever sprained a joint you know: it is not broken, but what was joined together has been forced out of place, and it hurts whenever it moves. God says “my soul was alienated from her,” and this is the word he uses. God’s heart and his people’s heart were originally joined together, and now they have been wrenched apart. This is not a word of anger; it is a word of pain.
@@10241@@
The third is “cup,” כּוֹס (kos), verses 31 to 33. This word also appears in Psalm 23:5, “My cup runs over.” It is the same word: it can be a cup of blessing, and it can be a cup of bitterness. The cup itself has not changed; what changes is which road a person has put it on. Which cup are you holding today?
@@10242@@
Finally, look at verses 36 to 49. Verses 37 to 39 say that there was blood on their hands, that they offered their children to idols, and that on the same day they also entered God’s sanctuary. This is the heaviest accusation, and we will not enlarge the picture or describe it word by word. Remember only one reminder: to mix God with other backers, to hold something for others in one hand and walk into God’s house with the other, is what grieves God most. If you have lost a child, please do not hear this passage as an accusation against you.
@@10243@@
Verse 45 says that “righteous men” will judge them as adulteresses and women who shed blood are judged. Who are these righteous men? Some say they are the foreign nations God uses to carry out judgment; some say they are those who act according to the standard of the law; and some say the meaning is unclear and should not be used as a guide for action. These notes set these views side by side and do not make any one of them a condition of faith. Remember only one thing: however it is read, this verse is not permission for anyone today to judge, to lay hands on, or to shame another person.
@@10244@@
The chapter ends with verse 49: “Then you will know that I am the Lord Yahweh.” This is the sentence most often repeated in Ezekiel. Judgment is not the goal; knowing him is the goal. God takes away the false backers one by one so that people will finally lift up their heads and see that he is the Lord. If a person comes to know him in the midst of loss, then even the loss is grace.
@@10245@@
Verse 45 says “righteous men” will judge. May I quote this verse to rebuke others?
@@10246@@
No. The “righteous men” of this verse are understood in several ways: some think they are the foreign nations who carry out judgment, some think they are people who act by the standard of the law, and some think the meaning is not clear enough to serve as a guide for action. Whichever it is, this is what God said to two cities; it is not a license for anyone today to judge, take revenge, or shame others. If you have ever been accused with a passage like this, please know that it was not God’s intention and it was not said to you. We read this verse to fear him more and to return to him more humbly, not to measure other people with it.
@@10247@@
The first deviation is to describe the pictures of chapter 23 word by word, to enlarge them, or even to use them to mock or judge any gender, marriage, or past. This is a parable God spoke to two cities, not a definition of any person. We want those who listen, especially those who have been hurt, to be protected, not to have their wounds touched.
@@10248@@
The second deviation is to quote this chapter to someone who has been harmed, implying “you are the one with the problem” or “you also carry some responsibility.” The text is not speaking to the victim, and God never stands on the side of the one who does harm. If a sermon makes people feel “it is my fault,” stop and add God’s heart.
@@10249@@
The third deviation is to use the “righteous men” judging in verse 45 of chapter 23, or the “stone them with stones” of verse 47, as a reason to lay hands on anyone, to take revenge, or to shame them. This chapter is not a license for violence. We want those who listen to fear God more and be more humble, not to feel that they may treat others this way.
@@10250@@
The fourth deviation is to speak only of judgment (the cup, the sword, the fire) and not of “they became mine,” or to use the cup of verse 31 to frighten people and make them live in panic. God’s heart is the pain of being forsaken, and the effect should be patience and loving the Lord more, not being more afraid of being thrown away.
@@10251@@
The fifth deviation is to apply the two sisters directly to some church or some group of people today, or to read this parable as a list of rules for emotional life. This chapter is about whom we lean on; it is not about who is right or wrong in matters of emotion, and it is not a behavior chart. We want those who listen to ask honestly: what is my backer? and not to point out others.
@@10252@@
I find that I am very afraid of this chapter, afraid of being thrown away by God. Is this normal?
@@10253@@
This fear is real, and we will not blame it. But please tell the difference: the point of this chapter is not that God may throw you away at any moment, but how much it hurts when God’s heart is forsaken, and that he never put down their names. Behind the judgment, Ezekiel 16:60–63 still says that God will establish that everlasting covenant. So fear may bring you back before God, but it should not push you away from him. You can say to God: I am afraid, but I am coming back. If this fear is already affecting your sleep and your life, please talk with a pastor or someone you trust, and you may also seek professional help.
@@10254@@
The first question: we are very used to “finding a backer.” In Chinese society, to get things done you look for connections, for your child’s schooling you look for a way in, when you are sick you look for a doctor you know, and so connections have become another name for security. This is not necessarily wrong, but the parable of chapter 23 asks us: when things really press down on you, who is the first person you call? You can check it this way: write down the first three people who came to mind when you ran into difficulty in the past month, and then ask yourself: was there a first one that I also spoke to God about?
@@10255@@
The second question: we are very used to “don’t let family shame go public,” and we swallow our pain in our hearts. A parable like chapter 23 can easily touch wounds that have never been spoken of. Some will think: just listen, and do not say it aloud. But if it is kept in too long, the wound does not heal by itself. You do not need to share details in the group, and you do not need to tell everyone; you can find one pastor or companion you trust and say just one sentence: this chapter, I found it very uncomfortable to hear. One sentence is enough. When necessary, please also feel free to go to a professional counselor. That is not a lack of trust in God; it is one of the helps God gives through people.
@@10256@@
In a Chinese church, how can I accompany a brother or sister who feels very uncomfortable hearing this chapter?
@@10257@@
First, do not press for details, and do not ask “what exactly is wrong with you?” Second, do not hurry to say “you need to forgive” or “you need to be strong,” because that will make him feel he is not good enough. Third, you can say just one sentence: I am here; you do not need to speak, and you do not need to leave. Fourth, after the meeting, check on him once privately, and ask him: right now, do you need quiet, or someone with you? Fifth, if he mentions self-harm, long-lasting exhaustion, or an experience of being harmed, please walk with him to find a pastor and professional help. To accompany is not to solve; it is to let him know that he has not been left behind, and that we did not leave him because he is uncomfortable.
@@10258@@
VII. Living Out 321: No More Other Backers
@@10259@@
Jesus is my Role Model. In the garden he handed himself over to the Father: not what I desire, but what you desire. That was the moment of most wanting to rely on himself, yet he handed everything over, and drank that cup himself. We who follow him learn his way: at the moment of greatest pressure, we do not first look for another backer; we first return to the Father’s presence.
@@10260@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 23 not to analyze every picture in the parable, but to let the sentence in verse 4 bring us back: they became mine. The standard the text gives us is not “have you done wrong,” but “where have you put your heart.” Let God’s word ask me first: whom do I most often lean on?
@@10261@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who lets us see the backers we are holding in our hands, and it is the Holy Spirit who gives us strength to let go. Take a quiet moment each day and ask: Lord, what am I most wanting to grip today?
@@10262@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is putting down the habit of finding my own backer and protecting myself; it does not mean having no connections, no savings, no abilities, but not letting them sit in the seat that only Jesus may sit in. Return to our position: let man be man, and let God be God.
@@10263@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we have been protected, let us not say it was my connections or my arrangements; all our security is given by the Lord who did not put down our names, and the glory is his.
@@10264@@
To build God’s system. A group that belongs to God is a group of people who “became his,” not a group comparing whose backer is bigger. In such a group, life first, then relationships, then ministry: first let him become what I lean on, and then there is secure relationship, and then there is a ministry that does not rely on people. A fellowship that puts its security in God’s hands is a small piece of God’s system on earth.
@@10265@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “no more other backers”?
@@10266@@
The relationship is very direct. There is room for only one on the king’s seat. The backers in our hearts, whether connections, savings, or reputation, take that seat the moment they become the first thing we think of under pressure. To let Jesus be King is not to throw all these things away, but to let them return to their proper place, as tools, not as backers. You can practice it this way: every day write down the one thing you are most worried about today, and beside it write: this, I first hand over to Jesus, and then I decide what I should do. After a while you will find that you still work and still make arrangements, only your heart is no longer bound by them.
@@10267@@
The proud type says: I can handle it myself, I do not need to rely on anyone, much less on God to arrange it. The text says to him: they became mine. Pride is leaving a person’s position and grasping God’s power, wanting to protect oneself. To return to our position is to admit: I belong to him. You do not need to say it all at once; first say one sentence to God: Lord, this matter, I will not carry by myself for now.
@@10268@@
The fearful type says: “If I do not hold on to something tightly, I am afraid no one will look after me.” Many of the backers in chapter 23 began with fear. God’s answer is: I have not put down your name. The fearful type needs to learn to replace “afraid there is no one” with “he is already here.” You can start with the smallest thing and hand over control once.
@@10269@@
The ashamed type says: “I saw it and still went; I have no right to come back.” The words “saw it” in verse 11 are not meant to shame you; they ask you to be honest. Shame says “you are not good enough”; the gospel says “you all became mine.” The ashamed type does not have to fix himself first; today, just let one backer loosen its hold.
@@10270@@
The wounded type says: “I have been hurt by people, so I only trust myself.” God does not blame you for being wounded, and this chapter is not an accusation against you. A wounded person can slowly find someone who truly loves you and say the one sentence you are willing to say; the one who accompanies should not hurry to say “you need to forgive,” but only say: “I am here, I am with you.”
@@10271@@
First, this week find a quiet time and write down the three backers you lean on most often (connections, savings, reputation, your own ability), and beside each one write: “This is not God.” When you have finished, put the paper where you can see it every day.
@@10272@@
Second, choose one habit you go to whenever pressure is high (scrolling the phone, shopping, complaining to people), and try this once this week: first stop for three minutes and say to the Lord: “I have come.” Write down which day and which moment it was.
@@10273@@
Third, tell a companion one backer you want to hand over to God, say the name and the time, and ask him to ask you once this week and also to pray for you.
@@10274@@
Fourth, find a lonely person and greet him once, only greet him, without teaching or judging anything about him, and say: “I thought of you.” Say the name and the date.
@@10275@@
Fifth, if this chapter touches your wound, this week find a pastor or someone you trust and say only one sentence: “This chapter, I found it very uncomfortable to hear.” You do not need to say any details.
@@10276@@
I declare: I belong to God; he has not put down my name.
@@10277@@
I declare: I will not use other backers to take the seat that only God may sit in.
@@10278@@
I declare: when I see it, I will come back; I do not need to fix myself first.
@@10279@@
I declare: I will not use this chapter to judge anyone; I will use his heart to accompany people.
@@10280@@
What is one thing I can do right away this week, so that I do not stop at only listening to the sermon?
@@10281@@
The simplest thing is to take a piece of paper today and write one sentence: What I most often use to protect myself lately is ______. No need to polish it, and no need to show it to anyone. Then say to God: Lord, this, I hand over to you. Next choose one small action you can do today: let go once of something you are used to gripping tightly. For example, tell a companion about something you have always wanted to solve by yourself; or, with an offering you have not dared to touch, pray first and then decide. At the next group meeting, just say one sentence: I did one thing this week, and I do not need to say what it was.
@@10282@@
Chapter 23 is a family photo hung on the wall. It begins with “born of one mother” and ends with “you will know that I am the Lord Yahweh.” In between there are two sisters, a cup both deep and large, and a heart that was forsaken yet did not put down their names. This chapter is heavy, and we did not enlarge the pictures, because God’s heart is not in the pictures; it is in that one sentence: they became mine.
@@10283@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see the pain of his forsaken heart, God wants us to say honestly what our backers are, and God also wants us to know that he has not put down our names. That God, in Jesus, drank that cup himself, so that we may no longer lean on anything else, and come back to his home.
@@10284@@
So please remember the three key points of today’s lesson: born of one mother, the elder sister’s cup, you will know that I am the Lord Yahweh. The focus of this lesson is not how many times you have seen that road, not how many times you have walked back onto it, and not how others see you, but Jesus, who has never put down your name.
@@10285@@
Lord, thank you that you have not put down our names. Forgive us for putting other things in your seat, and forgive us for seeing it and still going. Protect every one who hears this chapter and whose heart aches, so that we no longer go looking for other backers, and set our hearts in your hands. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, amen.

There is a kind of pain where you must still go out as usual and answer as usual, while inside everything has long since collapsed. In chapter 24, the prophet Ezekiel’s wife died in one evening, and he was commanded: do not weep, only sigh, but not aloud. This chapter is very heavy; we will not press for details, and we will not add one word on the text’s behalf. It sits between the cauldron of cooked meat and the bloody city, just before God is about to lose his own sanctuary. The answer of this chapter is not “you must be this strong,” but this: God knows the desire of your eyes, and he hurts too; silence is not leaving, and one day the mouth will be opened again.

In the end, the prophet’s mouth will be opened again.

<p><strong>① What does “this same day” mean?</strong></p><ul><li>An idiom: “on this very day,” stressing that it is exactly this day</li><li>Literally “the bone of this day,” foretelling the “bones” image below</li></ul><p>The same word עֶצֶם in verses 4, 5, and 10 is the bones in the cauldron.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: the idiom first, while also hearing the echo.</strong></p></blockquote><p>What the prophet is asked to write down is an exact day; and the “bone” of this day is about to be put into the cauldron.</p>
@@10352@@
<p><strong>Far away, he wrote down a day.</strong></p><p>Ezekiel was in the land of exile, far from Jerusalem. He could not see the city, and no one gave him news. But God said: “write down the name of the day,” because on this very day the king of Babylon drew close to Jerusalem.</p><p>This is the first quiet but weighty fact: God’s word arrives before the news. Later the one who escapes will come with the report (24:26), and only then will the people know whether the day the prophet wrote down was right. That day became the proof that the words in the prophet’s mouth came from God.</p><p>Please also hear the echo: the word used for “this same day” is the word for “bone.” Before the cauldron is even seen, the bones are already in the text.</p><p>Bringing it home: in the places you cannot see, God is not absent. He knows that day and remembers that day. Some things we cannot understand at the time, and we can only “write them down” first, and wait for God’s time to give them meaning.</p>

@@10355@@
<p><strong>This parable was originally someone else’s saying.</strong></p><p>In chapter 11 some said: this city is the cauldron, and we are the meat; they meant, we are inside the walls, we are safe. God now takes that saying and says it again.</p><p>The words are the same: the city is the cauldron, the people are the meat. But the ending is different. The meat is not protected; it is cooked. The cauldron is the wall, true, but the wall becomes the vessel that cooks them.</p><p>Notice the details in verses 4 and 5: “the best,” “the choice,” “the fat.” Those chosen for the cauldron are not rotten meat; they are first-rate goods. <p><strong>The blood that people thought no one knew about is set on a bare rock.</strong></p><p>This passage speaks first of the cauldron and then of the blood. The cauldron is “the cauldron whose rust is in it”; the rust grows on the cauldron’s body: it is not an impurity that happened to fall in, but the condition of the vessel itself.</p><p>Then comes the blood. The law said that shed blood is to be covered with dust (the principle of Lev 17:13). But here the blood is set “on the bare rock.” Smooth, with no crack, no dust, and no one can cover it. God says: this is from me; I will let it not be covered.</p><p>It means: some blood, people think, once buried is gone. God says, no. The first blood in Genesis cried out from the ground (Gen 4:10).</p><p>For the one who did harm these are heavy words, but to the one who was hurt they are a comfort: your pain has not been quietly covered over; God saw it.</p><p>Bringing it home: what are the things we work so hard to cover with dust? God sets the blood on the rock not to shame people, but because he does not allow the wounded to be buried in silence.</p>

<p><strong>First cook the meat, then burn the cauldron.</strong></p><p>The second round of fire is not like the first. The first round cooked the meat, cooked until it fell apart and was scorched. In the second round, the meat is poured out, and the empty cauldron sits on the coals, burned until the bronze glows. The purpose is not food; it is the cauldron itself: to melt the filthiness and remove the rust.</p><p>This is the picture in the judgment with the most “shape of hope”: the purpose of the fire is to cleanse. But verse 12 goes on to say: the rust is too deep, and even fire cannot remove it.</p><p>The cauldron is very tired. “Weary with toil”: it has used all its strength, and the rust is still there. This does not mean God’s fire is not hot enough; it means the rust and the bronze have grown into one.</p><p>Bringing it home: some habits and some roots are no longer something that can be handled by “changing a little.” God’s fire is not lacking in strength; some things need a brand-new vessel, not a fiercer fire. The answer to this comes only in chapter 36.</p>

<p><strong>“I have cleansed you and you weren’t cleansed.”</strong></p><p>This sentence is the heart of the whole judgment. It is not that God never tried another way. He cleansed, again and again, and the people did not become clean.</p><p>Then comes “until I have caused my wrath toward you to rest.” This sentence has an end point. Judgment is not endless ill will; it will “rest.” God’s wrath is directed at one matter; it is not forever.</p><p>The three negatives of verse 14 are heavy: I won’t go back, I won’t spare, I won’t repent. It is not that God has no feelings; it is that God does not take back his word. Once the word is spoken, it is done. Only a God like this has the right to make the promises that come later, because he keeps his word.</p><p>Please also remember the stance of the whole book (the issue “Personal Responsibility: Does ‘The Soul Who Sins Will Die’ Deny the Consequences for the Group?”): God has no pleasure in the death of anyone; he is pleased when people turn from their way and live (18:23, 32; 33:11). This verse is speaking of a community that kept refusing cleansing, not of a person who grieves over sin. For the person who grieves over sin, there is another word waiting later (36:25).</p><p>Bringing it home: “I have cleansed you and you weren’t cleansed.” Today, which cleansing are you still refusing?</p>

<p><strong>These verses are very heavy. If you are in the middle of a loss, please stop first.</strong></p><p>This instruction was given to Ezekiel alone; it is not a rule for anyone. If you are in the pain of bereavement, weeping is allowed; you do not need to be silent as he was, and you do not need to look strong in front of others.</p><p>Now look at the text. God says: “I will take away from you the desire of your eyes.” God uses the language of love: “the desire of your eyes.” He does not say “one of your tools”; he acknowledges that she is the most precious person to his heart. Only in verse 18 do we learn that this is his wife.</p><p>God also does not explain why she must die, does not say it was her fault, and does not say it was a punishment on her. The text says only: this will come, and his grief will have a special form.</p><p>The form is this: no public weeping, the headdress stays on, the sandals stay on, no mourner’s bread. The reason is told later: when the temple is destroyed, those in exile will not be able to hold funerals either.</p><p>Bringing it home: God did not tell him not to hurt; he only told him not to make a sound. The word “sigh” was still left to him. Even in the quietest place, there is a crack that opens toward God.</p>

<p><strong>① What is “what these things are to us” asking?</strong></p><ul><li>What does this action mean for us</li><li>Why are you doing this</li></ul><p>The original is “what are these things to us,” and the emphasis is on “to us”: the first thing they ask is how this relates to themselves.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: they are asking about meaning, and also about their own place.</strong></p></blockquote><p>This question is very natural and very honest, and it makes the explanation that follows possible.</p>
@@10370@@
<p><strong>In one verse, there is his whole night.</strong></p><p>“I spoke to the people in the morning; and at evening my wife died.” The text says only this one sentence. No name of an illness, no process, no word of what he said. One verb, “died,” and it is over.</p><p>Then: “So I did in the morning as I was commanded.” This is the heaviest line in the whole book. He obeyed, and the text does not say how he got through it.</p><p>This silence is not coldness. It is so that we do not look at this night as a story. And we do not need to fill in details for him.</p><p>Then the people ask: “Won’t you tell us what these things are to us, that you do so?” They see a man who has lost his wife and does not weep, and it seems strange to them. People are always like this: the first thing they ask is “what does this have to do with me?”</p><p>Bringing it home: when we see someone else’s pain, what is our first thought? Is it “what does this mean for me,” or first to be quietly with them? These two questions do not necessarily exclude each other, but there is an order.</p>

<p><strong>① Are the wife and the sanctuary a deliberate correspondence?</strong></p><ul><li>Yes: the same word מַחְמַד (a precious thing) is repeated in verses 16, 21, and 25; what the prophet loses and what the people are about to lose use the same word</li><li>Only a coincidence of wording</li></ul><p>All three places are “the desire of your eyes,” with verse 21 adding “the pride of your power” and “that which your soul pities.”</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: it is a deliberate correspondence.</strong></p></blockquote><p>What the prophet went through is a message put into his body in advance: you lose what you love most, and you cannot weep as usual.</p>
@@10373@@
<p><strong>What they boast of most is also what they love most, and God says: I will let it be profaned.</strong></p><p>The prophet was asked, and he told them God’s explanation exactly as it was. “I will profane my sanctuary, the pride of your power, the desire of your eyes, and that which your soul pities.” Three expressions, each closer to the heart than the last. The temple was their pride, their treasure, and the thing their hearts cherished.</p><p>Please notice the subject of this sentence: the one who profanes the sanctuary is God. It is not Babylon, and it is not the people. God can let go, because his name does not depend on a building. This is on the same line as the glory leaving in chapter 10.</p><p>So the prophet’s wife became a mirror of the people’s future: “according to all that he has done, you will do.” When that day comes, they too will not cover their lips and will not hold funerals: not because they do not grieve, but because the grief is so deep that there is nothing they can do.</p><p>Here we should not go off course: this is not asking the people to learn the prophet’s endurance. It is a sign: God first lets his servant walk ahead.</p><p>Bringing it home: what is the “pride of your power”? Once anything is put in God’s place, it becomes the first thing to be taken back. Let man be man, and let God be God.</p>
