<p><strong>The net is “my net.” The great beast thought it was in the water, but it had been under God’s net all along.</strong></p><p>The pictures in this passage are heavy: dragged ashore, thrown on the open field, made food for birds and beasts. We do not need to look closely at every picture. The point is that the deep water a person thinks is safe is not a safe place.</p><p>The original word for “spread” (<em>paras</em>) is the movement of a fisherman opening his net: slow, steady, effortless. God does not need to chase him. He only needs to spread the net.</p><p>What is most worth noticing is “my net.” The nations are the mesh, and Babylon is the one carrying it out, but the owner of the net is Yahweh. Pharaoh’s greatest mistake was thinking that history has no owner.</p><p>These four verses are a string of actions: spread, pull, throw, cast, lay, fill, water. The subject of every verb is “I.” Pharaoh’s power could not have been greater, yet in this passage he becomes a passive object.</p><p>If you are being pressed down by a power stronger than you, this passage reminds you that that power also lies in someone else’s net. He is the Lord, and the net is in his hand.</p>

<p>The “extinguish” of verse 7 (the form of <em>kabah</em>) and the “make dark” of verse 8 (<em>qadar</em>) belong to one set of images: light put out, light covered. <p><strong>When a person falls, even the heavens put on mourning for him.</strong></p><p>Verses 7 and 8 pull the camera up to the sky: sun, moon and stars all go dark. This is common language in the Old Testament, used to say that the fall of a great nation is an event that shakes the whole universe.</p><p>We should not read it as an astronomical forecast. It says that Pharaoh’s world once thought it had a light that would never go out. God says: I can take it away.</p><p>As we read this passage, we need to notice one detail: the light is put out by God, and the darkness is something God “sets.” The darkness of judgment is not out of control; it is in his hand.</p><p>The prophets often use darkness in the sky to speak of the end of an old order. The Egyptians worshiped the sun, and the sun god was one of their greatest gods; to say that the sun is covered by a cloud is to say that the gods they relied on have lost their light. Light is in God’s hand, and so is its coming and going.</p><p>Landing it: if you are in a very dark moment, first sort out whether this darkness is judgment or testing. 1. A Song to Be Sung in Tears (32:1–8)
@@14306@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Has your phone ever popped up a notification like this: something has happened to a very famous person? What was your first reaction? For some, the heart sinks; for some, they scroll down to read the comments; some cannot help saying, “I knew it would end like this.” We rarely admit it, but that split second of the heart is really who we are. In today’s passage, God tells the prophet to sing a song, and to sing it for the fall of a strong man. He wants the prophet to sing it in tears, not with a smile.
@@14307@@
First, the time and the task. Verse 1 says that in the twelfth year, in the twelfth month, on the first day of the month, Yahweh’s word came to the prophet. Verse 2: “Son of man, take up a lamentation over Pharaoh king of Egypt.” Please notice that God did not tell the prophet to write a denunciation, and did not tell him to stand on the city wall and shout slogans, but to take up a lamentation. A lament is a song sung at a funeral. This verse sets the tone for the whole chapter: words of judgment are to be spoken in the sound of tears.
@@14308@@
The words of the song are very vivid: “You were likened to a young lion of the nations; yet you are as a monster in the seas. You broke out with your rivers, and troubled the waters with your feet, and fouled their rivers.” The picture Pharaoh had of himself was a young lion, majestic and in command. God’s picture was a great fish thrashing about in the water, bursting out into the rivers and trampling the water until it was muddy. Imagine a child splashing wildly in a pond: he feels very powerful, but all he has done is stir the whole pond until no one can see. This is sometimes how it is wherever a strong person goes: he feels he is moving forward, and others only see the water turn muddy.
@@14309@@
In verses 3 to 6, God says he will spread his net over this great fish with a company of many peoples, and bring it up. The pictures are intense: thrown on the ground, birds and beasts, blood flowing over the mountaintops. We do not need to imagine every detail word by word, and we should not blow it up into a horror film. What the text wants us to hear is: even one who thought he was a lion is in God’s net. Verses 7 and 8 say that the heavens will be covered, the stars made dark, the sun covered with a cloud, and the moon will not give its light. This is the language of poetry: the whole heaven and earth dim for a fallen king, and we should not treat it as a table of astronomical events that can be calculated. When God says these things, he says them in the tone of a lament.
@@14310@@
God is a righteous judge. Why would he still tell the prophet to “take up a lamentation” for Pharaoh? Shouldn’t he simply make the announcement?
@@14311@@
Because this chapter wants us to see God’s heart. The judgment is real, and the pictures of verses 2 to 8 are not light, but God’s command is to “take up a lamentation,” not to “sing a victory song.” A lament is sung for one who has passed away, which shows that God takes no pleasure in people’s ruin, and he grieves over it. Elsewhere Ezekiel also says that he has no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live (33:11). So when we face the fall of an enemy, or the failure of someone we do not like, our first posture should be like God’s: bow the head first. This is not defending sin, nor denying justice, but learning to see things with God’s heart. You can practice it like this: the next time you hear that something has happened to someone, pause for three seconds in your heart and say: “Lord, have mercy on him.” Then decide whether to say anything.

2. The Sword, the Rumor and the Clearing Water (32:9–16)
@@14313@@
Verses 9 and 10 say that the news of Pharaoh’s ruin will reach nations he did not know: “I will also trouble the hearts of many peoples,” and kings will be “horribly afraid for you.” The fall of a strong man is never only his own affair. Verse 11: “The sword of the king of Babylon will come on you.” God writes Babylon’s sword as a tool in his own hand. Verse 12 says these warriors are the ruthless of the nations, and they will bring the pride of Egypt to nothing. Please notice that the text does not praise Babylon; it calls them “ruthless.” God uses an unrighteous power, but does not approve it. This is the same as what we saw in chapter 31: God rules history, and the tools still have to answer to him.
@@14314@@
Verses 13 and 14 have a very quiet picture: God will destroy the animals from beside the many waters, “the foot of man won’t trouble them any more, nor will the hoofs of animals trouble them,” and he will make Egypt’s waters “clear, and cause their rivers to run like oil.” Did you notice that this faces verse 2 from far across the chapter? Verse 2 says that the great fish troubled the waters with its feet and fouled the rivers; verse 14 says that the water is still, clear, flowing slowly like oil. Only after the strong one has left does the water become quiet for the first time. That is a deep line: sometimes we think we are pushing things forward, but we are really stirring up the water. True calm appears only after we put down the hand that stirs.
@@14315@@
Verse 15 is the center of the whole passage: God will make the land of Egypt desolate and waste, “then they will know that I am Yahweh.” This line appears many times in Ezekiel; it is the main melody of the whole book. The purpose of judgment is not destruction but knowing: that people may know that the real Lord is Yahweh. Verse 16 returns to the lament: people will lament with this song, and the daughters of the nations will mourn for Egypt and her multitude. This song belongs not only to the prophet, but to everyone who sees. God hopes that when people see a great nation fall, they will not clap but mourn, and come to know him.
@@14316@@
There is one more reminder here. Verse 9 says the news of the fall will reach “the countries which you have not known.” Each of us has an influence that reaches farther than we think. A harsh word at home will travel to your child’s circle of friends; a look on your face in the small group will be carried back to someone’s dinner table. The water a strong person stirs never stays only under his own feet. This is not to frighten us, but to make us value our own weight: if stirring the water can travel so far, so can clearing it.
@@14317@@
The text says “they will know that I am Yahweh.” Is the purpose of judgment to make people afraid of God?
@@14318@@
Not to make people afraid, but to make them know. Ezekiel says many times “they will know that I am Yahweh,” both in judgment and in restoration. Knowing him is not a kind of fear but a kind of acknowledgment: I am not the Lord; he is. Pharaoh thought he was the master of the river (chapter 29 records him saying the river was his own), so God let him see that even the river was not his. This knowledge is the same for us today: we are often stirring our world and thinking we control everything; on the day we put down our hands, we see that the water had been in God’s hand all along. You can ask yourself: in what area of my life am I stirring too hard? Give that area to God in prayer.

3. Light from the Original Language: Lament, Great Fish, Terror (32:2, 25)
@@14320@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth a close look. The first is “lament,” the Hebrew קִינָה (qinah). Verse 2 says: “take up a lamentation over Pharaoh king of Egypt.” This is a specific kind of funeral poem, with a very particular rhythm, one long and one short, like the breath of someone weeping, drawn in and let out. The most famous קִינָה in the Bible is the one David sang for Saul and Jonathan: “How the mighty have fallen!” (2 Samuel 1). David sang such a song for the king who had hunted him. So when God tells the prophet to sing a קִינָה for Pharaoh, he is asking the prophet to walk into a very high standard: to weep even for a strong man who has fallen.
@@14321@@
The second is “great fish,” the Hebrew תַּנִּים (tannim), which verse 2 renders “a monster in the seas” (WEB; the CUV has “great fish”). This word can refer to a great serpent, a great fish, a crocodile, or some such monster of the water, and in the Egyptian setting it easily brings to mind the great beasts of the Nile. The point is not which animal it is, but the picture: it charges about in the water, yet is pulled up by the net. The lion is how Pharaoh saw himself; the great fish is how God saw him. The distance between the two pictures is the distance between pride and truth. Ask yourself: how far apart are the picture in my heart and the way God sees me?
@@14322@@
The third is “terror,” the Hebrew חִתִּית (khittit). Verse 25 says: “their terror was caused in the land of the living.” This word recurs in this passage, in verses 23, 24, 26 and 30. It means a fear that makes people tremble, the reputation a strong man builds by frightening people. But the text gives a very simple contrast: they once caused terror in “the land of the living,” and now they lie beneath the earth. The power built on frightening people reaches only as far as the land of the living. If you find that you are leading people by frightening them, by your volume, by the look on your face, this word was prepared for you.
@@14323@@
Put the three words together and you have the journey of this chapter: God tells us to sing a lament (qinah) for the strong man, because in God’s eyes he is only a great fish (tannim) stirring up the water, and everything he built by causing terror (khittit) stops at the border of the land of the living.
@@14324@@
I am not good at singing, and I do not cry easily. What does “taking up a lament for someone” look like in today’s life?
@@14325@@
Today a lament is not necessarily a song; it can be a posture. First, when you hear that someone has failed, bow your head first and do not comment first. Second, pray for someone who has made a big mistake: not to make excuses for his fault, but to ask God to have mercy on him. Third, when it is needed, sit quietly with him for a while, without rushing to teach him. David sang a lament for Saul not because Saul was without fault, but because a person’s fall is itself worth grieving. If you were deeply hurt by this person and do not yet have the strength to grieve for him, that is all right too. God does not require you to do it at once. You can look after your own heart first, and when you have the strength, say one prayer for him.

Every fallen nation has its own place there.
@@14328@@
Verses 22 to 30 are like a roll call, naming them one by one: Asshur, Elam, Meshech and Tubal, Edom, the princes of the north and the Sidonians. Each nation’s graves are “in the uttermost parts of the pit,” with the multitude around them, all of them slain by the sword. One line comes like a repeated drumbeat: “who caused terror in the land of the living.” Verse 25 even says that they “set her a bed among the slain with all her multitude.” It is a cold and very level picture: those who once defined themselves by the sword all lie in the same row of beds, their heads on their swords, and never move again.
@@14329@@
“Uncircumcised” is the word that recurs in this passage, and it is often misused. Its meaning here is read differently by different interpreters: some think it refers to nations without the covenant sign, that is, people who do not belong to Israel’s covenant people; some think it stresses a shameful death and a burial without honor; others think it is mainly a repeated poetic phrase that strengthens the color of “shame.” The position of these notes: first read it as a poetic lament over the ancient nations, and do not apply it directly to any group of people today, nor use it to judge anyone’s destination after death. This passage does not ask us to point at others and say “you are the uncircumcised.” It asks us: what do I rely on to define my strength? In the end, can that thing go down with me?
@@14330@@
Verse 31 is a very bitter line: “Pharaoh will see them, and will be comforted over all his multitude.” Pharaoh sees that so many nations are here, and receives a little comfort. This is irony: having many companions cannot truly comfort a person. Verse 32 is the conclusion of the whole chapter: “For I have put his terror in the land of the living. He will be laid among the uncircumcised, with those who are slain by the sword, even Pharaoh and all his multitude.” Please notice that this is God’s word, and also the end of a lament. We do not need to use it to calculate who is where. We only need to remember: those who live by terror end with only silence.
@@14331@@
My loved one has passed away. Could the “Sheol,” “the slain” and “going down to the pit” in this passage be about my loved one?
@@14332@@
Please be at ease: no. This passage is a poetic lament whose subjects are the ancient nations and their kings, used to show one thing: those who made people afraid by power end up lying in the same silence. It is not a map of the afterlife, and it cannot be used to work out where any deceased person has gone. To someone who is grieving, I want to say first: God has seen your sorrow, you do not need to blame yourself because of these verses, and you do not need to let anyone use these words to comment on your loved one. If you have just lost someone, just let yourself cry and let people be with you; there is no need to rush to find answers in this chapter. Elsewhere the Bible teaches clearly about death and hope, and you can read it slowly at another time, accompanied by a pastor or a trusted companion. When you need to, please seek professional counseling.
@@14333@@
The first deviation is to preach “a lament for Pharaoh” as cheering and cursing against some enemy. God told the prophet to sing in tears, not with a smile. We want the congregation to become more compassionate and to pray more for others, not to take more pleasure in other people’s falls.
@@14334@@
The second deviation is to apply “the uncircumcised” directly to people today who do not believe, and to judge their destination after death. This is a poetic lament over the ancient nations, and the church also understands this word in different ways. We should first set the different readings side by side, name no one, and make the congregation more willing to share the gospel with unbelievers, rather than stick labels on them.
@@14335@@
The third deviation is to preach “Sheol” and “the pit” as a complete map of the afterlife, or to use them to guess where a deceased person has gone. These are poetic images. We should say to the bereaved: this chapter is not about your loved one, so that they are comforted rather than frightened.
@@14336@@
The fourth deviation is to preach “the heavens covered, the stars made dark” (verses 7 and 8) as an astronomical prophecy that can be calculated, or to draw up a timetable of the end times. This is poetry describing heaven and earth dimming for a fallen king. We should lead the congregation to a quieter awe of God, not to a more anxious counting of days.
@@14337@@
The fifth deviation is to preach Babylon’s sword as “God approves of violence,” or to use “Pharaoh is comforted” in verse 31 to teach people to comfort themselves with other people’s failure. The text calls Babylon “ruthless,” and verse 31 is bitter irony. We should lead the congregation to pray more for others when they fall, not to breathe a sigh of relief.
@@14338@@
Some say chapter 32 is about “God hating proud strong men,” so I can safely hate those who oppress me. Is this a right reading?
@@14339@@
This reading reads only half. The text does say that God will bring the pride of the ruthless to nothing, and this gives the oppressed a real comfort: God sees, and God will not be silent forever. But in the same chapter, God also tells the prophet to take up a lament for them and to wail for them. So what we can be at ease about is God’s justice, but we cannot take hatred as a virtue because of it. You can honestly tell God your anger and your pain; this is permitted. You can also place that person in his hands and ask him to deal with it, without taking matters into your own hands. If the resentment has been impossible to put down for a long time, or affects your sleep and your life, please do not carry it alone; talk to a pastor or a professional. Letting go is not excusing his wrong; it is not letting his wrong go on living inside you.
@@14340@@
The first question: we are very used to “watching the show.” When something happens to another family, we chat about it a little longer at the dinner table; when someone stumbles, we forward it in the group chat; whether it is a celebrity, an official or a co-worker, whenever someone falls there is someone nearby who says softly, “I heard about it long ago.” The lament of chapter 32 hits exactly this: God tells the prophet to weep for the fallen strong man. You can begin like this: this week, when you want to forward something or comment on another person’s failure, stop first, say a prayer in your heart for that person, and then decide whether to speak.
@@14341@@
The second question: we are very used to “relying on presence.” An elder’s glance, a boss’s expression, a leader’s cough can make a whole room go quiet. Chinese culture respects authority, which is a virtue, but sometimes it slides easily into “terror”: the way we lead people is not love but making them afraid. Chapter 32 says again and again that those who “caused terror in the land of the living” all end up in the same pit. You can examine yourself like this: this month, was there a time when someone did not dare tell me the truth because they were afraid of me? If so, find him and talk once, and say: “I want to hear what you really think.”
@@14342@@
If I am the one who has long been stirred up and pressed down, will this chapter make me more bitter? How should I read it?
@@14343@@
This chapter has two comforts for the oppressed. First, God sees how the strong one stirs the water, and he also sees the one under the water. Verse 14 says that one day the water will clear and flow slowly like oil; this is a promise to everyone who has been stirred up. Second, God does not ask you to sing a victory song for the strong one, nor to forgive at once; he only lets you know that judgment is not in your hands. You can honestly speak your pain, and you do not need to force a smile with “it is God’s will.” If one day you have the strength to say a prayer for that person, that is grace, not an obligation. If you are in long-term bullying or control, or even danger to your personal safety, please seek a pastor, someone you trust or a professional organization as soon as you can; protecting yourself comes first.
@@14344@@
7. Living Out 321: Let the Water Clear
@@14345@@
Jesus is my Role Model. When he came near Jerusalem, he saw the city and wept over it (Luke 19:41). That city would in the end reject him, yet he shed tears for it. Those who follow him learn his way: they weep for the fallen, and do not sing victory songs over them.
@@14346@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 32 not to find a Pharaoh we can point at and say “that’s him,” but to let God’s word ask us: wherever I go, is the water clear or muddy? The text says Pharaoh troubled the waters and fouled the rivers; God says that one day the rivers will run like oil. This is God’s standard, and also my homework.
@@14347@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who makes our hearts soften first, rather than comment first, when others stumble. Each day, take a quiet moment and ask: Lord, is there someone today for whom I need to sing a lament, rather than gossip?
@@14348@@
Let Jesus be King. The pride in this chapter is stirring the rivers with one’s own strength and wanting to rule oneself. To let Jesus be King is to put down the hand that says “I must be in control”: what we hand over is not influence, but the place in the heart where “everyone must fear me” sits. When he is King, my volume, my position and the people I lead all return to his hand.
@@14349@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. A name built on terror reaches only to the land of the living; a name built on love and service is the one God remembers. If we have a little influence, let us not say “it is my prestige”; everything is his grace, and the glory goes to him.
@@14350@@
To build God’s system. God’s system is a community that weeps for the fallen and does not lead by terror. In such a community, the one who fails is accompanied, not forwarded; leaders lead the way servants do, not with their facial expressions. Life first, then relationships, then ministry. Such a fellowship is a small piece of God’s system on earth: a place where the river runs clear.
@@14351@@
What is the connection between “Let Jesus be King” and “no longer muddying the water”?
@@14352@@
The connection is very direct. Muddying the water is what we look like when we want to “be in control”: getting involved in everything, having the last word on everything, so that others cannot see clearly or stand steadily. To let Jesus be King is to admit that this water was never mine. You can begin with something small: this week, pick a setting where you are used to stepping in, such as a family decision, a small group’s arrangements or a team discussion, and try saying one sentence less and letting others speak first. Then watch whether the water becomes a little clearer. This is not passivity but selflessness: not having nothing, but loosening the hand and entering his sovereignty. In time you will find that things go on without being stirred, and go on more quietly.
@@14353@@
The proud type says: “I am a lion, and wherever I go, everyone must listen to me.” The text says to him: “Now you are as a monster in the seas.” The proud leave their position and grasp at power, wanting to take God’s place; to return to our position is to admit: I am a man, not the master of the river. You do not need to say everything at once; first say one sentence to God: “I am willing to put down the hand that stirs.”
@@14354@@
The fearful type says: “I am afraid of the strong, so I do not dare say anything.” God sees those who are terrified by the strong, and says in the text: the strong man’s might reaches only to the land of the living. Your fear is real, and God does not despise it. The fearful type should learn, step by step, to say one true sentence, and to find a trusted person to stand beside you.
@@14355@@
The ashamed type says: “I too have hurt people. Will I also lie in that pit?” Shame says “you have no hope left”; the gospel says “the one who turns will live” (33:11). This chapter is a lament, not a verdict; it is meant to make people turn back. The ashamed type does not need to prove innocence first; today, just bring your wrongs before God, and before the people you have hurt.
@@14356@@
The wounded type says: “I have been stirred up and pressed down for many years, and when I hear that someone else has fallen, I actually feel glad.” God does not blame you for being wounded; he sees the one under the water. This gladness may be a voice suppressed for too long. The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you and tell your story; those who accompany should not rush to say “you must forgive,” but just say: “I am here, I am with you.”
@@14357@@
First, this week, when you hear that someone has fallen, pause for three seconds in your heart and say: “Lord, have mercy on him.” Write down which day and which matter.
@@14358@@
Second, pick a setting where you are used to “stirring things up,” at home, in your small group or at work, and this week say one sentence less and let others speak first. Write down which day.
@@14359@@
Third, write down one thing where you made someone afraid of you by your presence or your expression, find a suitable person and say: “I want to hear what you really think.” Say the name and the time.
@@14360@@
Fourth, pray once for a person for whom it is very hard to weep: do not ask that he change at once, only that God have mercy on him. Say the name and the date.
@@14361@@
Fifth, find someone who is failing or being talked about, and send one line: “I am praying for you, and I stand with you.” Do not forward it, do not gossip, and say the name.
@@14362@@
I declare: I will not breathe a sigh of relief at another’s fall; I will sing a lament for him.
@@14363@@
I declare: I will not lead by terror; I will put down the hand that stirs, and let the water clear.
@@14364@@
I declare: what is built on presence reaches only to the land of the living; I build only on Jesus.
@@14365@@
I declare: let man be man, and let God be God; Jesus is King, and I am his servant.
@@14366@@
I really want to do this week’s tasks, but when I think of the person who hurt me deeply, I cannot pray for him. Does that mean I am not spiritual enough?
@@14367@@
No. Praying for the one who hurt you is a very high step, and not everyone can do it at every time. You can start from something smaller: the first step is not to pray for him, but to tell God honestly about your pain, without polishing it. The second step is to say just one sentence: “Lord, I give this person to you.” The third step, if one day you have the strength, add one more line: “Have mercy on him.” These three steps have no timetable and need no one to check on you. If the harm involved violence, sexual harm or long-term control, please do not force yourself to go near him, and do not forgive too quickly for the sake of being “spiritual”; first seek help from a pastor or a professional. Protecting yourself is more important.
