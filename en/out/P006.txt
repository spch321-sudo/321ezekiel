I. A Sword and a Pair of Balances (5:1–4)
@@2244@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Is there a small kitchen scale in your kitchen? We have one at my house. Every year when we make dumplings, my mother divides a big bowl of filling into three parts: one part is cooked and eaten right away, one part goes to the neighbors, and one part is frozen. She does not just grab by hand. She stands in front of the scale and weighs it part by part, and she stops only when no one is shortchanged. When I was little I thought this was a lot of fuss. When I grew up I understood that she weighed it so carefully because that filling was something she had chopped up bit by bit since early morning. She could not bear to divide it carelessly. This chapter today also has a pair of balances, and also three parts. But what is on the scale is not dumpling filling; it is a prophet’s own hair and beard.
@@2245@@
Let us set the scene first. The prophet is in the land of exile, and the people are watching him. Verse 1 says: “You, son of man, take a sharp sword. You shall take it as a barber’s razor to yourself, and shall cause it to pass over your head and over your beard. Then take balances to weigh and divide the hair.” Please notice that the text does not say this is a weapon. It is a razor, a tool that barbers use every day. God did not tell him to look for something special; he told him to use an ordinary blade. When God speaks, he often uses what is at hand. The scissors in your home, a spatula, a phone: in his hand, any of them can become a sentence.
@@2246@@
There is also a detail that is easy to read past. Ezekiel was the son of a priest, and a priest’s head is not shaved casually. Now God tells him to shave himself in front of everyone. This is not a comfortable assignment. The prophet is not standing on a high place pointing at others; he puts himself into the picture, stroke by stroke. For God’s word to be passed on accurately, the one who passes it on pays a price in his body first. This is the pattern of a prophet, and it reminds everyone who speaks God’s word: do not move only your mouth; let this word touch you first.
@@2247@@
Next are the balances. The hair and beard are to be divided with balances, not cut wildly. In this detail, God wants the people to see that judgment has weight, and also measure. He has weighed it. Verse 2 makes the three parts clear: a third part burned in the fire in the middle of the city, a third part struck with the sword around the city, a third part scattered to the wind; and then God adds a line himself: “I will draw out a sword after them.” This sentence is important. Do you think the part blown far away by the wind has escaped? No. Wherever the wind blows, his eye follows.
@@2248@@
One third, one third, one third: are we meant to calculate who will end up how, and in which year?
@@2249@@
No. Through the ages, some have read the three parts of 5:2 as three kinds of historical outcome, and some have read them as a symbol of the completeness of judgment; both readings have scholars supporting them. These notes do not do arithmetic with any proportion, and they do not make either reading a condition of faith. You need only see one thing: God has weighed it, and even the part scattered by the wind is under his eye. If you find yourself beginning to count and compare as you read this passage, please stop, and ask instead: Lord, what does this passage want me to fear more? If the result is panic, you have read it off course; if the result is that you love the Lord more and are more willing to turn back, that is what this passage wants.
@@2250@@
Then come verses 3 and 4, where most people breathe out in relief and then feel their hearts sink. Verse 3 says: “You shall take of it a few in number, and bind them in the folds of your robe.” A few. The folds of the robe are a place close to the body, like keeping a very important photograph against your chest. Reading this, we all think: surely someone will be left? God has a heart; he did not shave people away completely. But then verse 4 comes: “Of these again you shall take, and cast them into the middle of the fire, and burn them in the fire. From it a fire will come out into all the house of Israel.”
@@2251@@
Why must even what was kept in the folds of the robe have some taken again and thrown into the fire? It is not that God is fickle; he is tearing down the last chance for self-excuse: I am among those who were left, so nothing will happen to me. This is the sentence many people hide deepest in their hearts. I belong to God, I pray, I serve, so I should be fine. But this chapter asks gently: Is your heart really with him? Those who are left must also pass through the fire. This sentence is not meant to frighten people; it is meant to wake them up.
@@2252@@
II. This Is Jerusalem (5:5–10)
@@2253@@
Verse 5 is the turning point of the whole chapter: “The Lord Yahweh says: ‘This is Jerusalem.’” God seems to point with his hand at those three heaps of hair and say, this is that city. Please notice the look back that follows: “I have set her in the middle of the nations, and countries are around her.” She did not happen to land there; he put her there. Put her in the middle of the nations, like hanging a mirror in the center of the marketplace. If people want to know how the people of God live, they need only look up. This is a very great trust, and also a very heavy responsibility.
@@2254@@
The church is placed in the world in the same way. The neighbors watch us: how we speak, how we earn money, how we treat the weak, how we treat that neighbor who is very hard to get along with. One brother once told me that his neighbor never came to church, but in the year he lost his job, that neighbor quietly left a bag of rice at his door. Later he found out that the neighbor had seen him greet people every morning as usual, and thought: this man is a little different from others. We are placed in the middle of the nations not so that we look impressive, but so that people can see there is a God who is real.
@@2255@@
But verse 6 says how she lived: “She has rebelled against my ordinances in doing wickedness more than the nations, and against my statutes more than the countries that are around her; for they have rejected my ordinances.” “More than the nations” does not mean she was a little worse than others; it means she was worse than those who have no statutes. Why? Because she had them. She had ordinances, statutes, the temple, priests. The more one receives, the heavier the responsibility. The saddest people are not those who have never heard, but those who have heard and known, and still do not listen. Verse 7 goes on to say that she did not even act according to the rules of the nations around her, and was not satisfied with the evil rules of the nations. The meaning is: her own good standard she did not keep, while others’ bad habits she learned very fast.
@@2256@@
Verse 8 is the heaviest sentence in this whole section: “Behold, I, even I, am against you; and I will execute judgments among you in the sight of the nations.” Please think about it. The nations surround her, and the nations will attack her, but the one who finally stands facing her is her own God. The One who was standing on your side turns around. This is more fearful than any sword. When we read this, we should stop and ask ourselves: Am I also like this? We have the Bible, church, hymns, sermons, and we have heard so much that we no longer tremble. It is not unbelief; it is habit. Habit is the quietest deviation. It is not in one night that people leave God; it is one day praying a little less, one day obeying a little less, one day putting him second.
@@2257@@
Why does God say that Jerusalem went against the ordinances “more than the nations”? Are people who have ordinances more deserving of condemnation than others?
@@2258@@
It is not about who is worse. The point of 5:6--7 is that those who have received revelation and do not obey carry heavier responsibility, and also grieve God more. Scholars read “more than the nations” in different ways: some read it as a contrast of responsibility and privilege, some as the breaking of the covenant relationship, some as rhetorical emphasis, and there is no need to compare the good and evil of each nation one by one. These notes take the position that these are words for us to ask ourselves, not words for us to comment on others. You can ask three questions: Which passage of Scripture am I most familiar with? Is there something I have recently known clearly and still have not done? Which small matter am I willing to begin with? Please do not use this passage to judge any present-day group.
@@2259@@
Verses 9 and 10 have a very hard picture. Verse 9 says that God will do what he has not done, and will not do anything like it any more. Verse 10 says: “the fathers will eat the sons within you, and the sons will eat their fathers.” I do not intend to magnify this picture, and I ask you not to add pictures to it in your mind. This sentence was already spoken as a covenant warning long ago, in Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28; later, when the city was besieged, Lamentations also recorded it. We need to grasp only one thing: this is what sin looks like when it reaches its end, not God’s pleasure. Sin does not stay where it is; it walks on, tearing down as it goes, down to the closest relationships. So God speaks so heavily, not because he is cruel, but because he is serious.
@@2260@@
If you have personally experienced famine, or the loss of a child or loved one, these verses are not spoken to you. He is not blaming you. Please do not force yourself to read on, and do not use them to say to anyone who is weeping: this is your sin. The purpose of this passage is to let those who have not yet reached that point return today.
@@2261@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Razor, Reject, Zeal (5:1, 6, 13)
@@2262@@
This chapter has three Hebrew words worth looking at slowly, and each can be turned in thirty seconds into how a person lives today. The first is “razor,” the Hebrew תַּעַר (taʻar). Verse 1 says: “as a barber’s razor.” This word means the barber’s knife, and also a small knife for trimming things. It is a small everyday tool, not a weapon of war. This is interesting: when God speaks of judgment, he uses a small knife you may also have at home. What do you have at hand today? That knife need not be big; it only needs to be used by God. You can ask yourself: the smallest thing at my hand, am I willing to hand it over for him to speak with?
@@2263@@
The second is “reject,” the Hebrew מָאַס (maʼas). Verse 6 says: “for they have rejected my ordinances.” This word means “to refuse, to regard as worthless”; it is not forgetting, but weighing it and then deciding not to want it. This is a very clear-headed action. Many times we do not forget God’s word; we weigh it, find it too heavy, find it in the way, and put it to one side. This word reminds us: disobedience is often not weakness, but choice. But there is good news: since it is a choice, it can be chosen again. Today, is there a verse of Scripture that you weighed and then put back on the shelf? Take it down, and just do one small thing according to it.
@@2264@@
The third is “zeal,” the Hebrew קִנְאָה (qinʼah). Verse 13 says: “They will know that I, Yahweh, have spoken in my zeal.” This word is also translated “jealousy” or “wrath,” and it is the kind of feeling a husband burns with over his wife’s betrayal. It is not a cold punishment; it is the fire of someone wounded by love. So behind the judgment is a heart that loves too much. This is the sentence in the whole chapter most easily missed: God’s anger is because he is zealous. Zeal is not being willing to hand you over to other gods.
@@2265@@
Join the three words together and you have the journey of this chapter: an ordinary small knife (taʻar), an ordinance weighed and thrown away (maʼas), and a heart burning with love (qinʼah). Judgment is not a stone dropped from the sky; it is a betrayed love, using what is at hand to say the matter plainly.
@@2266@@
Is God’s “zeal” the same thing as human jealousy? Can we use this word to find excuses for God’s anger?
@@2267@@
It is not the same thing, and we may not use it to find excuses for our own anger. The “zeal” of verse 13 is God’s exclusive devotion to his covenant with his people, not willing to sit with other gods. Human jealousy is often mixed with selfishness and insecurity, and wants to tie a person to one’s own side. God’s zeal is for the good of the other: when his anger has accomplished what was set, it rests, and he is comforted. So when we read this word, we do not learn to be angry like him; we learn to be devoted like him: the people I love, I will not hand over to anything else. If you find that your anger is for the sake of face or control, please first say to God: Lord, this is my jealousy, not your zeal.
@@2268@@
IV. I Swear by My Own Life (5:11–17)
@@2269@@
In verse 11 God speaks in a very rare way: “As I live.” When people swear, they swear by something greater than themselves. God has nothing greater to swear by, so he swears by his own life and says: this matter is as certain as my own existence. Why so solemn? There is only one reason: “because you have defiled my sanctuary with all your detestable things, and with all your abominations.” It is not that they did not worship God at all; it is that idols came into God’s temple and sat beside God.
