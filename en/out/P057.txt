<p><strong>After the seven days he says: I will accept you.</strong></p><p>The last sentence of the whole chapter falls on the eighth day: “When they have accomplished the days, it shall be that on the eighth day, and forward, the priests shall make your burnt offerings on the altar, and your peace offerings. I. The Glory Comes from the East (43:1–5)
@@19743@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever lived through a whole night without electricity? On the night a typhoon passes, the whole street is pitch dark, the refrigerator is silent, the fan has stopped, and you lie in bed thinking: there will probably be no power before daybreak. But toward dawn, suddenly there is a “beep,” the television in the living room lights up by itself, the refrigerator starts to hum, and the whole house comes alive again. You have not even got up yet, but you know: it is back.
@@19744@@
Today we read Ezekiel 43, and this chapter is the “beep” of the whole book. The prophet has already seen the darkest night before this: the glory rose from the Most Holy Place, stopped on the mountain east of the city, and then disappeared. The exiles had an empty place in their hearts, and everyone was wondering: he has gone, will he ever come back? The first sentence of chapter 43 has no thunder and no trumpet. It is very quiet.
@@19745@@
Verse 1 says: “Afterward he brought me to the gate, even the gate that looks toward the east.” By now the prophet is already inside the vision of the new temple, and a man holding a measuring rod is leading him from gate to gate, measuring. For three chapters before this it is all measurements: how wide the gates, how long the courts, how high the rooms. Some people get tired reading this far. But those numbers are not there to test your memory. They are paving the way: only when the plans have been drawn is it the turn of the master who is coming to live there.
@@19746@@
Verse 2 says: “Behold, the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east.” Please stop at the word “east.” Why the east, of all directions? Because that is the way he left. In 11:23 the glory rose from the middle of the city and stood on the mountain east of the city. Now, from the same direction, light is coming. By the way he went out, by that way he comes back. He did not take a detour, and he did not change to another gate. This is a very deep comfort: the road you took away from him, he knows it; the place where you fell, he knows it; the direction you do not dare to look back at, he knows it too. Because that road is the very road he comes back by.
@@19747@@
Verse 2 goes on: “His voice was like the sound of many waters; and the earth was illuminated with his glory.” The sound of many waters is not a scream and not a racket. If you have stood beside a great waterfall you know: that kind of sound does not need to be loud, it wraps you up by itself. “The earth was illuminated with his glory” is worth noticing too: the light is not the earth’s own, it is lit. We are not the light. We are people lit by the light.
@@19748@@
Verse 3 is heavy. The prophet says that the appearance of the vision was according to the vision he saw “when I came to destroy the city,” and like the vision by the river Chebar, “and I fell on my face.” He lays two old pictures on top of each other: one is the day he was first called, when he saw the glory by the river; the other is the day the glory came to judge and the city was destroyed. He says: the appearance is the same. It means that the one who returns is the one who left. When the glory returns, it is not that a softer God has taken his place. It is the same one, coming back in the same splendid appearance. So the prophet’s response is the same too: he falls on his face. Not because he fears being killed, but because it is too heavy, and his knees give way by themselves. That is not humiliation. That is what it looks like when a person meets God.
@@19749@@
Verses 4 and 5 are the turning point of the whole book. “Yahweh’s glory came into the house by the way of the gate which faces toward the east. The Spirit took me up, and brought me into the inner court; and behold, Yahweh’s glory filled the house.” Look at where the prophet is: he falls down first, and then “the Spirit took me up.” He did not walk in by himself. He was lifted up and brought in. This is the order of grace: a person falls down first, he lifts him up, and only then does he go in. We often have the order backwards, always wanting to clean the house first before daring to invite the guest. But this verse says: he comes back, not because the house is tidy, but because he himself has decided to come.
@@19750@@
When the glory of God left, did that mean he had stopped wanting us? And now that he returns, is it because we have become good enough?
@@19751@@
The text does not say that. The glory left because the house was full of abominations, not because he was sulking and rejecting them. And the glory does not return because people have tidied up either. There is not one sentence in chapter 43 that says, “I come because you are good enough.” The verb in verse 2 is “came,” and that is his own initiative. So you do not have to wait until you are good enough to draw near. If you feel that he is very far from you, do not hurry to prove yourself. First say one sentence to him: Lord, come back. That is the first act of welcoming him. His leaving was his righteousness toward sin; his returning is his mercy toward people. Both are true.
@@19752@@
II. I Will Dwell Here (43:6–9)
@@19753@@
After the glory entered the house, the prophet next heard a voice. Verse 6 says: “I heard one speaking to me out of the house; and a man stood by me.” The voice came out of the house, and beside him stood the man who was leading him. After the glory has filled the house, the first thing is not sightseeing. It is listening.
@@19754@@
Verse 7 is the heart of this chapter. “Son of man, this is the place of my throne, and the place of the soles of my feet, where I will dwell among the children of Israel forever.” Please look at it word by word. “Throne” is where a king sits; it is ruling and authority. But the next phrase at once says “the soles of my feet.” A king with his feet on the ground is a very near picture. He is the king sitting on the throne, and he is also the one who walks and steps in through your door.
@@19755@@
Then he says: I will dwell here. Not passing through, not looking around, not coming for one night. Dwelling. And he adds: forever. How did the exiles feel when they heard “forever”? They had once thought the temple was forever, and it was destroyed. Now he says: I will dwell, forever. This time the “forever” does not rest on their faithfulness. It rests on what he himself has said.
@@19756@@
Then he turns back and counts up the past. Verses 7 and 8 say: “The house of Israel will no more defile my holy name, neither they, nor their kings, by their prostitution, and by the dead bodies of their kings in their high places; in their setting of their threshold by my threshold, and their door post beside my door post. There was a wall between me and them.” This passage must be read slowly. The problem was not that they were too far from him. It was that they were too near him, and put other things beside him. Threshold against threshold, door post beside door post, only a wall in between. Beside his house other things were set, and they were things that were already dead.
@@19757@@
What does “the dead bodies of their kings” mean? Views differ. Some say it is a worship connected with the dead and with idols, set too near the sanctuary; some say the palace was joined to the temple, so that royal power and the holy place were mixed together; some say both. These notes do not ask you to take a side. The core on which all agree is: something else was standing side by side with him. This verse asks every person’s heart a question. It is not for pointing out who has the problem.
@@19758@@
We do this too. With our mouths we say, you are my God; and beside him, one wall away, we keep another thing we cannot bear to let go of. It may be a job, it may be a reputation we will not give up. He says he was angry, because he will not stand side by side with anything else. But please look at verse 9, the end of this passage: “Now let them put away their prostitution, and the dead bodies of their kings, far from me. Then I will dwell among them forever.” It is not “therefore I am leaving,” and not “I will never come again.” It is “I will dwell among them.” He counts up the past, not to condemn, but so that this house can be lived in.
@@19759@@
How can I check myself by the picture of “threshold against threshold” without falling into self-blame?
@@19760@@
You can use a very light question: with my mouth I say he is my God, but how near to him have I placed what I cannot bear to lose? Do not hurry to answer “I have nothing,” and do not hurry to condemn yourself. You could write down the three things you care about most and ask: is this one beside him, or in front of him? Then do just one thing: move one of them a small step farther away, for example, put your phone on the other side of the desk before you pray. The purpose of the text is that he may move in, not that you blame yourself. If someone has just lost a loved one, please do not take the picture of “burying” as a rebuke of his grief.
@@19761@@
III. Light from the Original: Glory, Dwell, Accept (43:2, 7, 27)
@@19762@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter worth taking out and looking at. The first is “glory,” כָּבוֹד (kavod). Verse 2 says: the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east. The root of this word means “heavy.” Glory is weight. So when he left, people felt light: prayers felt weightless, days felt weightless, as if there was nothing to hold on to. Now this weight comes walking from the east. The word appears in verses 2, 4 and 5. What the exiles needed most was not a better plan but a presence with weight. If your days feel weightless today, perhaps it is not that you are too weak, but that you are looking for something heavier than your own strength.
@@19763@@
The second is “dwell,” שָׁכַן (shakan). Verse 7 says: “I will dwell here”; verse 9 says: “I will dwell among them.” The meaning of this word is moving in and settling down, and it shares a root with the word “tabernacle.” He did not come to look around. He came to move house. John 1:14 says: “The Word became flesh, and lived among us.” That “lived” means “pitched a tent,” and it joins with the picture of this word: the glory dwells in the house, and in the end dwells in a person.
@@19764@@
The third is “accept,” רָצָה (ratsah). Verse 27 says: “Then I will accept you.” This word means to delight in, to accept, to look on with pleasure. Please notice its position: it is in the last verse of the whole chapter, after the altar has been cleansed, on the eighth day, before people have offered the first burnt offering. The order is: he cleanses first, he says “accept” first, and only then is it the people’s turn to offer. Put the three words together and they are the road of this chapter: the glory comes from the east, to settle down, and before people have offered anything, he accepts them first. Today, before you pray, do not ask first “Am I good enough?” Ask first: “Has he already settled down here?”
@@19765@@
The original word for “glory” means “weight.” Why do we often feel his presence as very light, or not feel it at all?
@@19766@@
Feeling light does not necessarily mean he is not there. When you are short of sleep, under heavy pressure, or grieving, a person’s feelings all become dull. The prophet also saw the glory because the Spirit lifted him up, not because he strained to see it himself. So first, do not use feelings to judge whether he is there; second, set aside one very small time each day, ask for nothing, and only say: Lord, you are here. Third, if for a long time you find it hard to sleep and are interested in nothing, please tell your pastor or a trusted companion, and if necessary seek medical or counseling help. His presence and the people who look after you are often one and the same grace.
@@19767@@
IV. Shame, the Pattern, and the Altar Cleansed First (43:10–27)
@@19768@@
Verse 10 is a sentence that is easy to misread. “You, son of man, show the house to the house of Israel, that they may be ashamed of their iniquities; and let them measure the pattern.” It sounds as if God holds up a very beautiful house and crushes people until they cannot lift their heads. But please look at the order in verse 11: “If they are ashamed of all that they have done, make known to them the form of the house, and its fashion, and its exits, and its entrances, and all its forms, and all its ordinances, and all its forms, and all its laws.” First shame, then the plan. He is in no hurry to hand people a to-do list. He first lets their hearts become soft.
@@19769@@
In the Bible shame is not the end. It is a door. A person stands in front of the perfect pattern, and for the first time sees: how far I was from this. That moment hurts very much, but it is the starting point of turning back. And he did not force it. He set the measurements before their eyes and let them see for themselves. So do not use shame to humiliate others; and if there is a shame in your own heart that you cannot speak of, you do not need to run from it. It is a door to grace. Verse 12 says that on the top of the mountain the whole limit around it shall be most holy: not only the Most Holy Place, even the boundary belongs to him.
@@19770@@
Verses 13 to 17 give the measurements of the altar, rising level upon level, with horns on the four corners of the altar hearth and the steps facing east. These numbers do not need to become a code. You only need to see that he measured even the altar clearly, so that when people come before him there is a way and an order. The steps face east, the very direction the glory came in by.
@@19771@@
From verse 18 he speaks. Please notice that once the altar is built it is not used at once. First it must be “cleansed.” Verse 19 asks for a young bull as a sin offering, and verse 20 says: “You shall take of its blood, and put it on its four horns, and on the four corners of the ledge, and on the border all around. You shall cleanse it and make atonement for it that way.” After that come seven days, with a sin offering prepared every day, and verse 26 says: “Seven days shall they make atonement for the altar and purify it.” Even the place where we offer to him is not clean by itself. Seven days is a complete, unhurried rhythm. It is not a schedule people must reach, and not a devotional formula.
@@19772@@
Finally, the sweetest sentence of the whole chapter. Verse 27: “When they have accomplished the days, it shall be that on the eighth day, and forward, the priests shall make your burnt offerings on the altar, and your peace offerings. Then I will accept you,’ says the Lord Yahweh.” Please look at the order: first cleansing, first acceptance, and only then the offering. It is not “you offer, and then I accept.” It is “I accept, and then you offer.” This sets the tone for the whole order of worship: we do not trade our offerings for acceptance, we offer within acceptance. When he asks people to come, they need not worry about being refused.
@@19773@@
How can the sin offerings and burnt offerings of this passage exist together with what the New Testament book of Hebrews says about Christ offering himself once for all? Views differ. Some say these sacrifices are shadows pointing to Christ, and their meaning has been fulfilled in Christ; some say they are a memorial of Christ’s once-for-all offering, not a repetition of atonement; and some believe in a literal restoration that does not conflict with the work Christ finished. These notes set the views side by side, and make none of them a condition of faith. However you read it, one thing does not change: we are made clean because Christ offered himself once for all (Hebrews 10:10). You can rest in his acceptance.
@@19774@@
Is the temple of chapter 43 literal, symbolic or spiritual? I am not sure. Does it affect my faith?
@@19775@@
No. There are sincere views on this topic. Some believe there will be an actual temple in the future; some say the temple is a picture of presence, a symbol of God dwelling with his people; and some say the temple points to Christ and his body (John 1:14; 1 Corinthians 3:16). These notes assume no timetable for building a temple or offering sacrifices, and do not judge brothers and sisters who see it differently. What the prophet wants us to see is that the glory returns, and he will dwell. You do not need to choose one interpretation first before you can hear the first sentence of this chapter: he is back. If someone has taken your peace away with this topic, please put it aside for now and read only verse 2.
@@19776@@
“First shame, then the plan”: does this mean I must first feel very ashamed before God will speak to me?
@@19777@@
No. The “if” in verse 11 is not a threshold people must reach. It describes a real order: a person sees the gap, the heart becomes soft, and only then is there room to receive the pattern. He did not say, “if you have no shame, I will ignore you.” Especially for someone who has been hurt: the harm was not your fault, and you do not need to feel ashamed for what was done to you. Shame is a response to our own choices, not an assignment for victims. If someone uses this verse to press you to confess quickly, please remember that his order is gentle. You can say just one sentence to God: Lord, I see it; please lead me in the next step.
