The deepest hurt a person suffers sometimes comes not from an enemy, but from someone he once leaned on: you put your whole weight on him, and he broke, and pierced your hand. Ezekiel chapter 29 speaks to Egypt, and it also speaks to the house of Israel, who once looked south and hoped Egypt would come to save them. Pharaoh said, “My river is my own, and I have made it for myself,” and God said he was against this great fish lying in the river. Egypt became a staff of reed to Israel, which breaks and wounds the moment it is leaned on. The days of desolation have an end, yet the Egypt that is gathered becomes a lowly kingdom. Even the toil of the king of Babylon receives its wages from God’s hand. This chapter asks us to see clearly that the one we should lean on is the God who made the river, and that he takes away the reed in order to free our hands.

<p><strong>“My river is my own, and I have made it for myself.” The most dangerous sentence often sounds most like a plain fact.</strong></p><p>The Nile was Egypt’s life. Every year the water rose, and every year there was grain. With Pharaoh sitting on all this abundance, it was easy for him to say, “This is mine.” The text picks one verb: “made.” In Genesis, this is what God does. The moment a person says “I made it,” he quietly sits in the place that belongs to God.</p><p>So God used the harshest picture. The great crocodile lies in the river, yet God takes a hook and catches him like a fish. The little fish sticking to his scales are drawn away with him. Those who depend on the great one will leave the river together with the great one.</p><p>Notice the opening line: “I am against you.” God’s opposition is face to face. It does not hide behind the scenes. He does not take a roundabout way, because he wants this man to hear.</p><p>There is no angry shouting in this verse, only one clear fact: the river is not yours.</p><p><strong>Landing it:</strong> What do you have that you “worked hard to build”? <p><strong>① Is “the lowest of the kingdoms” punishment, or grace?</strong></p><ul><li>Punishment: a permanent demotion, never to rise again</li><li>Grace: gathered and settled, only no longer standing high above others</li></ul><p>The text has “gathering” and “returning” and also “diminishing” side by side.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: both.</strong> God’s discipline tears down pride, but it does not tear down the people.</p></blockquote>

<p><strong>Even Egypt is promised a homecoming.</strong></p><p>This passage is unexpected. Egypt will be scattered, but “at the end of forty years” God will gather them and bring them back to Pathros. They are placed in a position, a lowly position.</p><p>The word “lowly” here is not a label of shame, but a new place to stand. Formerly they exalted themselves, and now they are no longer above the nations. This is not being stripped of dignity, but returning to a human being’s proper position.</p><p>Verse 16 turns back to Israel: Egypt will no longer be their confidence. When Israel looks to Egypt, “it brings iniquity to memory.” A lowly Egypt becomes a reminder instead: why did I turn and lean on it in the first place?</p><p>God’s grace is not only for his own people. For a nation that was proud, he also left a road home, only that road goes downward.</p><p>The text places this nation among “the lowest of the kingdoms,” a position the once-highest least want to go to. But lowly does not mean abandoned. Since God’s hand gathers, there is a place for them to be settled.</p><p><strong>Landing it:</strong> Have you ever been placed in a position “lower than before”? Perhaps it was not punishment, but God teaching you to stand back in a human being’s place. Selflessness is not having nothing. It is no longer clutching.</p>

I. This River Is Mine (29:1–5)
@@12958@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. I know an old farmer who had an irrigation channel in front of his house. All year round the water gurgled along, watering his three hectares of rice paddies. He would often pat the stones at the edge of the channel and say, “This water is my family’s. My grandfather dug the channel, my father repaired the bank, and I look after it every day.” One summer it did not rain in the mountains for a long time, and when the sluice gate was shut at the other end, all that was left at his channel mouth was a few strands of waterweed. The old man squatted in the dried-up channel bed and looked for a long time, and then said quietly, “So the water was never mine.” Today we read Ezekiel chapter 29. There was a king who also stood by his own river and said the very same thing as that old man, only he never squatted down, right to the end.
@@12959@@
The date of this chapter is very clear: the tenth year, the tenth month, the twelfth day. The prophet’s words in the earlier chapters fell on Tyre, and now they turn to face Egypt in the south. God told the prophet to set his face against Pharaoh, and to prophesy against him and against all Egypt. Please notice that God did not tell the prophet to comment from a distance. He told him to stand face to face and speak the word right onto a king. God’s word is often like this. It does not take a detour and does not hang in the air. It falls on a real person and a real river.
@@12960@@
The words in verse 3 are short, but very heavy. “The Lord Yahweh says: ‘Behold, I am against you, Pharaoh king of Egypt, the great monster that lies in the middle of his rivers, that has said, “My river is my own, and I have made it for myself.” ’ ” Egypt lived its days by one river: when the river rose, the fields were rich; when the fields were rich, the granaries were full; when the granaries were full, the army was strong. Pharaoh said the river was his, and at first hearing it sounds like a statement of fact: the river lies within his kingdom, and the river feeds his people. But please notice the second half: “I have made it for myself.” What he said was not merely “I own it.” What he said was “I am the source.” When a person says he is the source, he has quietly sat down on a chair that does not belong to him.
@@12961@@
Do you have a “my river” at home? My business, my profession, the children I raised, the house I built with hard work. All of these are real. You sweated and you paid a price, and God does not ask you to pretend you did not. The problem is not in saying “I did it.” The problem is in saying “I made it.” Who sends the rain? Who gave you your strength? Who prepared these hands and this mind of yours? When we keep saying all of this is “made by me,” we become, bit by bit, the great fish lying in the river: very big, very steady, very much at ease, until one day the sluice gate is shut.
@@12962@@
The picture in verses 4 and 5 is rough. God will put hooks in his jaws and draw him up out of the river, together with the fish sticking to his scales, and cast him out into the wilderness. We need not enlarge this picture. Just hear one thing: in the water, that fish was the master and the water was his whole world. Once God hooked him, he left the water. It turns out that even his water was given. There is one more sentence to notice: God said, “I am against you.” God’s opposition is face to face. He does not hide behind the scenes and plot against people. He stands in front of them and speaks. This is a kind of fear, and also a kind of honesty: he wants this man to hear it plainly.
@@12963@@
God says he is “against you.” Does this mean God dislikes people who achieve things?
@@12964@@
No. The point of this chapter is not that Pharaoh had a river, but that he said he made it himself. Elsewhere the Scriptures often praise diligence and wisdom. When Joseph managed the famine in Egypt, God was with him. The problem is one of position: when people call “what God gave” “what I made,” they put God outside the river. So you need not pretend, for the sake of safety, that you have no ability and no achievements. You can practice one very small change: the next time someone compliments you, do not rush to deflect it, and do not rush to accept it. In your heart say to God, “This is what you gave.” This sentence will not make you smaller. It only lets the river return to its true owner. If you find it hard to say, do not blame yourself. You can start by giving thanks for one small thing.
@@12965@@
II. The Staff of Reed (29:6–9)
@@12966@@
Verse 6 turns the camera to another group of people: “All the inhabitants of Egypt will know that I am Yahweh, because they have been a staff of reed to the house of Israel.” So this passage is not only about Egypt. It is also about Israel. At that time Jerusalem was under siege, and many people were looking south: Egypt had horses, chariots, and soldiers, and perhaps they would come and save us. So some people slowly put their whole weight on Egypt. God said, that is not a staff. That is a reed.
@@12967@@
Verse 7 is very concrete: “When they took hold of you by your hand, you broke, and tore all their shoulders. When they leaned on you, you broke, and paralyzed all of their thighs.” A reed by the river looks tall and straight, and from a distance it looks just like a walking stick, but inside it is hollow. You grip it, and it breaks, and when it breaks it pierces your palm. Someone who leans on the wrong object is not only disappointed, but is hurt once more. Please notice this detail: God cares about that shoulder and that back. He is not standing by laughing at those who are hurt. He is saying: I know what has wounded you.
@@12968@@
We need not laugh at the people of old. We have our own reeds too. Some people put all their security in their savings, some in a well-connected network, some in a famous doctor, a stable job, or someone who says “he will surely help me.” None of these things is bad in itself, and God often uses them to take care of us. But once we hand our whole weight over, they become a reed staff. At that moment the pain is a reminder. What it says is not that you leaned on the wrong staff, but that you leaned on the wrong object. Changing the staff does no good. You must change the one you lean on.
@@12969@@
Verses 8 and 9 return to judgment: a sword will come on Egypt, the land will become desolate and waste, and then that sentence comes again: “Then they will know that I am Yahweh.” This is the key to this chapter. The aim of God’s judgment is not destruction, but that people know him. Please notice that this sentence is not spoken only to Israel. The inhabitants of Egypt are also named. Knowing Yahweh is not the privilege of one people. It is God’s heart for every person. Even a nation that most relied on itself, God wants one day to lift its head and call out his name.
@@12970@@
If we cannot lean on people, may I still see a doctor when I am sick? Do saving money and buying insurance count as “leaning on a reed”?
@@12971@@
Yes, you may. These are vessels God gave people to use. The fault is not in using them, but in putting your whole weight on them so that they sit in God’s place. You can tell the difference with a simple question: if this staff broke tomorrow, would what I lose be a sense of security, or my whole self? Doctors, savings, and friends are all channels of God’s grace. Be thankful for them and use them well. But keep one place in your heart only for the Lord who can hold you up. If you are in the pain of being let down by someone you trusted, there is no need to hurry to say “I should not have leaned on him.” That is not your fault. First let yourself be accompanied, and when your heart is quiet, slowly hand the center of your weight back into the Lord’s hand.

III. Light from the Original Language: Great Fish, Lean, Horn (29:3, 6–7, 21)
@@12973@@
There are three Hebrew words in this chapter that can change in thirty seconds the way we read it. The first is the “great fish” in verse 3, in Hebrew הַתַּנִּים (hatannim), which refers to a great monster in the water, and here refers to the Nile crocodile. This word is interesting: the “large sea creatures” God created in Genesis 1:21 use the same root, with a slightly different spelling. It means that even the biggest and most fearful creature in the water was made. Pharaoh thought he was the great fish in the river, but forgot that the great fish itself was made by God’s hand. The greater a creature is, the more it needs to remember that it is a creature.
@@12974@@
The second is the “staff” in verse 6, in Hebrew מִשְׁעֶנֶת (mish‘enet), whose root שָׁעַן (sha‘an) means “to lean.” “When they leaned on you” in verse 7 uses a verb from the same root. Elsewhere in the Bible this word also has a good use: the psalmist says that Yahweh is my rock, in whom I take refuge. So leaning itself is not wrong. This is even a godly word. What is wrong is the object of the leaning. You lean every day, and you must lean. The only question is: where do you put your weight? Keep this word in your open hand: I am leaning, and on whom am I leaning?
@@12975@@
The third is the “horn” in verse 21, in Hebrew קֶרֶן (qeren). The horn is a symbol of strength: an ox has horns, so it dares to resist. God said, “I will cause a horn to sprout for the house of Israel.” The word “sprout” means to grow like a plant. A horn is not attached from outside. It is planted, and slowly grows. The exiles had been without strength for a long time. God did not say he would immediately give them a suit of armor. What he said was: I will let strength grow from within you. This is God’s rhythm: slow, yet real.
@@12976@@
Put the three words together, and this is the journey of this chapter: the great fish (tannim) says, “I am the source,” yet is made. We are all leaning (sha‘an), and what matters is on whom. After God takes away the reed, he does not leave people out in the wind, but lets the horn (qeren) grow from within. Great fish, lean, horn: these are three steps for a person, from “I made it,” to “you gave it,” and then to “you made me stand up.”
@@12977@@
God said, “I will cause a horn to sprout.” Does that mean that if I only believe, I will become very strong and very successful?
@@12978@@
No. This sentence was spoken to people who had been exiled, laughed at, and worn down until they had no strength. The point of the promise is that in that day God himself acts and makes them strong again. The horn grows from God. It is not pushed up by gritting our teeth. So this is not a guarantee of success, and it is not telling people to pretend to be strong in their weakness. You can use this verse like this: tell God in one sentence the place where you have recently felt “I have no horn.” Then notice, in the coming week, whether there is one very small thing that you could not do before and now can open your mouth and do. Strength often grows this way: not overnight, but little by little.

IV. Forty Years, Lowliness, and Wages (29:10–21)
@@12980@@
Verse 10 repeats the words of judgment: “Behold, I am against you, and against your rivers. I will make the land of Egypt an utter waste and desolation, from the tower of Seveneh even to the border of Ethiopia.” Seveneh is in the north, and the border of Cush is in the south: the whole country from one end to the other. Verse 11 says: “No foot of man will pass through it, nor will any animal foot pass through it. It won’t be inhabited for forty years.” Please notice that the heart of that picture is emptiness: no one passing through, no sound of footsteps. Even the words “I will scatter the Egyptians among the nations” are saying the same thing: the place beside that river that was once the liveliest has gone quiet.
@@12981@@
This “forty years” has different readings in the church. Some say it is a real stretch of days. Some say it is a symbol, like Israel’s forty years in the wilderness, a complete season of being pruned. And some say the text does not give enough detail, so there is no need to insist. We lay all the views on the table, and we do not make any one of them a condition of faith, much less use this number to build a timetable or count days. We only pay attention to the sentence the text itself repeats: “I will.” Desolation is in his hand, and even empty days have a Lord whose word decides.
@@12982@@
But there is one thing I especially want to say. If you are now walking through a very long, very empty season, where no one passes by your door and no one asks how you are, please do not read this chapter as “you have been judged.” The desolation in this chapter was spoken against Egypt’s sentence, “I made the river.” It is not any person’s circumstance. Your emptiness does not need to be explained by this chapter. All we can say, slowly, is this: desolation has an end, not because the days pass by themselves, but because there is a God who says, “At the end of forty years I will gather.” Please do not just count the days. Hold on to the God whose word decides.
@@12983@@
Verses 13 to 16 are the most unexpected place in the whole passage. After judgment comes gathering: “I will gather the Egyptians.” This is not an unconditional restoration. They will be the lowest of the kingdoms, will no longer lift themselves up above the nations, and will no longer rule over the nations. This is not humiliation. It is what remains after pride has been cut away. A deeper sentence is in verse 16: “It will no longer be the confidence of the house of Israel, bringing iniquity to memory, when they turn to look after them.” The reed is taken away, not to make them fall, but to free their hands. Only empty hands can reach out to God.
@@12984@@
Have you noticed? Sometimes God takes away our crutch, and it is not punishment. He wants us to change the one we hold on to. A person who leans on people a great deal is suddenly let down by the one he leaned on. That moment hurts, hurts like a shoulder pierced through. But that pain may also be a gentle gesture from God: come, hold on to me. We need not rush to call this “a good thing.” Pain is pain. But we can slowly say: what I lost is not my end.
@@12985@@
In the last section, the date changes: the twenty-seventh year, the first month, the first day. “Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon caused his army to serve a great service against Tyre. Every head was made bald, and every shoulder was worn; yet he had no wages, nor did his army, from Tyre, for the service that he had served against it.” Think about that picture: the soldiers worked hard for a long time, their hair rubbed off and their shoulders worn raw, yet it seemed to be all for nothing. Here is a question many people ask: was what chapter 26 said about Tyre not completely fulfilled? The church has different readings. Some say prophecy has layers, partial first and then complete. Some say this is a supplementary explanation added later by the prophet. And some say it shows God’s sovereignty in history. This chapter’s issue column lays the views side by side. We do not make any one of them a condition of faith, and we do not shut the mouth of the one who asks with a single sentence.
@@12986@@
Then the sentence God spoke is the center of this section: “I have given him the land of Egypt as his payment for which he served, because they worked for me.” For me. Even a king of Babylon who did not know him was working in his hand. He had his army, his plans, his ambition, and he thought everything was his own decision. But God said, this is the payment I give. This does not mean that God approved of his violence, nor that war is good, and we must never say to someone who lost family in a war, “This was God’s arrangement.” There is only one thing we need to see: nothing is outside his hand.
