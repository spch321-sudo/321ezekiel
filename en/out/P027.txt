@@8858@@
Next, God said a whole string of “I will.” I will bring you out from the peoples and gather you. I will bring you into the wilderness, and there I will punish you face to face. Verse 37: “I will cause you to pass under the rod, and I will bring you into the bond of the covenant.” “Pass under the rod” is what a shepherd does when he counts his sheep, one by one, each passing under the rod. This is judgment, and it is also recognition: he counts them because every one of them is his. Verse 38 speaks of purging out the rebels. The various interpreters see the details here differently, and we will not set up a timetable or put a label on anyone. We only remember this: God is cleansing, not destroying.
@@8859@@
Then comes the gentlest verse in the whole chapter, verses 40 and 41: “In my holy mountain … all the house of Israel, all of them, shall serve me in the land. There I will accept them.” “I will accept you as a pleasant aroma when I bring you out from the peoples.” Notice the word “accept.” It is not taking them in reluctantly; it is gladness. This God, who was hurt by them again and again in history, finally says: I will accept you. But verse 43 also says: “There you will remember your ways, and all your deeds in which you have polluted yourselves. Then you will loathe yourselves in your own sight for all your evils.” This is a very deep order: first we are caught and held, and only then do we have the strength to see ourselves; it is not that we see ourselves first and are accepted afterward.
@@8860@@
Verse 44 is a summary: “You will know that I am Yahweh, when I have dealt with you for my name’s sake, not according to your evil ways, nor according to your corrupt doings, you house of Israel.” This sentence gathers up the whole history of the chapter. God’s work is not according to our evil deeds; it is according to his name. Finally, in verses 45 to 49, God tells the prophet to prophesy to the forest of the south: a fire will be kindled and will not be quenched. The prophet says: “Ah Lord Yahweh! They say of me, ‘Isn’t he a speaker of parables?’” Even the prophet had times when his heart hurt. He feared that people would take God’s word as only a story. That is a very lonely kind of faithfulness: he spoke, and no one understood. But God still sent him.
@@8861@@
God says he will punish “face to face” and will “purge out,” and then says he will “accept.” How can these two kinds of words fit together?
@@8862@@
They do not cancel each other; they are one after the other in the same passage. Verses 35 to 38 speak of cleansing and judgment; verses 40 to 44 speak of acceptance and knowing. Please notice that the two sections use the same action: bringing out. First he brings out, then he cleanses, then he accepts. This is the picture of a shepherd, not the picture of an auction. We should not arrange these verses into a schedule, nor use them to point at any one person or any one group. What you need to remember is the direction: he judges in order to keep; he cleanses in order to accept. So when you feel you are being disciplined, you can ask: What does he want to cleanse in me? Then say to him: I am willing to be counted under his rod.
@@8863@@
The first deviation is to take “I will not be inquired of by you” in 20:3 and preach it as God refusing all prayer, or as saying that if prayer goes unanswered, the person is not spiritual enough. This was said to people with idols in their hands, not to everyone who is weak or in suffering. We want the congregation to be more willing to put down what is in their hands and come before him, and not to be more afraid of prayer or more self-blaming.
@@8864@@
The second deviation is to take “But I worked for my name’s sake” and preach it as God being self-centered, or as permission for people to indulge themselves because God will not give up anyway. The point of the text is the order of grace: he chooses first, and then people respond. But the rebellion in verses 13 and 21 had consequences again and again, and God also let them go. We want the congregation to be more thankful and more careful, not more careless.
@@8865@@
The third deviation is to take the “statutes that were not good” in verse 25 and preach it as God handing down bad laws, or as saying that God is cruel. The interpreters explain it differently. The verb in the text is “gave them up” (CUV: “let them be”); and the purpose in verse 26 is that they might know that he is Yahweh. We want the congregation to keep a sense of reverence, and not to rush to a conclusion about this verse, much less to enlarge any frightening picture.
@@8866@@
The fourth deviation is to take 20:18–21 and preach it as children being destined to carry their parents’ sin, or as parents being an excuse for their children’s sin. God said to the children: “Don’t walk in the statutes of your fathers.” The children had a choice, and they are responsible. This agrees with the personal responsibility of chapter 18, and it does not deny the influence of the older generation. We want the congregation to see both the influence of the family and that they themselves can choose a new road.
@@8867@@
The fifth deviation is to arrange the punishment and purging of verses 33 to 38 and the acceptance of verses 40 to 44 into a timetable, or to use them to point at a particular nation or group of people. The text itself gives no dates, and the interpreters read it differently. We want the congregation to rely more on the Shepherd, not to be more panicked or more eager to count days.
@@8868@@
I am very afraid of “not being inquired of.” There is one thing in my heart that I often cannot let go of. Will God ignore me because of it?
@@8869@@
You do not need to be so afraid. The elders in chapter 20 were not refused because of weakness in their hearts, but because they inquired of God while holding tightly to their idols and refusing to admit it. So the thing you cannot let go of does not have to be hidden first. You can say directly to God: Lord, there is one thing I cannot let go of, and I do not know what to do. Please help me. A prayer like this is honest, and it is one he accepts. The Psalms also have many prayers like this. If the thing is a habit or an addiction you feel you cannot control, or a long-term depression, please look for a pastor and a professional at the same time, and do not face it alone. Letting go is not a decision made once and finished, but a handing over, a small step at a time.
@@8870@@
The first problem: “Our family has always done it this way.” Chinese people value family and tradition, and there is much that is beautiful here; honoring our parents is a command of God. But in verse 18, God said to the children: “Don’t walk in the statutes of your fathers. Don’t observe their ordinances.” This does not teach people to be unfilial. It says that some habits need to stop in this generation. You can begin like this: think of one habit that has been passed down in your family (a way of speaking, an attitude toward money, a refusal to admit being wrong) and ask yourself: Is this one to be passed on to the next generation, or to be put down in my generation? Choose only one, and make only one small change. As for ancestor worship and family rituals, churches handle them differently, so please talk about it patiently and respectfully with your pastor and your family.
@@8871@@
The second problem: treating inquiring of God as a transaction. We are used to saying “God bless me,” and used to saying “I have worshiped for so long” and “I have given so much,” as if having seniority means God owes us an answer. The elders in chapter 20 sat properly, yet held idols in their hands, and used their own history to demand an answer from God. You can examine yourself like this: this week, is there anything I asked of God that had behind it the thought “I have already done so much”? If so, try changing the words: “You first gave yourself to me, and I come to respond.” God owes us nothing, yet for his own name he has given himself to us.
@@8872@@
I have been in church for many years. Does that count as “seniority,” so that God will listen to me a little more?
@@8873@@
No. The point of the whole of chapter 20 is that God keeps his people not because of their seniority but for his name. The elders had bloodline, tradition, and the suffering of exile, but none of these was a reason they could demand anything from God. Your years are a testimony of accumulated grace, not a chip for bargaining with God. This does not belittle your faithfulness; the Lord sees every offering. But what pleases him is not how much we have, but how much we are willing to hand over. You can pray like this: Lord, I do not rely on the road I have walked; I rely only on your name. If you are weary because you have given for many years without being seen, you may also say so to God. That is not disrespect.
@@8874@@
VII. Living Out 321: For the Sake of My Name
@@8875@@
Jesus is my Role Model. When he was tempted in the wilderness, he answered with “It is written,” not with his own qualifications, and not with bloodline from his ancestors. He lived for the Father’s name, not for himself; on the cross he said: Father, forgive them. The “for my name’s sake” of chapter 20 is seen at its fullest in Jesus. Those who follow him learn his way: not bargaining with God by seniority, but living only for the Father’s name.
@@8876@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 20 not to make a timetable, and not to condemn the rebellion of our forefathers, but to let this chapter turn our eyes from our own history to his name. Verse 44 says that he does not deal with us according to our evil ways. The Standard is not for measuring others; it brings us back to that order of “he chooses first and gives first.”
@@8877@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. The elders held idols in their hands and did not know they were holding them. We need the Holy Spirit to point out that one thing my eyes delight in. You can be quiet for a moment each day and say to him: Holy Spirit, please show me what I am still holding in my hands. When he points it out, it is usually not a rebuke, but an invitation.
@@8878@@
Let Jesus be King. Verse 33 says: “I will be king over you.” This is God’s decision, not my resolve. Selflessness is putting down “my history,” “my conditions,” and “my high place,” and no longer asking God to answer in my way. When he is King, I am counted under the rod and become truly his.
@@8879@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If we are brought out and accepted, we should not say “I am finally good enough.” Verse 41 says that he will be sanctified in us; this is his name. All the glory goes to the Lord who, for his own name, does not give up on us.
@@8880@@
To build God’s system. Verses 34 and 40 speak of a people whom God himself brings out, gathers, and accepts. God’s system does not rely on seniority or family; it relies on his name and his covenant. First life is caught and held, then relationships are renewed, and after that ministry is cleansed. A God-centered community is a group of people who were accepted first and then learned to put down their high places; in such a community, no one bargains by years of service, and no one is looked down on because of background.
@@8881@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “he does not give up on me for his name’s sake”?
@@8882@@
They are two sides of the same thing. Verse 33 says, “I will be king over you,” and verse 44 says that for my name’s sake I do not deal with you according to your evil ways and your corrupt doings. That he is King is his decision; that he does not give up is also his decision. So when we let Jesus be King, we are not trying to please a harsh king, but admitting that this King has already staked his name on me. What we put down is not freedom, but the weariness of “I must prove myself.” You can practice like this: each morning say a sentence: King, today I do not need to rely on my seniority; I rely only on your name. In the evening say another: Today, which high place of mine did you see?

VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Name
@@8884@@
The proud person says: “I have served for so many years and given so much; God should answer me.” The text says to him: “I will not be inquired of by you.” Pride is leaving one’s position as a human being, treating one’s own history as capital, and trying to take God’s place in deciding how he should answer. You do not need to put down everything at once. First say to God: “Lord, I am holding on to something.”
@@8885@@
The fearful person says: “I am afraid that if I cannot let go of that one thing, God will ignore me.” But the God of 20:9, even at the edge of his anger, held back his hand for his name’s sake. The fearful person needs to learn to stop measuring God’s heart by “whether I am good enough,” and to look instead at the hand he has staked for his name. You can begin by telling God the smallest thing you are most afraid to say.
@@8886@@
The shamed person says: “In our family, this generation has always done wrong, and I will only repeat it.” In verse 18, God specially spoke a word to the children, giving a new beginning. Shame says “You are destined to be this way”; the text says “You have a choice.” The shamed person does not need to become perfect first; today, in one small matter, they only need to turn in a different direction.
@@8887@@
The wounded person says: “I have given so much for God, yet he is silent. I do not want to inquire anymore.” God did not blame you for your wound, and he did not tell you to hurry and find your own sin. The prophet also knew the loneliness of “They say of me.” The wounded person can slowly find one person and say how weary you are; the one who accompanies should not rush to say “You need more faith,” but only say: “I am here. I will wait with you.”

@@8898@@
What is most distinctive about chapter 20 is that it begins with a “no” and ends with “accept.” It begins with the elders sitting before the prophet, and God swearing: “I will not be inquired of by you.” In the middle is a very long history: Egypt, the wilderness, the children, the high places, rebellion again and again, and again and again, one sentence: “But I worked for my name’s sake.” At the end are “I will be king over you” and “I will accept you.” This is a very long story, and God never left.
@@8899@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is the God who lifts his hand and swears; he wants us to put down what we are holding in our hands, and he also wants us to know that, for his own name, he does not give up on us. That God, in Jesus, came among us; he was tempted in the wilderness, and he acted not for himself, but only for the Father’s name.

When disaster comes, the first thing people ask is: Why us? Then the answer others most easily give is: He must have done something wrong. Chapter 21 does not give that kind of answer. God first tells the prophet to bend over and sigh, and then sings a short, hard song of the sword: the sword is sharpened and polished, to be drawn, to overturn, and overturn again. <p><strong>“I am against you.” This is the heaviest thing God ever said to his own people.</strong></p><p>At the end of the previous passage, the prophet saw a forest in the south on fire; in this chapter, God changes the picture: a sword. <p><strong>In this “song of the sword,” the verbs are all passive: the sword is sharpened, polished, handed over.</strong></p><p>What the prophet hears is a short, urgent poem: a sword, a sword! <p><strong>The crown is taken off, but the last sentence of this passage is a promise that is not taken back.</strong></p><p>Verse 25 calls the king of Israel “deadly wounded, wicked” (CUV: “wounded and doing evil”), meaning one who has already been struck down (חָלָל, a word that often refers to one pierced to death). He is still standing, yet already judged. “The time of the iniquity of the end” has come.</p><p>Verse 26 is the verdict of the whole chapter: “Remove the turban, and take off the crown. This will not be as it was. Exalt that which is low, and humble that which is high.” The turban is the priest’s headdress; the crown is the king’s crown. Priest and king, the two things thought never to fall, are taken off at the same time.</p><p>Then comes the threefold repetition: “overturn, overturn, overturn” (עַוָּה עַוָּה עַוָּה). Like a dirge, each cry lower than the one before.</p><p>But the second half of verse 27 is the real light of this passage: “This also will be no more, until he comes whose right it is; and I will give it.” Not “forever no more,” but “until.” The “scepter” and the “until” of Genesis 49:10 are an echo of this sentence. God places a hope with no date in the darkest place.</p><p>Landing: we often think that when everything is torn down, the story is over. This verse says: some things are torn down in order to wait for the one who truly has the right. If you are standing in a place that has been torn down, there is no need to hurry to rebuild; only know this: the one whose right it is will come. The place where people put down the crown is the very place where he becomes King.</p>
@@8985@@
<p><strong>“Cause it to return into its sheath!” To whom is this said?</strong></p><ul><li>To the sword: God commands the sword, the work is done, return to the sheath</li><li>To the Ammonites: put away your sword; you are not the judge, I will judge you</li><li>To Babylon, which carries out the judgment: end it, and afterward I will deal with you</li></ul><p>The interpreters disagree on who “you” refers to; the original is brief, and the context can support either side.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Best understanding: there is no need to force out a single “you”; the point is that the sword has a master.</strong> Whoever is commanded, this sentence says: the sword being drawn is God’s command, and its going back into the sheath is also God’s command.</p></blockquote>
@@8986@@
<p><strong>This chapter begins with the sword being drawn, yet ends with a command: put it back.</strong></p><p>In verse 28, the sword turns toward the Ammonites. They had mocked the fall of Judah (25:3), and also relied on false visions and divination for security (verse 29). The sword is drawn, “for the slaughter, to cause it to devour, that it may be as lightning”: the same words, with a new object.</p><p>When we reach verse 30, the rhythm suddenly changes: “Cause it to return into its sheath!” Who is speaking and who is commanded, the text does not make clear. But the sentence itself deserves a pause: a sword that is drawn can also be sheathed. Verse 5 says of Jerusalem that “it will not return any more,” yet here someone commands it to return (the two places have different objects). The sword is not the last to speak.</p><p>Finally, the end of the Ammonites: “Your blood will be in the middle of the land. You will be remembered no more.” Notice the last sentence: no longer remembered. It is the same root as “iniquity remembered” earlier, with the opposite meaning. Some names God remembers to the end; some names God himself has let go.</p><p>Landing: this chapter lets us see that neither the sword nor the crown is the end. People live in history, and their eyes often see only the biggest action, but the text leaves the last word to God. If you are still under the sword, this is a reminder: it has a master.</p>
