I. A Brick, a City (4:1–3)
@@1831@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. A brother who works in interior design told me that when he talks with a client, he can speak for an hour and the client still nods and shakes his head at the same time. But if he casually sketches the layout on a paper napkin, the client looks at it for three seconds and says: Oh, so that’s how it is. A picture is harder to dodge than words. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 4. God does not first give the prophet a lecture. He hands him a brick and says: you draw.
@@1832@@
Verse 1 says: “You also, son of man, take a tile, and lay it before yourself, and portray on it a city, even Jerusalem.” The people in the land of exile still held on to one hope: Jerusalem is God’s city, and it will not fall. This small brick in the prophet’s hand points straight at that hope. He is not drawing scenery. He is drawing a city under siege. Verse 2 says to “lay siege against it, build forts against it, and cast up a mound against it,” and to set camps and plant battering rams all around. One brick, filled with the equipment of a siege.
@@1833@@
Verse 3 has another detail: “Take for yourself an iron pan, and set it for a wall of iron between you and the city. Then set your face toward it. It will be besieged, and you shall lay siege against it. This shall be a sign to the house of Israel.” The iron pan is a flat pan for baking bread. The prophet sets it between himself and the city, and it becomes an iron wall. Please notice: between whom does this wall stand? Between the prophet and the city. While he declares to the city on God’s behalf, he is himself shut out, able to see but not to touch.  A barrier like this lets the word of judgment come with tears, not as a cold sentence.
@@1834@@
There is one more small matter: the prophet does these things in front of everyone. There is no stage, no lights. It is one man sitting on the ground in front of a brick, doing it for many days. The exiles passing by his door would probably stop to look, and after looking long enough, they could not help asking: What is he doing? The function of a sign is to make people ask. God often first uses a small thing that can be seen to open a crack in a person’s heart.
@@1835@@
There was a sister whose mother was very ill. She went to the hospital every day and looked at her mother through glass. She said that the feeling was not a lack of love, but loving someone so close and being blocked by a wall. The prophet standing on this side of the iron wall perhaps felt something like this: what he says is judgment, but he does not gloat. He is one of the exiles, and he loves this city.
@@1836@@
Why does God first have the prophet draw pictures and make movements, rather than simply teach the doctrine?
@@1837@@
Because the exiles had a wall in their hearts: they would not listen. Ezekiel 3:7 says they are obstinate and hard-hearted. When it is an argument, they can ignore it; but when a man is in front of everyone every day, holding a brick and besieging a city, they cannot help seeing it. A picture puts the word into the eyes, and into the memory. This also reminds us that sometimes the strongest witness is not saying a lot, but living it out. Your family may not listen to what you say, but they can see your days. You may ask yourself: today, which action of mine is speaking for me? And please also think: is there someone near you who is watching your days right now?

II. Lying on His Side: Putting the Weight on His Body (4:4–8)
@@1839@@
In verse 4 God says: “Moreover lie on your left side, and lay the iniquity of the house of Israel on it. According to the number of the days that you shall lie on it, you shall bear their iniquity.” Verse 5 says that I have appointed the years of their iniquity to be to you a number of days, even three hundred ninety days. Verse 6 says that you are also to lie on your right side and bear the iniquity of the house of Judah; I have appointed forty days to you, each day for a year.
@@1840@@
These numbers have been explained in many ways over the years. Some want to calculate corresponding years from them. Some see them as a symbolic length. Some say it was something the prophet experienced in a vision, while others ask whether he really lay down; interpreters differ. These notes do not lay out the days and do not do arithmetic, and we do not make any one view a condition of faith. We see only one thing: sin has weight, and also length, and this length is set by God, not calculated by people.
@@1841@@
We should also notice that what God says is “I have appointed the years of their iniquity…” This “appointed” is God’s hand. If someone takes these numbers and tries to find from them the moment he is living in, he has gone astray. What the text asks us is not “How much longer?” but “He is here; how do I respond?”
@@1842@@
Have you ever tried lying still for a whole day? The first hour is fine. By the third hour your shoulders ache. By the sixth hour you want to turn over. But verse 8 says: “Behold, I put ropes on you, and you shall not turn yourself from one side to the other, until you have accomplished the days of your siege.” Even if he wanted to turn over, he could not. The weight of sin does not press down all at once; it comes day by day, leaving a person no way to pretend it is not there.
@@1843@@
Verse 7 also says: “You shall set your face toward the siege of Jerusalem, with your arm uncovered; and you shall prophesy against it.” To bare the arm is to look ready to act. He is lying down, but he is not sleeping. His body and his direction are all turned toward that city. We sometimes think that bearing for others means quietly swallowing it. Not here. His eyes are fixed on that city, and with his whole body he is saying: this is real.  His arm is bared; the wind can reach it and everyone can see it. There is no place to hide.
@@1844@@
We must be careful about one thing here: Ezekiel’s bearing of iniquity is a sign, not the savior. The one who truly bears our iniquity is Jesus, and it was finished on the cross. The prophet lies down to point to the one who is to come. He cannot atone for anyone, and we need not think that we must carry everything for someone else’s sin. You can keep company, you can intercede, but you do not have to take over anyone’s responsibility.
@@1845@@
Ezekiel “bears” iniquity. Does that mean I must also carry my family’s sins?
@@1846@@
No. Verses 4 to 6 are a special assignment God gave to the prophet. It is a sign, using the body to speak of the weight of sin; it does not mean the prophet makes atonement. The one who truly bears iniquity is Christ, and he has finished it. What we can do is keep company and intercede, but we cannot replace the other person’s responsibility, and we need not count all our family’s choices as our own. If you are living under such a burden, you can say to the Lord: this person, I give to you; please make him turn back himself. Then find a companion and tell him about your weariness, and do not carry it alone. This is also mercy toward yourself.
@@1847@@
III. Light from the Original: Sign, Bear, Ah (4:3, 4, 14)
@@1848@@
There are three words in this chapter worth slowing down for. The first is “sign,” in Hebrew אוֹת (oth). Verse 3 says: this shall be a sign to the house of Israel. This word means “sign”: the rainbow is a sign of the covenant, and the Sabbath is also a sign. A sign is not meant to frighten; it is meant for people to see and remember. The prophet’s brick, his lying on his side, his bread: the whole person became a walking sign. Your days are also sending out a sign to the people around you, though you may not know it.
@@1849@@
The second is “bear,” in Hebrew נָשָׂא (nasa), which is used for “bear” in verses 4, 5 and 6. This word has three meanings: to lift up, to carry on the shoulder, and also “to forgive, to take away.” The same verb is used elsewhere to speak of God forgiving sin (Exodus 34:7). So this word shows us a very wide picture: the weight is lifted up, carried on the shoulder, and in the end, taken away. The prophet lay there carrying a sign; the one who truly takes the weight away is the One who came later.
@@1850@@
The third is “Ah,” in Hebrew אֲהָהּ (ahah). Verse 14 says: “Ah Lord Yahweh! Behold, my soul has not been polluted.” This is a sigh, a protest, with a catch in the throat. In the whole Bible, the places where a prophet says “Ah” to God’s word are not many. This “Ah” is the prophet’s honesty. He did not pretend that obeying was easy. And God’s response, as we will see below: he heard.
@@1851@@
Did you notice? This word has no fancy meaning. It is just a gasp. Yet the whole Bible recorded it. This means that God remembers not only the words of people’s prayers, but also their gasps. You do not have to wait until you can put together beautiful words before you come to him.
@@1852@@
Put the three words together: the brick, the lying on his side and the bread are the sign (oth); his lying down is the bearing (nasa); and in the middle he cries out an Ah (ahah). Sign, bear, Ah: this is the soul of chapter 4. God speaks through the body of one man, and this man has weight and also a voice, and God hears that voice.
@@1853@@
I often want to say “Ah” before God, and I feel it is complaining. Is that all right?
@@1854@@
Yes. The prophet in verse 14 said exactly “Ah Lord Yahweh!” It was a sigh, and it was also speaking of his difficulty. God did not rebuke him, but answered him in verse 15. In the Psalms, too, many people pour out their hearts to God and plead. An honest “Ah” is not unbelief; it is still speaking to God. But please distinguish: an “Ah” is spoken to God, not a throwing up of the hands against God. You can begin to pray like this: Lord, I am hurting, I cannot do this, please see me, and please tell me the next step. If this pain is already affecting your sleep and eating, please also ask a pastor or a professional for help.
@@1855@@
IV. Bread, Water, and the Dung Fire (4:9–17)
@@1856@@
In verses 9 to 11, God tells the prophet to make bread of various grains: wheat, barley, beans, lentils, millet and spelt, put in one vessel. The eating is by weight, twenty shekels a day; the drinking of water is also by measure, the sixth part of a hin a day. This amount is days tightened one day at a time. The suffering of a siege is not only the enemy outside the city, but the rice jar at home reaching the bottom a little more each day.  The prophet’s body becomes a miniature of the days of the whole city: day after day, not one mouthful more and not one mouthful less.
@@1857@@
Have you ever eaten rationed food? A person holds a small bowl and counts as he eats, thinking over every bite. Verse 16 says: “They will eat bread by weight, and with fearfulness. They will drink water by measure, and in dismay.” Notice those two words: fearfulness and dismay. The suffering of lacking food is not only in the stomach, but even more in the heart. An older person told me that when he was young there was a period when, every time he opened the refrigerator, his heart skipped a beat first. That kind of fear lasts longer than hunger.
@@1858@@
In verse 12 God says to bake it with human dung in their sight. Verse 13 explains: the children of Israel will eat their bread unclean among the nations where I will drive them. This is a command that makes people uncomfortable. Please notice the prophet’s reaction in verse 14: “Ah Lord Yahweh! Behold, my soul has not been polluted; for from my youth up even until now I have not eaten of that which dies of itself, or is torn of animals.” He is not bargaining. He is stating his bottom line.  This command is especially heavy for a man from a priestly family, because he spent his whole life learning to tell clean from unclean. His “Ah” is not willfulness; it grew out of a lifetime of devotion.
@@1859@@
Then verse 15: “Behold, I have given you cow’s dung for man’s dung, and you shall prepare your bread on it.” God changed it. Please look closely at this word “changed.” God did not rebuke his “Ah” or say, how dare you, but responded. The method changed; the truth of the judgment did not change: the bread is still bread of an unclean land, and the city is still under siege. But in the hardest command, God heard the difficulty in a man’s heart. He has compassion, and he does not take judgment lightly.  This is also why, in the hardest situations, we can still open our mouths to him. He is not a God who cannot take in words.
@@1860@@
Verses 16 and 17 return to the center of the judgment: God will break their staff, that is, cut off their bread, so that they lack bread and water, are dismayed one with another, and pine away in their iniquity. The “staff,” in Hebrew מַטֵּה (matteh), is what people lean on as they walk, what they secretly rely on. For the people in the city, that staff is food; for you and me it may be savings, work, health, reputation. What God breaks is not the person, but the part of the person’s reliance that has made the staff a king.
@@1861@@
People’s reliance on food is the most ordinary reliance. Three meals a day, never needing to think about it, until one day there is nothing to think about. So God chose “bread” as the sign: not to frighten people, but to let them see, in the most ordinary place, what they are leaning on. A reminder like this is closer than a big argument.
@@1862@@
There was a brother who was dismissed by his company and stayed home for a long time. He said that the hardest part of those days was not the money, but waking up every morning not knowing who he was. Then one night he said an “Ah” to God. He said he did not hear any voice, but the next day a neighbor quietly left a bag of rice at his door. He said it was not an answer, but a reminder: he heard. Later he said he understood that being heard comes earlier than being solved. 
@@1863@@
“Break their staff”: does it mean that if I am in financial difficulty or unemployed, God is punishing me?
@@1864@@
No. What verses 16 and 17 describe is the judgment on the people in the city at that time because of their own iniquity; the text is not speaking to every person in financial difficulty. You cannot conclude from your lack of food that God is punishing you, and you cannot say to someone who has lost a job: you must have done something wrong. What people who suffer need most is to be accompanied and to be heard. You can ask the Lord: is this staff that I lean on something I have made a king? If so, put it down; if not, do not carry unnecessary guilt. His heart is never to make you more ashamed, but to bring you back to him.
@@1865@@
The first deviation is to treat the number of days the prophet lay on his side as a timetable to calculate. What the text gives is a sign, not a formula, and different interpreters hold different views on the three hundred ninety days and the forty days; these notes do not lay out the days and do not do arithmetic. We want the congregation to love the Lord more and be more humble, not to want to calculate days more.
@@1866@@
The second deviation is to preach this chapter as “everyone who suffers is suffering because of sin.” The siege in chapter 4 is judgment on the people of that time, not a condemnation of every person who is now in suffering. We want those who suffer to be accompanied, not blamed.
@@1867@@
The third deviation is to use “God enjoys humiliating people” to explain the command about the dung fire. The text goes on to record the prophet’s “Ah,” and also God’s response: cow’s dung instead of human dung. We want people to see that he has compassion and also does not take judgment lightly, rather than portraying him as a God who loves to humiliate people.
@@1868@@
The fourth deviation is to equate Ezekiel with Christ’s atonement. The prophet’s bearing is a sign, not the savior; the true bearing is at the cross and is finished. We want the congregation to know the Lord who truly lay down, rather than taking on other people’s sins for themselves.
@@1869@@
The fifth deviation is to tell someone under siege, “You just need to be strong,” or to describe the details of the siege as vividly as possible in order to frighten people. In this chapter the prophet himself said “Ah,” and God heard. We want the listeners to have more room to say what is in their hearts, and to be more willing to turn back, not more afraid.
@@1870@@
Some say this chapter is only about judgment. Do we still need this chapter?
@@1871@@
Yes, because this chapter is not only judgment. You see, the prophet said “Ah,” and God changed the command; you see, what God set was a sign, not the details of destruction, and he did not leave the prophet to lie there alone. The weight of judgment is real, and God’s compassion is real too. If you remember only the dung fire and the lack of bread, you will miss that sentence in verse 15: “Behold, I have given you.” You can read it this way: every time you read a hard word, find one place where God responds to a person, and mark it. Then your heart will fear sin more, and love him more. Mark it, and share it in the group next time. Then your heart will fear sin more, and love him more.
@@1872@@
The first question: we are used to “not saying it out loud.” We swallow suffering; we save face when we are sad. The prophet’s “Ah” is exactly a reminder against this habit. He did not say “I’m fine.” He said it out loud, and God heard. You can begin like this: this week, find a trustworthy person and say in one sentence the one thing in which you are being besieged: Lately I have been finding it very hard with… You do not need to solve it, only say it. Saying it out loud is not weakness. In the days of a siege, being able to say one true sentence is already a step out.
@@1873@@
The second question: we are also used to “counting”: counting savings, counting days, counting who owes whom. The siege in chapter 4, however, lets people see that the real support is not that staff. You can examine yourself like this: if my income this month were cut in half, what would I fear first? Write it down, and then say to God: this staff, I no longer treat as king. You do not have to stop being afraid immediately; you only need to stop pretending not to be afraid.  Counting itself is not sin; it is wisdom. But when counting replaces prayer, the numbers become a new king.
@@1874@@
My family truly lacks food and money, and I cannot even manage to pray. What should I do?
@@1875@@
First, do not demand that you pray beautifully. The prophet in verse 14 said only one word: Ah, and God heard. You can say only this one word, or say: Lord, I cannot hold on. At the same time, please also tell your practical needs to trusted brothers and sisters, a pastor, or a community aid organization, and do not carry them only in your heart. Sometimes God’s response is a bag of rice a neighbor leaves at the door. You do not need to prove you have faith before you can ask for help. If you already have thoughts of harming yourself, please tell someone near you at once and seek professional help.
@@1876@@
VII. Living Out 321: The One Who Lay Down
@@1877@@
Jesus is my Role Model. The prophet lying on his side to bear was only a sign; Jesus is the One who truly lay down. In the garden of Gethsemane he too spoke his own “Ah,” then brought it to the Father, and stayed. As we follow him, we learn to bring the “Ah” in our hearts honestly to the Father.
@@1878@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read chapter 4 not to calculate a timetable from the number of days, but to let the text finish speaking for itself: sin has weight, and God hears the voice of people. The standard is in the text, not in my curiosity.
@@1879@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. In the days when the prophet was bound with ropes, no one rushed him to get better quickly; it was God’s Spirit who kept him in the sign and did not let him leave. When we are besieged, we also do not rely on gritting our teeth; we ask the Holy Spirit to remind us: he still sees you.
@@1880@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is admitting that the staff in my hand is not the king. Work, savings, health, reputation: which one am I secretly leaning on? To let him be King is not to have no staff, but to stop treating the staff as king. Put it down first, then receive the portion he gives.
@@1881@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. The prophet’s brick, his lying on his side and his bread: none of these actions was meant to show how faithful he was, but to let people see that God’s word is true. If we stand firm in a siege, it is not because we have the ability, but because of his grace, and the glory belongs to him.
@@1882@@
Build God’s system. In the picture of the siege, every action was set by God, and the prophet only obeyed, adding nothing and taking nothing away. God’s system is letting man be man and God be God: life first, the prophet is first bound by God; then relationships, he acts in front of everyone; then ministry, only then does he open his mouth to prophesy. The order cannot be reversed.
@@1883@@
What is the relationship between “Let Jesus be King” and “the staff in my hand”?
@@1884@@
The relationship is very direct. What God breaks in verse 16 is the staff the people secretly leaned on. The staff itself is not a bad thing; work, savings and health are all given by God. The problem is that I have made it king. You can practice like this: write down three things you are most afraid of losing, and next to each write one sentence: this is what you gave me; it is not my king. Then this week, make one small act of letting go for one of them, for example a gift, a rest, or one time of not defending yourself. Letting go is not rejecting; it is returning to one’s position: you are King, and I am a man. This is a lifelong lesson; one step at a time is enough.
@@1885@@
The proud type says: “I can hold on by myself. I do not need others, and I do not need to say Ah.” The text says to him: the prophet said “Ah,” and God heard. The proud person leaves his position and wants to be his own staff; to return to one’s position is to admit: I am not that staff; he is. You can start by saying one sentence before someone you trust: I am actually very tired from holding on.
@@1886@@
The fearful type says: “I am afraid that the next one to be broken will be my staff.” The judgment in verse 16 is real, but the compassion in verse 15 is real too. The fearful type needs to learn to replace “afraid of being broken” with “he hears my Ah.” You can start with the smallest thing: today, entrust just one thing, and do not try to entrust everything at once.
@@1887@@
The shame type says: “I lost my job and I am in debt. I must have done something wrong.” The judgment in this chapter was spoken to the people of that time, not a condemnation of every person in suffering. Shame says “you deserve it”; the gospel says “he heard.” The shame type does not have to prove himself good enough first before he can say Ah to God.
@@1888@@
The wounded type says: “I have worked so hard for so long and I am still under siege. I do not want to believe anymore.” God did not rebuke your Ah. The prophet was also bound with ropes, and no one rushed him to get better quickly. A wounded person can slowly look for someone willing to listen to you; the one who accompanies should not hurry to say “you must be strong,” but only say: I am here, I am listening.
@@1889@@
First, every day, take three minutes to say one true sentence to God, beginning with “Lord, today I am finding it hard with…” and write down the date.
@@1890@@
Second, write down the one “staff” you lean on most, that is, the one thing you are most afraid of losing, and say how you are willing to let go by one small step.
@@1891@@
Third, tell a companion one thing in which you are being besieged, give a name and a time, and ask him not to give advice, only to listen.
@@1892@@
Fourth, this week do one practical thing for someone in need: a meal, a phone call, a visit. Write down who and which day.
@@1893@@
Fifth, find a quiet time and read Ezekiel 4:14–15 slowly once, and write down which sentence you see God answering the man with.
@@1894@@
I declare: God sees my difficulty, he hears the “Ah” that I say, and he does not despise me.
@@1895@@
I declare: I do not treat the staff in my hand as king; Jesus is my King.
@@1896@@
I declare: sin has weight, and I do not pretend nothing is wrong; God has compassion, and I am not afraid to speak to him.
