I. Stand on Your Feet, and I Will Speak with You (2:1–2)
@@849@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. I have a friend who is a physical therapist, and he helps stroke patients learn to walk again. He says that every time a patient tries to stand up for the first time, he says the same sentence: “Stand up. I’m right here.” That sentence has two layers. One layer is a command, telling the patient to try with all their strength. The other layer is his hand, which already has a firm grip on the belt around the patient’s waist. If the patient does not hear that second layer, the first sentence is only pressure. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 2, and what we see is exactly these two layers.
@@850@@
Let us pick up from the end of chapter 1. When Ezekiel saw the glory, he fell to the ground. What is the next step for someone who has fallen down? Chapter 2, verse 1: “He said to me, ‘Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you.’” Please notice the order. God did not tell him to stay down, and he did not say, “Stand when you have the strength.” What he said was, stand up, because I will speak with you. The purpose of standing is to hear.
@@851@@
Verse 2 goes on: “The Spirit entered into me when he spoke to me, and set me on my feet; and I heard him who spoke to me.” The command came from God, and the strength to stand was also given by God. He was lifted by the Spirit while God was speaking. It is like the therapist’s hand, already on the belt. Only when the son of man stood up did he hear the voice of the one speaking to him. Have you noticed how many times “speaking” appears in this chapter? God speaks, the Spirit enters, he hears. The whole center of this calling is not what the prophet will go and say, but that the prophet first becomes one who listens.
@@852@@
Here is an old question: is standing up God’s work, or our responsibility? The text has the command “stand,” so we cannot stay still; the text also says it was the Spirit who made him stand, so we cannot boast. Some stress that grace comes first; others stress that a human response cannot be left out. These notes set the two side by side: the command is God’s, and the strength is also God’s; our responsibility is to hear and obey, not to stand up by our own power. It is not a choice of one or the other.
@@853@@
There is also a title: “son of man.” God calls him this throughout the whole book. Some say it reminds him that he comes from the dust and is not a superman. Others say that “son of man” in Daniel and the New Testament carries further weight, to be read alongside only when the text gets there. These notes stay for now with Ezekiel’s own usage: in the presence of the glory, God calls him “man.” This reminds him of his position, and it is also a gentle kind of clarity. God is God, man is man, and yet God still wants to speak with man.
@@854@@
For weary people, this passage must first say that the strength is his, and then speak of obedience. If today you do not even have the strength to stand, what you need to hear first is “I will speak with you,” not someone urging you, “Be strong.” This week, if there is someone near you who cannot stand up, please do not preach to them yet. First sit down beside them and say: I’m here.
@@855@@
I really cannot stand up. I do not even have the strength to pray. Does God still want me?
@@856@@
Yes. The order of 2:1–2 already answers you: God first says, “I will speak with you,” and only then does the Spirit enter him. He did not wait until the person had strength; he came near first. So you do not need to prove how strong you are. You can do just one very small thing: sit down, read 2:1 aloud to yourself, and say: Lord, I am here; please make me stand. If you have not been able to stand for a long time, cannot sleep, cannot eat, or have thoughts of harming yourself, please let a pastor or someone you trust walk with you at the same time, and seek professional help when necessary. This is not a lack of faith.
@@857@@
II. I Send You to a Rebellious House (2:3–5)
@@858@@
Next, God speaks the words of sending: “Son of man, I send you to the children of Israel, to a nation of rebels who have rebelled against me.” Imagine a messenger carrying a letter with hard words in it. The person who should receive it moved to a new home long ago and does not want to open it. He may travel far and knock on the door, and no one inside answers. This is the situation Ezekiel was sent into, and God tells him plainly: “They and their fathers have transgressed against me even to this very day.”
@@859@@
Verse 4 is heavier: “The children are impudent and stiff-hearted.” This is what God says about his own people. Please do not be too quick to lift your head and count other people. These words are God’s heartache. They are not labels we can stick on any person today or any group. When we read, we first bow our head: is this describing the times when I am like this?
@@860@@
Some ask: why does God still send him? Different views are held. Some say God points out sin in order to call them back, not to condemn them. Some say the sending itself is grace, because verse 5 says they will know that there has been a prophet among them, so God has not given up. Others remind us that this term must not be used to label people. The judgment of these notes is this: these are God’s words to his people, the plain speech of his heartache, and also the grace of still sending someone.
@@861@@
Then comes verse 5, a sentence that brings both relief and heaviness: “They, whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse, for they are a rebellious house, yet they will know that there has been a prophet among them.” God places the outcome outside the prophet’s shoulders. You only go and say, “This is what the Lord Yahweh says.” Whether they listen is not your work.
@@862@@
But please notice what this verse does not say. It does not say, so you may be cold. It does not say, so you need not love or wait any longer. It only lifts a heavy load from the prophet’s shoulders: the response is theirs, the sending is God’s, and faithfulness is yours. With this division of work, the prophet’s steps become light. He does not have to carry other people’s decisions on the road. He only carries that one sentence: “This is what the Lord Yahweh says.”
@@863@@
The words “rebellious house” are very heavy. May I use them to remind people around me who will not listen?
@@864@@
No, or at least not in that way. These are words God spoke to his own people. They are his heartache, and also the grace of still sending someone. They are not a label we can point at others. What you can learn is God’s attitude: speaking plainly without giving up; having spoken, still sending someone. If you want to remind someone near you, use your own words and tell them your worry and your love, for example: I have seen that you have been very tired lately, and I care about you. Look at yourself first, then draw near with love. This is much more powerful than sticking on a label. You can also write that person’s name down and pray one sentence for them every day. Do not ask them to change right away; ask only that God soften your own heart first.
@@865@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Stand, Rebel, Eat (2:1, 5, 8)
@@866@@
This chapter has three original words that you can take home in thirty seconds. The first is “stand,” עֲמֹד, the command in verse 1, “stand on your feet.” By verse 2, the same root grows into another shape: וַתַּעֲמִדֵנִי, meaning “set me on my feet.” Do you see it? The command is עֲמֹד, you stand; the accomplishment is וַתַּעֲמִדֵנִי, he made me stand. The same root: one is his word, one is his Spirit. When we hear the command, we must also hear the hand behind it.
@@867@@
The second word is מֶרִי, “rebellious,” which appears once in verse 5 and once in verse 8. Verse 5 says, “they are a rebellious house,” but verse 8 says, “Don’t be rebellious like that rebellious house.” The same word is used first for the people, then to warn the prophet. This reminds us that rebellion is not someone else’s label; it is a road that lies under everyone’s feet. While God sends the prophet to speak to the rebellious house, he also says to the prophet: do not take that road yourself.
@@868@@
The third word is אֱכֹל, “eat,” as in verse 8, “Open your mouth, and eat that which I give you.” Before it in the original there is also פְּצֵה פִיךָ, meaning “open your mouth.” The order is: open the mouth, then eat. Not one part of this action depends on himself: the mouth is opened, and the food is given by him. All the prophet has to do is not keep his mouth shut.
@@869@@
Put the three words together and you have the journey of this chapter: he made me stand (וַתַּעֲמִדֵנִי), he sent me to the rebellious house (מֶרִי), he told me to open my mouth and eat (אֱכֹל). He did all three steps. I am only the one who obeys.
@@870@@
The same root, once as a command and once as “he made me.” How does that help me?
@@871@@
It helps because you do not have to struggle between the command and the strength. God says “stand” (עֲמֹד), and he also makes you stand (וַתַּעֲמִדֵנִי). So when you read a command in the Bible that you cannot do, you do not need to blame yourself first. You can first ask: is there a hand behind this command? Then say to him: you have commanded this, so please also do this in me. For example, “Do not be afraid,” or “Love one another.” You turn the command into a prayer. In this way, obedience is no longer gritting your teeth and holding on alone; it is responding to a hand that has already been stretched out. Any command you read today that you feel you cannot do, you can first pray like this.
@@872@@
IV. Do Not Be Afraid, and Eat the Scroll First (2:6–10)
@@873@@
After the words of sending, God immediately says something very down-to-earth: “Son of man, don’t be afraid of them, neither be afraid of their words, though briers and thorns are with you, and you dwell among scorpions.” Briers, thorns, scorpions: this is a very uncomfortable picture. With every step you fear being pricked, and you cannot even sleep in peace. But have you noticed that God did not say, “I will take these away”? What he said was, do not be afraid. He puts “do not be afraid” after “the briers are still there.”
@@874@@
Then he says: “nor be dismayed at their looks.” Many people feel this sentence most deeply. We do not necessarily fear the knife; we fear the look on a face. The boss frowns, the family is silent for three days, a friend does not answer a single word in the group chat. Son of man, God knows what you are afraid of. He says that these looks must not become your master. You only tell them my words. This is a way out that God gave the prophet: not “go when you are no longer afraid,” but “you may go even while afraid,” because the words are his, and the hand is his too. If you are afraid today, go with your fear. That is also fine.
@@875@@
Then God did something strange. He did not tell the prophet to open his mouth right away, but said: “hear what I tell you. Don’t be rebellious like that rebellious house. Open your mouth, and eat that which I give you.” Before speaking, eat. It reminds me of a mother at home cooking soup: before she brings it to the table, she always tastes a spoonful. Only someone who has tasted dares to say what flavor the pot has. God’s word, too, must first enter the one who speaks before it can come out of the mouth.
@@876@@
In verse 9 the prophet saw: “a hand was stretched out to me; and, behold, a scroll of a book was in it.” It was not words falling from the sky; it was a hand holding out a scroll to him. Verse 10 says the scroll was spread before him, “written within and without; and lamentations, mourning, and woe were written in it.” This scroll was full, even the back was filled with writing. God did not give him a light scroll; he gave him a very heavy one.
@@877@@
Why eat first? Different readings are held. Some say that eating lets God’s word enter oneself first; the prophet is not a machine that passes on messages. Some say that eating is a symbol of obedience and receiving, a response to “don’t be rebellious.” Others remind us to read the lamentations and mourning of this chapter together with the next chapter’s description of honey-sweet in the mouth, and not to let one overshadow the other. The judgment of these notes is this: eating means receiving first and obeying first, and only then speaking; sweet and bitter stand side by side. The weight of the word is given first to the one who speaks, not pressed first onto the one who listens.
@@878@@
If you are carrying very heavy words right now, do not force yourself to “feel it as sweet.” What the text says first is lamentation and mourning. God did not tell the prophet to pretend it was light; he was simply beside him, spreading out the scroll. You can let that scroll be heavy for now. God will not say you lack faith just because you feel the weight.
@@879@@
The scroll was filled inside and out with lamentation, mourning and woe. Why did God still tell him to eat it first?
@@880@@
Because the weight of the word is to be given first to the one who speaks. Each school of thought has its own understanding: some say that eating lets the word enter oneself first; some say eating is obedience and receiving. These notes set the two side by side, and also remind you: do not separate sweet from bitter; in the next chapter you will see it was as sweet as honey in the mouth. You can apply it this way: when you are about to say a hard word to someone, first put that word into prayer and ask yourself: can I swallow this word myself? Am I saying it with love, or with anger? Someone who eats first speaks more gently and more honestly. You can also ask a companion to pray for you before you speak, so that you are not carrying the scroll alone.
@@881@@
The first deviation is to preach “stand on your feet” as “be strong, have faith,” and so to blame those who cannot stand. The text says that the command is God’s and the Spirit is his gift, and that standing up does not depend on a person alone. We want weary and wounded people to receive hope, not to feel even more that their faith is not enough.
@@882@@
The second deviation is to treat “rebellious house” and “impudent and stiff-hearted” as labels to stick on a certain group of people. These are words God spoke to his own people. They are heartache, not words we can use to point at others. We want the congregation to look down at themselves first, not look up and count others.
@@883@@
The third deviation is to preach “whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse” as “I need not care about them; I have done my duty.” This verse releases a person from carrying another’s response. It does not teach people to be cold, to stop loving, or to stop waiting. We want the congregation to love the one who will not listen even more, not to want to give up on them.
@@884@@
The fourth deviation is to use the lamentation, mourning and woe written within and without the scroll as material for frightening people. It was a word that had to be eaten first into the prophet’s own belly; its fruit is endurance and a deeper love for the Lord, not panic. We want the congregation to be more willing to bow their heads and hear God speak, not more afraid.
@@885@@
The fifth deviation is to preach “eat first, then speak” as meaning that people must first make themselves perfect before they are fit to pass on the word. The text speaks of receiving first, not of being perfect first; the prophet himself was reminded, “Don’t be rebellious.” We want the congregation to accept the commission with more humility, not to be more afraid to open their mouths.
@@886@@
I have many weaknesses of my own. Can I still go and speak God’s word to others?
@@887@@
Yes, but you must eat first. The fifth deviation reminds us: eat first, not be perfect first. In verse 8 God said to the prophet, “Don’t be rebellious like that rebellious house,” so even the prophet was reminded. So you can go with your weakness, as long as you also bow your head before God’s word. In practice: before speaking to others, say to God first: Lord, I also have this in me; please speak to me first. When you speak, use “we” in place of “you,” and testimony in place of teaching. A person like this is not speaking from a height but standing beside the other person. You do not need to wait until you have no problems at all before you begin to love others. You only need to be willing to bow your head first.
@@888@@
The first question: we are very used to “looks.” In Chinese culture we are very good at reading people’s faces, and we are also very afraid of being read: an elder’s frown, a colleague’s silence, a neighbor’s glance can all make us swallow a word that ought to be said. The picture in 2:6 is God saying: “nor be dismayed at their looks.” You can begin like this: this week, choose one small matter you have never dared to say, say it in the gentlest words, say a person’s name, say the time, and before you speak, say to God: I will eat your word first, and then open my mouth.
@@889@@
The second question: we are very used to “just say it and be done,” and also very used to “if saying it does no good, forget it.” Many people speak to family and friends a few times, the other person does not listen, and they say: I have done my duty. This verse’s “whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse” is meant to put down the outcome, not to put down the person. You can practice this: think of someone you have stopped speaking to, write down their name, and this month accompany them once in a way that does not preach, for example a meal or a phone call of greeting, without talking about whether they listen. If there is harm or violence in this relationship, please protect yourself first, and seek the help of a pastor and professionals.
@@890@@
A colleague gives me very hard looks, and I am afraid to speak. What should I do?
@@891@@
First tell them apart: are you afraid of the other person’s words, or of the other person’s look? God said two things to the prophet: do not be afraid of their words, and do not be dismayed at their looks. You can go step by step like this. First, tell God your fear without dressing it up. Second, shorten what you want to say into one sentence and write it down. Third, choose a calmer time and say it in a gentle tone. Fourth, after you have said it, hand the result to God. If the other person’s look comes from threats, oppression or harm, please protect yourself first, and ask for help from your supervisor, a pastor or a professional. You do not have to carry it alone.

VII. Living Out 321: Stand, Receive, Then Be Sent
@@893@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the one sent by the Father, and he also received the Father’s word first, and only then opened his mouth to speak. He said that man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds out of God’s mouth. What we learn is his way: before standing up, hear; before speaking, eat.
@@894@@
The Bible is my Standard. God’s word is a scroll spread out before me (2:9–10), not a tool in my hand. When we read chapter 2, it is not to find a sentence to deal with other people, but to let this word enter ourselves first. This week, as you read Ezekiel chapter 2, ask only one thing: which sentence does God want me to eat first today?
@@895@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. “The Spirit entered into me when he spoke to me, and set me on my feet” (2:2). The one who stands up does not stand up by himself. Every morning, before you start doing anything, be quiet for one minute and say to the Holy Spirit: Lord, today please make me stand; I want to hear you speak.
@@896@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness means putting down the part of me that says, “I must stand on my own,” and also putting down the part that worries, “What will others think of me?” The one who lets Jesus be King is sent to speak to a rebellious house, yet is no longer mastered by their looks (2:6).
@@897@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. Standing up was his doing, being sent is his commission, and eating is his gift. If someone listens, it is his word; if someone does not listen, his word is still there. Our work is to speak faithfully, and the glory belongs to the Lord who sent us.
@@898@@
Build God’s system. A community that belongs to God is a group of people who receive first and are sent afterward: inside it, the weary are lifted up, not hurried along; those who speak have eaten God’s word first, and have not first picked up a measuring stick. The order is: life first, then relationships, then ministry.
@@899@@
What does “let Jesus be King” have to do with “eat God’s word first”?
@@900@@
The connection is direct. Letting Jesus be King is not a slogan; it is handing the right to speak over to him: I hear him speak first, and then decide what I will say. The prophet is one who is sent, but he is not the one who decides what to say. You can practice this way: every time before you say an important word to someone, ask yourself three questions: Did he say this? Am I saying it to love them, or to win? After I have said it, can I hand the result to him? If the answer is no, do not say it yet; eat first. In this way, the one who reigns is Jesus, not my emotions. You can also write these three questions on your phone and glance at them when you need to, to help yourself slow down.
@@901@@
The proud type says: “I can stand up, I can speak, because I have the ability.” The text says to him: the Spirit entered you and made you stand. Pride is leaving one’s position as man and seizing God’s power, wanting to take God’s place. You do not need to put everything down at once. Today, just say one sentence to God: “Lord, it is you who made me stand.”
@@902@@
The fearful type says: “I cannot bear their looks; I dare not open my mouth.” God said to the prophet: the briers are still there, the scorpions are still there, yet do not be afraid. The fearful type does not need to wait until people are no longer fierce before speaking. You can start from the smallest sentence, and before you say it, eat it first, that is, say it to God first.
@@903@@
The shame type says: “I have rebelled too. What right do I have to be sent to speak to others?” In verse 8 God said to the prophet, “Don’t be rebellious like that rebellious house.” God knows that everyone has that road under their feet, yet he still handed the scroll to him. Shame says, “You are not worthy.” The text says, “He handed the scroll to you.” You do not need to be perfect first. Today, just open your mouth.
@@904@@
The wounded type says: “I have spoken so many times, and they do not listen. I do not want to open my mouth again.” God does not blame you for being hurt. “Whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse” lifts the outcome off your shoulders; it does not ask you to be cold. The wounded can slowly find someone who truly loves you and say aloud those words you have said again and again. Those who accompany them should not hurry to say “you must forgive.” Just say: I’m here, I’m with you.

Fifth, send a word of encouragement to a coworker or family member who is facing people who will not listen: “You only need to be faithful; the result is God’s.” Say the name and the date.

@@915@@
Chapter 2 begins with a man lying on the ground and ends with a scroll spread open. The beginning is one sentence: “Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you.” In the middle is a sending, to a rebellious house, whether they hear or refuse. At the end is a hand holding out to him a scroll filled with lamentation and mourning. From standing, to being sent, to eating first: this is the journey of a person being prepared by God, step by step.
@@916@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he not only tells people to bow down, he also tells them to stand, and with his own Spirit he lifts us up. God wants us to see that his commission does not depend on people’s response, and we only need to be faithful. God also wants us to learn to eat his word before we speak. The Lord who was sent by the Father, who received the Father’s word first, and who also took up our burdens for us, is Jesus Christ.
@@917@@
So please remember the three key points of today’s lesson: he makes me stand, he sends me, he tells me to eat first. The focus of this lesson is not whether your strength is enough, not whether others will listen, and not how heavy the scroll is, but Jesus, who says to you, “I will speak with you.”

<strong>What he requires is silence, not the other person’s refusal to listen.</strong><p>This passage is heavy to read. “But I will require his blood at your hand.” But please read it whole; it has two sides.</p><p>The first side: if you see and do not speak, and that person dies in sin, the responsibility is yours. The second side: if you spoke and he still did not turn back, you have delivered yourself, and the responsibility is no longer yours.</p><p>Please notice that the watchman does not have to answer for the other person’s decision, only for “whether he speaks or not.” For the prophet this is a great release: the outcome is not in your hands.</p><p>But it is also a great challenge: you can no longer use “he will not listen” as a reason not to speak.</p><p>This passage must also be read under his heart: he takes no pleasure in anyone’s death. He sent the prophet not to add to people’s guilt, but to give people a chance to turn around.</p><p>Landing: have you never spoken up because you were afraid of being refused? Speaking is not to prove you are right; it is to give that person one chance to turn around.</p>

<strong>Even the righteous need to be warned.</strong><p>It is easy to think that the watchman’s task is only to speak to the wicked. Verse 20 goes on to say: the righteous too.</p><p>One who walks in righteousness may also go astray. He says: I lay a stumbling block before him. This is a very serious picture: even the road of going astray lies under his eyes.</p><p>But the last verse is gospel: “if you warn the righteous man, that the righteous not sin, and he does not sin, he will surely live.” The word “live” in the original is doubled: live, surely live.</p><p>The purpose of the warning, from beginning to end, is these two words. Not exposing, not condemning, but letting people live.</p><p>Landing: the brother or sister near you who looks very steady may also need a reminder. A reminder is not criticism; a reminder is a kind of love, and its aim is that he may live.</p>
