<p><strong>What does “I will open your graves” refer to?</strong></p><ul><li><strong>A figure for exile and return</strong>: the grave is the land of exile, and coming out of the grave is the return (the context: “bring you into the land of Israel”).</li><li><strong>Literal resurrection</strong>: both Jewish tradition and the early Christians included some who took this as a foretelling of resurrection.</li><li><strong>Both layers together</strong>: the near meaning is the return, and this language also leaves room for a greater hope.</li></ul><p>The New Testament’s own echo, such as Revelation 11:11, which says that the breath of life from God entered them and they stood on their feet, borrows the picture of verse 10.</p><blockquote><p><strong>The best understanding: the first layer is the return and renewal of the exiled people, but the language of “bringing people up out of the graves” is not only rhetoric.</strong> These notes do not work out timetables, and only follow the connections the New Testament itself has already made. I. A Valley Full of Bones (37:1–3)
@@16803@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. In the bottom of a drawer in my old home there is a bunch of keys pressed under everything else. One is for the front door of the old house, which was sold more than ten years ago. One is for the little lock of my father’s toolbox, and the toolbox has long since disappeared. And there are two that nobody remembers what they open. Every time I tidy up, I want to throw them away, and every time I put them back. If you ask me why I don’t throw them away, I can’t say. Probably because throwing them away would mean admitting that those doors really will never open again.
@@16804@@
Today’s text is the “key” to a whole valley, except that these keys are bones. The prophet Ezekiel was in the land of exile. His city was broken, the temple was burned, and his fellow countrymen, one by one, said they were finished. At that very moment the text says: “Yahweh’s hand was on me, and he brought me out in Yahweh’s Spirit, and set me down in the middle of the valley; and it was full of bones.”
@@16805@@
Please notice the subject. It was Yahweh’s Spirit who brought him there; he had not taken a wrong turn. We often think that being led by the Holy Spirit must mean being led to green pastures and quiet waters. But this verse says that sometimes he brings a person into the middle of a pile of bones. He does not do it so that a person will be frightened there. He does it so that a person will see clearly there.
@@16806@@
Verse 2 says he passed by the bones all around, circle after circle, and there were very many, and they were very dry. He was not allowed to turn his head, not allowed to say, “It’s probably not so bad.” “Very dry” means it has been a very long time. They did not fall yesterday; this is the kind where, after many days, even the smell is gone. Do you recognize this picture? An illness that has dragged on for years, and the doctor has nothing new to say. A relationship so cold that you can’t even be bothered to quarrel. A hope that the one who once spoke it now hardly dares to mention.
@@16807@@
Then God asked a question: “Son of man, can these bones live?” Listen to what this question asks. It is not “Do you want to?” and it is not “Will you have faith?” It is “Can they?” The prophet had gone around and around, and he knew how dry these bones were. He did not say they could, and he did not say they could not. He said: “Lord Yahweh, you know.”
@@16808@@
This is the first prayer of the whole chapter, and it is only four words: you know. Some people read this sentence as dodging; I read it as honest. The prophet placed “I don’t know” into the hands of “you know.” If you came in today with a key in your heart and you don’t know which door it opens, and all you can say is these four words, that is already enough.
@@16809@@
I think of an elderly person who, after her husband passed away, still cooked an extra bowl of rice every day, then threw it away, then cooked it again. Her children urged her to stop, and she did not answer. She was not confused. She just could not yet let that door close. The prophet’s valley is probably a place like that: it is not that he did not know the bones were dry, it is that he could not yet walk away. God did not hurry him away. God let him stay there and see it through.
@@16810@@
Right now all I can say is “you know,” and I am too tired even to pray. Does this count as faith?
@@16811@@
Yes. The prophet in verse 3 did not say, “I believe he will surely accomplish it,” and he did not say, “It is completely impossible.” He only handed the question back to the God who asked it, and God did not correct him. Faith is not always full certainty. Sometimes it has just this shape: placing “I don’t know” in the hands of “you know.” You can practice it like this: say one thing you cannot let go of in a single sentence, add “you know” at the end, and then stop there. You do not need to say any more. If this feeling of helplessness has gone on for a long time, and it affects your eating or sleeping, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please also tell someone you trust, and seek help from a doctor or a professional.

II. Prophesying to the Bones (37:4–10)
@@16813@@
In verses 4 and 5, God commanded something very strange: prophesy over these bones. Speak to bones. A heap of things with no ears is to “hear Yahweh’s word.” There is an absurdity that keeps returning in this chapter: opening your mouth to those who cannot hear. The prophet did not bargain. He spoke.
@@16814@@
Please notice every verb God says in verses 5 and 6: I will cause breath to enter into you; I will lay sinews on you, and bring up flesh on you, and cover you with skin. The one who lays on is him, the one who brings up is him, the one who covers is him. The prophet’s share is only one thing: to open his mouth. The breath is not his to manage. We all have some words that are spoken to those who “cannot take them in”: to a weary heart, to a relationship that has gone cold, to a child who will not turn back. Your share is to open your mouth, not to join the bones together one by one.
@@16815@@
Verses 7 and 8 say that as he prophesied, there was a noise, there was an earthquake, and the bones came together. Imagine that sound: one bone after another, rattling, drawing close from every direction. Then there were sinews, flesh, and skin. Then the text says a very quiet sentence: “but there was no breath in them.”
@@16816@@
This is the heaviest sentence in the whole passage. Complete bodies, one after another, lay there with no breath. God deliberately let the prophet see this half-finished work. Being neat on the outside is not the same as being alive. Our gatherings, our ministry, our marriage, our home, may also be like this: the shape is all there, the work is all done, the smiles are all there, only the breath is not. If you find yourself standing in this scene, there is no need to panic and no need for shame. This is the second act of the story, not the end.
@@16817@@
In verse 9, God told the prophet to speak a second time, and this time not to the bones but to the wind: “Come from the four winds, breath, and breathe on these slain, that they may live.” Please notice those words, “the slain.” These bones were once people who died under the sword, who died with no one knowing why. God’s word did not abandon even them. Verse 10 says the breath came into them, and they lived, and stood up on their feet. Standing up is not lying there gasping; it is standing on your own feet. What God gives people is not only being alive; it is the strength to stand.
@@16818@@
Since the breath is not mine to manage, and I am only responsible for opening my mouth, does that mean I don’t need to make any effort?
@@16819@@
No. Both times the prophet opened his mouth, he was obeying a command, and it was not easy. The difference is that the effort goes into obedience, and not into guaranteeing the result. Each day you can ask one question: what is my “opening of the mouth” in this matter today? Perhaps it is an apology, a visit, a short prayer. When it is done, let go of the result, because the one who joins the bones and causes the breath to enter is he. Letting go is not laziness; it is admitting that one part was never mine. Sometimes what wears a person out most is not doing too much, but doing the part that was his. Today you can start by counting what is in your hands that was actually his.
@@16820@@
Think again: by this point the prophet had already asked, already answered, and yet was still told to open his mouth. He did not say, “You already know everything, why ask me to speak?” He spoke as he was told. Faith is sometimes not having it all figured out, but being willing to open your mouth one more time.
@@16821@@
III. Light from the Original: Breath, Hope, Knowing (37:5–14)
@@16822@@
In verse 11, God opened up the vision: these bones are the whole house of Israel. They say: “Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost. We are completely cut off.” So those bones can speak. The ones who say this are not dead people but people who are still breathing. They are still alive, yet they call themselves bones. Three sentences, each heavier than the last: the strength is gone, the heart is dead, and it seems the ties to God, to the land, and to the covenant have all been cut.
@@16823@@
Look at how God responded. He did not say, “You should not say this.” He did not say, “Have faith.” He took their words exactly as they were, and then said: “I will open your graves, and cause you to come up out of your graves, my people.” Inside the grave, the title did not change; it was still “my people.” Three actions, and the subject of each is him: I open, I cause you to come up, I bring you in. Verse 14 closes it: “I will put my Spirit in you, and you will live.”
@@16824@@
Over the years this passage has been read in different ways. Some say the bones are a figure, and the resurrection means a return from the land of exile. Some say the figure works because God is the God who raises the dead. Others say the two do not exclude each other. We do not have to force anyone to choose only one; we do not work out timetables, and we do not pass judgment on any modern country. We hold on only to the three clearest things in this chapter: he can, he is willing, and his Spirit will enter.
