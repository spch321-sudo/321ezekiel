<p><strong>To those in power, God says only two words: enough.</strong></p><p>In the Hebrew this line is רַב־לָכֶם. I. Draw the Middle Piece First (45:1–8)
@@20684@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever watched children share a birthday cake? When a grown-up picks up the knife, the children beside her do not blink. They are not staring at the cake; they are staring at the knife, watching where it will land. Whose piece is a little bigger, whose has one more strawberry: they are sharper than the adults. We have grown up, but our eyes have not changed; we have only learned not to say it out loud. Today we read Ezekiel chapter 45, and this too is a picture of cutting a cake. The prophet divides the land among the twelve tribes, but the one holding the knife is God, and the place where his first cut falls surprises us.
@@20685@@
Verse 1 says: “Moreover, when you divide by lot the land for inheritance, you shall offer an offering to Yahweh, a holy portion of the land. The length shall be the length of twenty-five thousand cubits, and the width shall be ten thousand.” Please notice the order. The land has not yet been given to anyone, and it is first given to God. An ordinary map starts from the borders, from the piece that belongs to our own house; this map starts from the very center, and it draws him first. We so often turn the order around: we work out our own share first, and then see whether we can give God a little of what is left. This map says, not so. Lift it out first, and what is left is yours.
@@20686@@
And that portion is not a single point; it spreads outward ring by ring. In the middle is the sanctuary, five hundred cubits square, with a further fifty cubits of open space all around. Fifty cubits where no house is built, no field is planted, no business is done: simply left empty. A brother told me that in his study he keeps a desk with nothing on it but a Bible. His wife laughed at him for wasting space. He said: this empty place is what I keep for the Lord. A ring of open space around the sanctuary is like saying: do not crowd here, do not press close and use it; between man and God there should be a little distance of reverence. “It shall be holy in all its border all around.” Holiness spreads from the center outward, to the priests, to the Levites, to the city.
@@20687@@
Verses 4 and 5 speak of the priests and the Levites. The land the priests receive is next to the sanctuary, “a place for their houses and a holy place for the sanctuary,” so that the place where they live and the place where they serve are joined together. The Levites receive twenty rooms, far smaller than the fields the other tribes are given, but they do not have to contend with anyone for land; their inheritance is to stand in the temple and serve. There is a very quiet truth here: those who are nearest to God do not necessarily receive the most; what they receive is nearness to God. There is a sister who quietly swept the floor of her church for twenty years and never once stood on the platform. She said: my greatest blessing is that every Sunday I am the first to enter the sanctuary, when it is the quietest inside.
@@20688@@
Verses 6 to 8 say that the land of the city belongs to the whole house of Israel, and the land of the prince lies on either side of the holy portion and the city’s land. Even the king’s land has been measured. The most important sentence is in verse 8: “My princes shall no more oppress my people.” Behind the words “no more” lies a long history, when kings took other people’s land and drove people out. Now God first marks out the king’s land, so that he has his own portion and does not need to seize anyone else’s. Power with borders is safe power. We think borders are limits, but in fact they are protection: they protect those who are being squeezed, and they keep the one who holds power from going farther and farther astray.
@@20689@@
Verse 1 says to offer a portion to God first. Is this teaching us that giving should be a fixed proportion?
@@20690@@
This verse is about how the land is divided; it is not a rule for how a church collects offerings. It cannot be used to demand money from people, and it cannot be used to measure who has given enough. What it asks us to see is the order: the center belongs to God first, and only then do the others have their shares. Brought into today, we can ask ourselves: do my time, my income and my strength have a center that is lifted to him first? How much is a matter between you and God, and it is not to be compared with others. Someone who is hard-pressed financially need not blame himself for this; sometimes the first portion is one minute in the morning, a single sentence: “Lord, I have come.” He does not look at the number; he looks at whether you have put him in the middle.
@@20691@@
II. Enough, and Only One Set of Scales (45:9–12)
@@20692@@
Verse 9 suddenly takes a turn. Before, the land was being divided; here God speaks to those in power: “Let it suffice you, princes of Israel: remove violence and plunder, and execute justice and righteousness; stop dispossessing my people.” In the Hebrew this line has only two short words, and the meaning is: enough. It is not a negotiation, and not a suggestion; it is a call to halt. I have a friend who sells vegetables in the market. He says the customers he fears most are not the ones who bargain, but fellow sellers with dirty hands on the scales: press down with one extra hand, the customer cannot tell, and by the end of the day you have made a few dozen dollars more. But he says those few dozen dollars feel hot in the hand. God says to those whose hands are hot: enough.
@@20693@@
Notice the way God speaks. He asks for two actions. One is to remove: remove violence, remove plunder, remove extortion. The other is to execute: execute justice, execute righteousness. Remove without executing, and you have emptied the house but no one lives in it; execute without removing, and you have sprinkled perfume on something dirty. Both are needed. There is also a title we must not overlook: God says “my people.” The ones squeezed out and the ones extorted, God does not call “those poor people”; he calls them “my people.” Whoever touches them touches him.
@@20694@@
Verses 10 to 12 walk from the court into the marketplace: just balances, a just ephah, a just bath. The ephah measures dry goods and the bath measures liquids, and the two must hold the same amount, each one tenth of a homer. The prophet is not teaching arithmetic; he is stating a principle: there can be only one standard. To use one ruler when buying and another when selling is to have two sets of scales. Worship cannot stay inside the temple. The one who bows his head in the sanctuary must, back in the market, have the scales in his hand bow their head too. The blueprint for this new temple draws not only a building, but also buying and selling.
@@20695@@
Two sets of scales are not only found in the market. One standard for our own children and another for other people’s children; for our own faults we say “the situation was special then,” and for other people’s faults we say “that is just the kind of person he is”; in church we are generous to those we like and picky with those we do not. Every home, every office, every small group has an invisible pair of scales. Ezekiel says: just balances. It means: the one in your hand must be the same as the one in God’s hand.
@@20696@@
I am not a person in power. Does this “enough” have anything to do with me?
@@20697@@
Yes, only the direction is different. We often think that “having power” means the king, the boss, the pastor, but as long as there is someone softer than you, or someone who needs you, you hold a little power in that position: parents over children, the senior over the newcomer, the one who came first over the one who came later, the one who talks a lot over the one who dares not speak. You can ask yourself: in which relationship have I taken a little more? Taken more time, taken more credit, taken more of the right to speak. Do not use this verse to point at some leader today; that turns God’s word into a weapon. Use it to shine on yourself, and that is the right use.
@@20698@@
III. Light from the Original Language: Lift, Enough, Just (45:1, 9, 10)
@@20699@@
There are three words in this chapter worth slowing down for. The first is “lift,” the Hebrew תָּרִימוּ, which in verse 1 means to “offer” a portion. The root of this word is “to raise high,” and it means lifting up from your own hands and giving to another. The noun form תְּרוּמָה is “the holy offering,” the thing that has been lifted out. You see, the picture of this word is not “throw it away,” and not “have it taken from you,” but you lift it high with your own hands and set it before him. This word can become an action within thirty seconds: on the day you receive your pay, stop for a moment, lift the first portion, and say: Lord, this was always yours.
@@20700@@
The second is “enough.” In verse 9 the word רַב, together with “to you,” means “you already have more than enough.” In the Bible this word means “much”: much water, many sins, many people. When God uses this word with those in power, he is saying: what you hold in your hands is already enough, and if you reach out further, it is robbery. This word is easily taken as a rebuke, but please listen from another angle: it is also a kind of mercy. If no one ever says “enough” to a person, he will keep taking and taking, until he is crushed by what he holds. One who can be told “enough” is one who is loved.
@@20701@@
The third is “just.” In verse 10 צֶדֶק is the same word as “righteousness.” God says he is righteous, and he wants the balances to be righteous, the ephah to be righteous, the bath to be righteous. The same word is attached to God’s character and to the scales in the market. This sews worship and life together: the righteous God you believe in wants to see his name on your scales.
@@20702@@
Join the three words together, and this is the road of the chapter: first “lift,” giving the center to him; then hear him say “enough,” so that power has a boundary; then with “just,” bring one pair of scales into the days. None of the three words asks more of us; they ask us to be lighter: lift it out, and the hands are lighter; stop, and the heart is lighter; one pair of scales, and the conscience is lighter.
@@20703@@
Is this act of “lifting” just the same thing as “giving an offering”?
@@20704@@
They overlap, but it is more than money. תְּרוּמָה means a thing lifted out, and it can be land, harvest, time and thought. The key is “lifting”: it is not taken from you by someone else, but you raise it up yourself and set it before God. So it is a posture, not a number. You can start with something very small: when you receive a new job, pray for it for one minute first; when you get good news, say thank you to God first, and only then tell others. After you have lifted a few times, you will find that what is in your hand is different. You no longer grip it so tightly, because you know whom it is lifted to.
@@20705@@
IV. What Comes In Is for Giving Out (45:13–25)
@@20706@@
Verses 13 to 15 give the proportions of the offerings: from wheat and barley, a sixth part of an ephah each; from the oil, one tenth; from the flock, one out of every two hundred. The proportion is small, and it is clear. This is not a scheme to collect more; it is a number fixed so that everyone can see it. Some people like to use these verses as the basis for a church collecting offerings, but these are rules for the people and the king of the Old Testament, and the weight of the passage lies not in how much is collected, but in where it goes once it has come in.
@@20707@@
Verse 16 says: “All the people of the land shall give to this offering for the prince in Israel.” If you read only this sentence, you would think the king has gained one more source of income. But verse 17 ties it together: “It shall be the prince’s part to give the burnt offerings, the meal offerings, and the drink offerings, in the feasts, and on the new moons, and on the Sabbaths,” and he prepares the sin offering, the meal offering, the burnt offering and the peace offerings, “to make atonement for the house of Israel.” This means that what the people hand into the king’s hands, the king must pass on in full, standing beside the altar to offer it to God for the people. He receives in order to offer. His position is there so that he can stand before God on behalf of others. This is very different from the kings we know: he does not sit on the throne waiting for tribute; he stands beside the altar, his hands full of smoke and fire.
@@20708@@
Verses 18 to 20 are very comforting. The new temple has just been drawn, and God says to cleanse the sanctuary, and to put the blood of the sin offering on the door posts of the temple, on the four corners of the ledge of the altar, and on the posts of the gate of the inner court. These are the places where people go in and out, where hands touch and feet tread. The new temple also needs to be cleansed, because those who come in are weak. Then verse 20 says that atonement is also to be made for the house “for everyone who errs, and for him who is simple.” These two kinds of people are not deliberately setting themselves against God; one has taken a wrong turn, and one does not understand. Yet God places them within the reach of atonement. Some people carry a worry: I have not sinned on purpose, but how do I know whether I have done wrong? This verse says to them: he has already prepared for you. Grace comes before our understanding. This does not mean we can do without turning back; it only means that you need not wait until you have understood everything before you dare to come in.
@@20709@@
Next come the feasts. First cleanse, then keep the feast; only when the temple has been cleaned does the celebration begin. Verse 21 speaks of the Passover, kept for seven days with unleavened bread. Passover was originally a night: God passed over the door with blood. Verse 22 says again that the prince prepares a sin offering “for himself and for all the people of the land.” He too is among those in need; he does not stand outside the people. The New Testament tells us that Christ is our Passover lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7); the king in the shadow offers on behalf of others, and the true King offers himself. The remembrance of the Old Testament and the fulfilment of the New do not replace each other; they are one line: he passed over us so that we would not be passed over.
@@20710@@
Since there is atonement even for “those who err and those who are simple,” does that mean what I do not know is not counted as sin?
@@20711@@
No. The text does not say that such people are fine; it says that God has prepared atonement for them. This is grace, not the cancelling of responsibility. There are things by which we hurt people without noticing; there are things we see clearly only after a long time. At such times, we need not first fall into self-blame, and we should not say “I did not know, so it is none of my business.” A better way to go is this: first thank him for preparing atonement; then ask him to give light; then be willing to turn back, apologizing where we should apologize and changing where we should change. Grace is the door, and turning back is walking through it. If you cannot sleep because of a guilt you cannot name, please talk with a pastor you trust.
@@20712@@
The first deviation is to preach the map of the division in chapter 45 as a land claim for some present-day region or people. This chapter is a blueprint within the prophet’s vision, and its weight is order and boundary, not who owns what on a map. We want the congregation to see more that God is the center, not to stare harder at other people’s land and borders.
@@20713@@
The second deviation is to draw up a timetable for the restoration of the holy land, the new temple or the feasts, or to hint that the days can be calculated. For chapters 40 to 48 there are literal, symbolic and spiritual readings; these notes set them side by side, do not make any one of them a condition of faith, and do not judge brothers and sisters who see it differently. We want the congregation to have more patience and love the Lord more, not to be more afraid.
@@20714@@
The third deviation is to use the “enough” of 45:9 to accuse some leader, pastor or employer of today. This verse is spoken to everyone who holds a position, and its best use is to ask ourselves: where have I taken more? Pointing at others is quick; shining it on ourselves is obedience.
@@20715@@
The fourth deviation is to tell someone who has been oppressed or exploited, “You must forgive,” or “Be strong,” or to use “God will vindicate” in place of being with them. God says “my people,” and he cares for those who have been pushed aside; accompanying them matters more than urging them on. Protect first, then listen, and do not add another burden to what they already carry.
@@20716@@
The fifth deviation is to preach “those who err and those who are simple” in verse 20 as meaning that those who do not understand need not take responsibility or repent, or to use the proportions of the offerings as a reason for the church to demand money from people. The weight of the passage is that he has prepared atonement, and that what the king takes in is to be offered on behalf of the people. We want the congregation to be more thankful and more willing to turn back, not more calculating or more self-blaming.
@@20717@@
I see that I have “two sets of scales,” but I do not know how to change. Where can I begin?
@@20718@@
Begin with one thing, and do not try to change everything at once. In today’s days, find the one occasion where you most easily use two standards, for example toward your children and toward your colleagues, toward family and toward outsiders, toward yourself and toward the other person. Write it in one sentence: with A, I use this scale; with B, I use that one. Then ask: if God were holding the just balances, how would he weigh it? Then find a companion and ask him to remind you about this one thing. As you change, do not wait until you get it perfectly right before you start: using two sets of scales one time less today is a small piece of justice. You also need not hurry to blame yourself; seeing it is already grace.
@@20719@@
The first question: when we divide things, we are used to caring about fairness on one side and about relationships on the other, and so we are embarrassed to speak plainly. Inheritance, red envelopes, gift money, shares, credit: we often discuss them with a smile at the dinner table, and then each goes home and keeps the account in their own heart. The result is that in name we are one family, but the accounts are two sets: the one that is spoken, and the one that is counted in the heart. Chapter 45 first measures the land, and then measures the scales, because being clear is real love: when the accounts are clear, the relationship does not have to rely on guessing. You can begin like this: in one matter that involves money or credit, speak plainly, and before you speak, pray first, setting “how much will I get” before him.
@@20720@@
The second question: we are used to “taking care of myself first, and giving God what is left,” putting the time for God last. Busy people, tired people, people with family burdens often feel this is reasonable. But the order of chapter 45 reminds us: the center is God. We do not mean to blame those who are worn out; we invite one small change of order: every day, give him one minute first, and then begin. This is not homework; it is positioning. When the position is right, there will be a place to put what comes after. If you are caring for the sick, for children or for aging parents, and are so tired that you have no strength, that minute can be just one sentence: Lord, I have come.
@@20721@@
There is something truly unfair in my family, and speaking about it now would hurt feelings. What should I do?
@@20722@@
Do not hurry to decide whether to speak. First, quietly place the matter before God. You can write three things on a sheet of paper: exactly which thing makes you feel it is unfair; what you hope it will become; and one small step you are willing to take first. Then find someone you trust, who will not pass on what you say, and talk with him to hear his view. If you are going to speak face to face, you can say only, “I would like to talk this through,” without accusing and without bringing up old accounts. If the other person is an elder or someone in authority, you can also ask someone both of you respect to sit with you. If the matter involves personal safety or long-term harm, please seek help from a pastor and from professionals at the same time, and do not try to carry it alone.
@@20723@@
VII. Living Out 321: Lift It First, Then Weigh Justly
@@20724@@
Jesus is my Role Model. He is the King, yet he said that the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve (Mark 10:45). The king of 45:17 offers on behalf of others, and that is the shadow; Jesus is the true King, who offers himself. I learn from him: authority is for standing before God on behalf of others.
@@20725@@
The Bible is my Standard. We read this chapter not to analyze a map, but to let God’s word measure me. Verse 1 asks me whether the center has been given to him first; verse 9 asks me where I should say enough; verse 10 asks me whether the scales in my hand are only one set. These three questions are not someone else’s; they are mine.
@@20726@@
The Holy Spirit is my Guide. It is the Holy Spirit who, when the thought “how much will I get” rises, gently reminds me to look first at the middle piece. It is also the Holy Spirit who shows me where I have taken more, and where I speak two sets of words. Each day, in a quiet moment, ask one question: Lord, which pair of scales today do you want me to change?
@@20727@@
Let Jesus be King. Selflessness is to lift first: not because I have more, but because that portion always belonged to him. In power, it is to accept the boundary; on the scales, it is to keep only one set. One who lets Jesus be King no longer relies on grabbing a little more to feel secure; he puts the center, the boundary of power and the standard into his hands.
@@20728@@
Let Jesus receive all the glory. If I can be content, I should not say, “I am a person of good character”; if I can be just, I should not say, “I am upright”; everything is his grace, and he first prepared atonement for me, and first gave the center to his own name. Glory belongs to the Lord who said enough to those in power, and prepared atonement for those who do not understand.
@@20729@@
To build God’s system. A God-centered community puts the center in God, keeps power within boundaries, deals justly in buying, selling and sharing, and does not forget at the feasts how he passed over us. First life: lift the first portion to him; then relationships: use one just pair of scales; then ministry: what comes in is for offering on behalf of others. Such a fellowship is a small piece of God’s system on earth.
@@20730@@
In daily life, what does “Let Jesus be King” have to do with the “map” of this chapter?
@@20731@@
The map is a picture of who is in the center. To let Jesus be King is to acknowledge that the middle piece is his. Brought into life, we can look at three places. First, my first portion: time, income, strength, given to him first. Second, my boundary: I know where to stop my hand and where to let go. Third, my standard: the words I speak and the scales I use are one set. You can choose one each week, not all three at once. For example, this week only practice “using only one pair of scales in buying, selling or sharing.” He prefers small, real obedience over great declarations that stay in the mouth.

VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Pair of Just Scales
@@20733@@
The proud say, “I earned this by my effort; it is only natural that I take a little more.” The text says to him: the land is an inheritance received by lot, not something you seized; and God also says to those in power: enough. The proud leave their position and grab power, wanting to be the standard themselves; to return to one’s position is to acknowledge that the portion was always his. You need not put everything down at once; start with one matter, and say: “Lord, this portion is yours.”
@@20734@@
The fearful say, “If I give to God first, will I have enough for myself?” The order of chapter 45 is: lift out the center first, and God distributes the rest; even for the priests and the Levites, God leaves a place to live. The fearful need to learn to change “hold on first, then feel safe” into “hand it over first, then feel safe.” You can start with one very small thing: this month, hand one portion to him first, and then see how he takes care of the rest.
@@20735@@
The ashamed say, “I do not even know what I did wrong; do I deserve to come near him?” The answer of verse 20 is clear: for those who err and those who are simple, he has also prepared atonement. Shame says, “You do not understand, you do not deserve it”; the gospel says, “grace comes before your understanding.” The ashamed need not first work themselves out; they only need to walk through the door today and let the blood be put on the door post.
@@20736@@
The wounded say, “I was pushed out, I was taken advantage of; what good is it to talk of justice?” God does not say you are being oversensitive. He says you are “my people,” the ones he cares about. The wounded can slowly speak of what they have gone through, without needing to forgive at once and without pretending to be strong; those who accompany them should not hurry to say “God will vindicate,” but only say: “I hear you, I am here.”
@@20737@@
First, each morning before you look at your phone, spend one minute telling God: “The first portion of this day is yours.” And write down the date.
@@20738@@
Second, find a moment when you receive income or some resource, and stop for a moment first. Lift the first portion to him alone, name what you are lifting (time, money or strength), and write down which day it was.
@@20739@@
Third, write down one thing where you have “had enough but still want more” (time, credit or the right to speak), and beside it write: “His saying enough is mercy.” Then say the name of one person who will remind you.
@@20740@@
Fourth, choose one matter where you use two standards for family and for outsiders, change it to one this week, and tell a companion which matter it is.
@@20741@@
Fifth, in a position where you hold a little power (home, work, service), find one person you have pushed into the corner, greet him, say his name and the time, and attach no demand.
@@20742@@
I declare: the center is God, and I will lift the first portion to him first.
@@20743@@
I declare: he says enough, and I am willing to be content and no longer take more.
@@20744@@
I declare: my scales are only one set, and I am willing to be just.
@@20745@@
I declare: he has prepared atonement for me, and I need not wait until I understand everything before I come into his presence.
@@20746@@
The simplest thing is to take a sheet of paper tonight and write one sentence: This week, I will lift ______ to him first. Fill in the blank with something you can do: ten minutes, an offering, the time of one meal, one time of not interrupting. Then put the paper where you are sure to see it tomorrow, such as behind your phone or on the shoe cabinet. When you see it the next day, say one sentence: Lord, I have come. At the next small group, only say whether you did it; there is no need to say whether you did it well, and no need to compare who did more. Justice and contentment both begin from one very small action, and they do not have to wait until you are ready.
@@20747@@
Chapter 45 begins with a map and ends with a feast. At the beginning, God first draws the middle piece for himself; in the middle, he says enough to those in power, asks that the scales be only one set, and asks that what the king takes in be for offering on behalf of the people; at the end, even the temple is to be cleansed, even those who do not understand have atonement, and then they keep the feast and remember how he passed over us. From the land to the scales, from the scales to the altar, it is one road: the order begins with him.
@@20748@@
In this chapter, God wants us to see that he is the center, and also the boundary of power; God wants us to see that he cares for those who are pushed aside, and calls them “my people”; God also wants us to see that in Jesus he became the true King and offered himself, so that even those who do not understand can walk in through the door.
@@20749@@
So please remember the three main points of this lesson today: the land is given to God first, there is only one pair of scales, and atonement comes before understanding. The focus of this lesson is not how much you have, not how much others have taken, and not how much you have understood, but Jesus, who first lifted the middle piece and first prepared atonement for us.
@@20750@@
Lord, thank you, you are the center, and you come first. Please forgive us for taking more than we should, and forgive us for weighing people with two sets of scales. Protect those who are pushed aside, make those in authority content, and make those who do not understand dare to walk through your door. We pray in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, amen.

For six days of the week, the gate is shut. We are busy with business, so busy that we forget where the gate is. Ezekiel chapter 46 brings us back to a gate facing east: closed for six days, open on the Sabbath and the new moon. The prince stands at the threshold and bows his head, the people at the doorway, and the same verb: worship. Then the prince goes in and out among the people; every morning the lamb goes up on the altar first; the prince must not take the people’s inheritance; and finally the prophet is brought to the kitchens. What this chapter answers is a very ordinary question: is worship only a matter for holy days? The text says that worship goes from the threshold to the field, and on to the table.
