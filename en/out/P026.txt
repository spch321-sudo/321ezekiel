<p><strong>They came to inquire, but God’s first words were: I will not let you ask.</strong></p><p>These elders sat in front of the prophet, upright and looking very devout. Sometimes the answer is slow in coming because he first wants to deal with what we are still unwilling to put down. But this does not mean every silence is a rebuke. In suffering, he is present even in the silence. If one prayer feels especially quiet, you might ask: What am I still holding in my hand?</p>

<p><strong>God did not spare them because they deserved it, but because of his name, in the sight of the nations.</strong></p><p>This is the pivot of the whole chapter, and the backdrop for the string of accusations that follows. First came the choosing (בָּחַר), then the raised hand of the oath, and then God “made himself known” in the land of Egypt. That is a Niphal: he himself walked into the place of slaves. He had staked his name on these people before the nations, and if he gave up, the nations would say that this God cannot save.</p><p>Landing it: your hope cannot be built on your performance, because your performance goes up and down. What can hold you up is the name God has staked on you. This is not a license to indulge; it is what finally gives people strength to repent. People dare to turn around only in the place where they are loved.</p>

<p><strong>“You will know that I am Yahweh”: at the end of this chapter, this line becomes grace.</strong></p><p>Look at the place first: the holy mountain, the mountain of the height of Israel. The high place in verse 28 was a high place people chose for themselves; the high place in verse 40 is a high place God chose. The same word “high”: in one, people climb up; in the other, God calls people up.</p><p>Then come two actions: accept (רָצָה) and be sanctified (קָדַשׁ, Niphal). The subject of both is God. <p><strong>The prophet spoke the hottest words, and the hearers said: He is speaking in parables again.</strong></p><p>The first picture in this passage is strange: God tells the prophet to face south and “drip” words toward a forest. נָטַף means one drop at a time, not a downpour. I. The Elders Who Would Not Be Inquired Of (20:1–9)
@@8832@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. An elderly man went to the hospital to see a doctor. He registered and sat waiting outside the consulting room. When his turn came, he walked in and sat down, but he held a cloth bag tightly in his arms and would not put it on the chair beside him no matter what. The doctor looked at him and said, “What is in the bag?” He said, “Nothing, just some old things.” The doctor said, “You want me to treat you, but you won’t even put down the bag. How can I examine you?” Today’s chapter has a picture very much like this.
@@8833@@
Verse 1 says that some of the elders of Israel came to inquire of Yahweh and sat before the prophet. They were exiles, with a real pain in their hearts: How much longer? Does God still care about us? This is not a bad question. We are often like this too, coming to prayer with a question and waiting for an answer. But when God opened his mouth, what he said was: “Is it to inquire of me that you have come? As I live, I will not be inquired of by you.” Please notice that this sentence was spoken with an oath. He staked his own life on it, and that is how heavy the refusal was.
@@8834@@
Why? God did not explain right away. He told the prophet to do one thing: “Will you judge them? Cause them to know the abominations of their fathers.” At this moment the prophet’s role changed: he was not an advisor but a prosecutor. His job was to spread history open, so that people could never again say, “I didn’t know.” The elders wanted to know the future, but God first opened up the past.
@@8835@@
History begins in Egypt. Verse 5 says: “In the day when I chose Israel, and swore to the offspring of the house of Jacob, and made myself known to them in the land of Egypt, when I swore to them, saying, ‘I am Yahweh your God.’” Please listen to the order: first the choosing, then the oath, and then God walked into Egypt and made himself known to them. At that time, Israel had not done a single thing. They were slaves, in the mud pit. God was there, and he recognized them. This is what grace looks like: first being recognized, then beginning to act.
@@8836@@
Then God said one thing to them: “Each of you throw away the abominations of his eyes.” Notice those words, “of his eyes.” Idols are not only in temples; they are in the place the eyes are most reluctant to look away from. But verse 8 says: “But they rebelled against me, and would not listen to me. They didn’t all throw away the abominations of their eyes.” Their hands left Egypt; their hearts did not.
@@8837@@
Next comes the most important turn of the whole chapter, verse 9: “But I worked for my name’s sake, that it should not be profaned in the sight of the nations.” There is no “they repented” in this sentence, and no “they pleaded.” The reason lies entirely in God himself. His name had already been staked on these people, and the nations were watching. This is not self-centeredness; it is the weight of a promise. Brothers and sisters, there is great comfort here: you are not saved because you are good enough, but because his name has fallen on you.
@@8838@@
God says, “I will not be inquired of by you.” Does that mean that if I do not pray well enough, God will not listen to me?
@@8839@@
No. The people addressed in 20:3 were elders with idols still in their hands, not ordinary people at prayer. Verse 31 says it again, and what comes just before is: “you pollute yourselves with all your idols to this day.” So the point of this sentence is not prayer technique but what is in your hands. The Bible also has many records of God listening to weak people. If you feel today that God is silent, you need not conclude at once that you have sinned, and do not rush to say that others have sinned either. You can do this: be quiet for a few minutes and ask yourself, “Is there something I am clutching tightly while I ask?” If there is, hand over one small step to him, and then speak. If there is not, keep waiting. He has not left.

II. The Wilderness and the Children (20:10–26)
@@8841@@
God led them out of Egypt and into the wilderness, and at that time he did one thing: he gave statutes and ordinances. Verse 11 says: “which if a man does, he will live in them.” Please look at the order: first the exodus from Egypt, and only then the statutes. God did not say: Keep the rules first, and then I will save you. He saved first and gave afterward. So the statutes are not the passing mark of an exam but a map for living. Being alive is what he has already given; keeping the statutes is what being alive looks like.
@@8842@@
Verse 12 goes on: “I gave them my Sabbaths, to be a sign between me and them, that they might know that I am Yahweh who sanctifies them.” The Sabbath is a sign. The point is not what you cannot do on that day, but that the day lets people stop and remember: it is not I who make myself holy, but Yahweh.
@@8843@@
But the record of the wilderness is very heavy. Verse 13 says: “But the house of Israel rebelled against me in the wilderness. They didn’t walk in my statutes, and they rejected my ordinances. They greatly profaned my Sabbaths.” God said he would pour out his wrath on them. Then, in verse 14, again that line: “But I worked for my name’s sake.” Verse 17 says it even closer: “Nevertheless my eye spared them, and I didn’t destroy them.” “My eye spared them” is a very tender line. At the edge of judgment, he looked at them and could not bear it.
@@8844@@
In verse 18 God turned to the next generation: “I said to their children in the wilderness, ‘Don’t walk in the statutes of your fathers. Don’t observe their ordinances.’” Please notice that this is a great hope: children are not destined to walk their fathers’ road. God gave them a new beginning, saying: “Walk in my statutes, keep my ordinances, and do them. Make my Sabbaths holy.” But verse 21 says: “But the children rebelled against me.” They walked the same road once more. This is a very familiar kind of weariness: we all want the next generation to walk a better road, but our own habits are often passed down one step ahead of us.
@@8845@@
Verses 25 and 26 are the hardest place in this chapter: “Moreover also I gave them statutes that were not good, and ordinances in which they should not live. I polluted them in their own gifts, in that they caused all that opens the womb to pass through the fire.” There are different readings here. One says that God “gave them over,” that is, he allowed them to walk the road they chose, which is a form of judgment. One says that the “statutes that were not good” refer to the evil rules of the surrounding nations, not something God decreed. Others consider it a misuse of the law. With all views set side by side, these notes lean toward the first: what God says is “allowed” and “gave over,” not that he designed evil rules. The picture is very heavy, so we will not enlarge it and will not explain it word by word; it is enough to remember one thing: God’s silence and giving over are also judgment, and the aim is “that they might know that I am Yahweh.”
@@8846@@
Verse 25 says God “gave them statutes that were not good.” Does that mean God also sets up bad rules?
@@8847@@
The reading in these notes is: no. The verb in verse 25 is “gave over,” and verse 26 speaks of letting them be polluted, meaning God let go and let them walk the road they chose; it does not mean he designed evil rules. Elsewhere the Bible says clearly that God’s statutes are holy, righteous, and good. This text is explained differently by different interpreters: some say it is God’s judicial giving over, some say it refers to the rules of the nations, and some say it refers to a twisting of God’s law. Please do not argue with people over this verse, and do not be frightened by it. What matters more is the purpose at the end of verse 26: that they might know that I am Yahweh. Even judgment is for the sake of knowing him. If you feel heavy in your heart as you read this, that is normal. You may pause and say to God: I do not understand, and I place this verse before you.

III. Light from the Original Language: Raised Hand, Name, Sign (20:5, 9, 12)
@@8849@@
This chapter has three original words, and each can be turned into daily life within thirty seconds. The first is “swear,” the Hebrew נָשָׂאתִי יָדִי (nasati yadi), literally “I lifted my hand.” In verses 5, 6, and 15, God speaks with this gesture. Raising the hand was the ancient posture of an oath. It means he did not only speak; he lifted his hand and staked himself on it. Everything God has promised you is not a casual word but a raised hand. You can use it like this: when you doubt, “Does God still care about me?”, think of this hand.
@@8850@@
The second is “for the sake of my name,” the Hebrew לְמַעַן שְׁמִי (lema‘an shemi), found in verses 9, 14, 22, and 44. The first part, לְמַעַן, means “for the sake of, on account of”; the second part, שְׁמִי, means “my name.” This phrase is like a nail driven into four places of the chapter. In the Bible a name is not a label; it is a person’s character and credibility. God’s name has been tied to these people, and if he gave up on them, he would be giving up his own credibility. You can use it like this: every time you feel, “I’m not good enough, God must have given up on me,” say this line aloud: it is not because of me, but for his name’s sake.
@@8851@@
The third is “sign,” the Hebrew אוֹת (ot). Verse 12 says the Sabbath is evidence between me and them, and this word is “evidence, sign.” The “sanctifies” in the same verse uses מְקַדְּשָׁם, the same root, meaning “to make people holy.” The Sabbath is a sign pointing to one thing: it is Yahweh who makes people holy. You do not need to become holy by your own effort; you only need, every week, to stop for a moment and remember that it is he.
@@8852@@
Putting the three words together gives the journey of this chapter: God raises his hand in an oath (nasati yadi), so he does not give up, for his own name (shemi); and he also gives a sign (ot) to remind people that becoming holy is his work. Oath, name, sign: three words, gentle and firm.
@@8853@@
“For the sake of my name” sounds as if God keeps people for the sake of saving face. Is that appropriate?
@@8854@@
This must be understood carefully. In the Bible “name” is not face; it is his credibility and character. The point of 20:9 is that the nations were watching: God had said he would bring them out of Egypt, and if he gave up, the nations would say: this God cannot do it. So when he acts for his name, he is also keeping faith with his word. This is grace, not vanity: because the reason lies in himself, our weakness cannot make him change. Nor is it a license to indulge; verses 17 to 26 show that when people rebel there are still consequences, and God also gives them over. You can sort it out like this: I am saved because of his name; I live as someone who bears his name as my response. Do not reverse the two.

IV. I Will Be King (20:27–49)
@@8856@@
They entered Canaan and thought everything was fine, but it was not. Verse 28 says: “then they saw every high hill, and every thick tree, and they offered there their sacrifices.” God asked them: “What does the high place where you go mean?” Verse 29 adds a note: “So its name is called Bamah to this day.” The name of the high place is still there, which means the habit is still there too. God gave the land, and people took the best places and used them to worship something else. Please think: after we have received grace, where do we most easily and quietly set up a “high place” again?
@@8857@@
In verse 31, God said it once more: “Should I be inquired of by you, house of Israel? As I live, says the Lord Yahweh, I will not be inquired of by you!” This is an echo of the beginning: the whole stretch of history runs from the elders sitting down to the elders being refused. But then the tone changes completely. Verse 33: “As I live, says the Lord Yahweh, surely with a mighty hand, with an outstretched arm, and with wrath poured out, I will be king over you.” You see, it is the same “As I live”: before, it was refusal, and here it is promise. He will not be inquired of, because what they wanted was a God they could use as they pleased; he will be king, because he is God.
